Big Tech Spends $740 Billion on AI, Investors Ask Where the Money Went

Alphabet, Amazon and Meta are burning cash, Nvidia is weighing co-signing a customer's loans, and Intel just lost 28% in a day.

The wire said: Cracks are emerging in the AI spending boom · TechCrunch AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AI3 min read

Wall Street spent the past week discovering that spending $740 billion is not, by itself, a business plan. According to TechCrunch, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft laid out capital expenditure plans above that figure for this year. Translation: capital expenditure is money spent on things like data centers and chips that you hope will someday pay for themselves. On Friday the Nasdaq fell 2.9%, and Nvidia, Broadcom and Qualcomm fell 4%, more than 5% and 11% respectively.

Alphabet supplied the clearest case study. Its stock fell 12% on Thursday, its worst day on record, erasing $500 billion in market value, and then fell again. The company reported negative free cash flow of $9.1 billion in the third quarter, its second straight quarter in the red. Translation: free cash flow is what is left after paying for everything, and negative means the company spent more than it earned. Capex rose to $51.4 billion for the quarter, and Alphabet expects to spend up to $205 billion this year, with more to come in 2027.

Poland Sends Riot Police to Protect Rule of Law From Its Own Judges

Prime Minister Tusk's government moves to strip the Constitutional Court's two top judges of immunity, and President Nawrocki turns his plane around to join the standoff.

Riot police were deployed at Poland's Constitutional Court in Warsaw, the BBC reported, amid an escalating standoff between Prime Minister Donald Tusk's government and the court's senior judges. Justice Minister Waldemar Żurek said he had asked to lift the immunity from prosecution of court President Bogdan Święczkowski and Vice-President Bartłomiej Sochański so they can face criminal charges. The police were there to keep demonstrators out of the building, which is a notable way to defend an institution whose purpose is to check the state.

Translation: lifting immunity means removing the legal shield that normally stops prosecutors from charging a senior official. Żurek's stated reason is that the two judges have refused to let certain colleagues hear cases. Those colleagues are five judges elected by parliament since March, whom President Karol Nawrocki has declined to swear in, citing alleged flaws in how they were selected.

Fake Claude Extension Spends Half a Day in Cursor Marketplace, Politely Asks for Everything

A tampered version of Anthropic's Claude extension reached up to 5,000 Cursor users before the marketplace was pulled offline, and the two sources disagree on who was responsible for what.

A malicious version of the Claude Code extension was available in Cursor's extension marketplace for roughly eleven to twelve hours, affecting at most 5,000 users, according to Cursor. Ars Technica reported the figure Friday, and The Decoder reported a similar window. The reports differ slightly on timing: Ars says nearly 12 hours, The Decoder says about eleven, and says the tampered version appeared Thursday.

The sources also differ on how the package got there. Ars Technica reported that researcher Kevin Beaumont and Luke Roberts, CTO and co-founder of security firm Phorian, said version 2.1.313 was distributed through Anthropic's official account on Cursor. The Decoder, citing Cursor, said Anthropic had no involvement in developing it. Both can be true: the account can be official while the author is not. Neither Cursor nor Anthropic has explained how attackers gained control of the account, according to Ars.

GM Pays $220 Million to Settle Claims It Sold Drivers' Habits to Insurers

General Motors reached a settlement with 35 states and the District of Columbia over Smart Driver data that ended up with insurance companies, and a third party will now check its homework.

General Motors will pay $220 million to settle a lawsuit brought by the District of Columbia and 35 states, whose attorneys general joined forces over the company's handling of data from its Smart Driver system, according to Ars Technica. The feature was sold as a way for parents to watch teen drivers or for adults to examine their own habits. It turned out that a third audience had been watching the whole time.

According to Ars Technica, GM shared that data with brokers, who then sold it to insurance companies. Some drivers found their insurance became more expensive as a result. Many of them said they had no idea they had agreed to any of this, which is a reasonable thing to say about a permission buried somewhere in a vehicle's enrollment process.

Fed Holds Rates, Then Sends Two Officials to Explain Why It Will Not Hold Rates

Two regional Fed presidents said more hikes are coming, hours after the committee held steady and its chairman declined to say what he planned to do.

The Federal Reserve held interest rates steady on Wednesday. By Friday, two of its regional presidents were publicly explaining that rates will need to go up, according to CNBC. Nothing says a pause quite like a speaking tour about the end of the pause.

St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem said "additional firming of monetary policy will be necessary, and soon." Translation: firming means raising rates, a word chosen because it sounds like something that happens to a mattress. Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan went further, saying the committee needs at least two more quarter-point increases. A quarter point, or 25 basis points, is the standard unit of Fed adjustment, small enough to be called gradual and large enough to appear on your mortgage statement.

Logan added that skipping a meeting does not change the destination, which is a reassuring thing to say about a road trip that has apparently lost a few exits. Traders took the hint: CME's FedWatch tool put the odds of a quarter-point hike in December at about 76%, according to CNBC.
