Senators Briefed on Plan to Restart Iran War; At Least One Calls It 'Insane'

Al Jazeera reports a closed-door briefing on renewed war with Iran, while NPR says Hegseth removed an Army general as the U.S. prepares for possible attacks.

The wire said: Senators briefed on Trump’s ‘insane’ plan to restart war on Iran · Al Jazeera

By The Daily Spin Desk · Geopolitics2 min read

According to Al Jazeera, senators have been briefed on President Donald Trump's plan to restart the war on Iran, and the outlet's headline describes the plan as 'insane.' The word is in quotation marks, which in journalism means someone else said it and the outlet would like you to know that. The source text does not say who used the word, so the identity of the critic remains a mystery for now.

The briefing is the part of the process in which Congress is told what the executive branch intends to do. Translation: a briefing is not a vote. Senators are informed, they react, and then events proceed on the schedule set by whoever has the authority to order the strikes. It is a bit like being invited to a meeting about a decision that has already been made, except the meeting has classified slides.

Israel Votes, Counts, and Finds Nobody Has Enough Seats to Govern

With roughly 90 percent of ballots counted and neither Netanyahu's bloc nor Eisenkot's opposition holding a majority, coalition arithmetic now decides everything.

Israel's election has produced what NPR calls a deadlock. With about 90 percent of votes counted, no clear winner has emerged. DW reported that Netanyahu's bloc is ahead of Eisenkot's, but being ahead in this system is like being the tallest person in a cab queue: it does not get you a cab.

The reason is the Knesset's arithmetic. A government needs a majority of seats, and neither bloc has a clear path to one. Translation: a coalition is a group of parties that agree to govern together, usually after weeks of negotiations in which everyone discovers how much they are worth.

Google Admits AI Search Earns Less, Plans to Spend Much More on AI

Alphabet's stock fell as much as 13 percent after search growth slowed and AI Mode proved less lucrative, while Amazon promised a trillion-dollar cloud and $220 billion in capex to get there.

Ars Technica reported that the 12 percent figure is a slowdown from 17 percent in the second quarter and 19 percent in the first. Tech Stocks Slide as Investors Notice AI Costs Money Before It Makes Any

Intel, Amazon, Alphabet and others fell sharply after earnings showed heavy AI spending, with the Nasdaq opening down 2.5% at a two-month low.

US tech stocks kept falling on Friday as worries grew over the rising cost of the artificial intelligence build-out, the BBC reported. The Nasdaq opened down 2.5%, its lowest level in more than two months. Investors had spent months asking when AI would pay for itself, and this week several companies answered with a bill.

Intel was the worst case. Its shares fell as much as 30% in early trading after the chipmaker warned of a weaker computer market while still planning to spend more on AI. The BBC said Intel was on track to lose more than $130bn in value. Finance chief David Zinsner said the company did not expect to bring in more cash than it spent this year, and forecast lower-than-expected fourth-quarter revenue. Translation: the company expects to spend slightly more than it earns, and it would like you to see this as a plan.

Economy Grows 3.4% While Prices Climb 3.6%, Giving Fed Two Reasons to Keep Watching

Third-quarter GDP beat forecasts as September inflation accelerated, a day after the Fed held rates at 3.75%-4% and promised to evaluate incoming data.

The U.S. economy grew at a seasonally adjusted annualized rate of 3.4% in the third quarter, beating the 3.1% economists expected and the 2.2% recorded in the second quarter, the Commerce Department reported Thursday, according to CNBC. In the same release cycle, the department said inflation picked up in September. The economy, in other words, is doing more of everything, including getting more expensive.

Translation: an annualized rate means the quarter's growth is projected as if it lasted a full year. It is a way of making a three-month period sound more decisive than it was.

The Fed's preferred inflation gauge, the personal consumption expenditures price index, rose 0.5% in September, the largest monthly gain since March. That put the annual rate at 3.6%, a tenth of a point below forecasts. Excluding food and energy, the core reading was 0.3% for the month and 3.1% for the year, matching expectations. Officials generally treat core as the better guide to longer-term trends, since they cannot do much about the price of gasoline, a fact they tend to mention only when asked.
