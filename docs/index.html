Inflation Hot Two Days Running as Fed Weighs Whether to Hike Now or Later

Consumer prices rose 3.7% and wholesale prices 6.3% in September, leaving the Federal Reserve to choose between raising rates in two weeks or merely promising to.

The wire said: Consumer prices rose 3.7% annually in September as gas prices skyrocket · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & Economy3 min read

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Wednesday that the consumer price index rose 0.6% in September and 3.7% from a year earlier, according to CNBC. Economists had expected 0.6% and 3.6%, so the forecasters were right on the month and merely optimistic on the year. Inflation has exceeded the Fed's 2% goal since March 2021, a stretch long enough that the target is now more of a pen pal than a neighbor.

Gasoline did most of the damage. Prices rose 9.1% on the month, accounting for about half of the headline gain, and are up 34.2% over the year, while fuel oil is up 74.2%. Translation: the thing you put in your car and the thing some people put in their furnaces both cost far more, and the war that caused it shows no sign of ending soon.

FTC Asks Anthropic and OpenAI 50 Pages of Questions About Their Misbehaving Software

The agency has sent civil investigative demands over AI safety incidents, while its chair maintains that a tool doing what it is told is not a suspect.

The Federal Trade Commission on Wednesday sent civil investigative demands to Anthropic and OpenAI, seeking information about recent safety incidents, according to an insider who spoke to Axios and was cited by Ars Technica. Translation: a civil investigative demand is a formal government request for documents and answers, which companies tend to treat as optional in the way a tax audit is optional. The FTC did not respond to Ars Technica's request to review the demands.

The source said the requests ran past 50 pages. They reportedly seek documents on the incidents, the internal security measures the companies took, and marketing materials that might suggest consumers were misled. That last category is the interesting one. It asks whether the brochure and the behavior of the product were describing the same thing.

French Supermarket Fridges Hacked Into Ovens, Because Passwords Were Basically the Date

An estimated 700 French stores lost perishables and in some cases suffered electrical fires after internet-connected refrigeration controllers with known flaws were compromised, according to Ars Technica.

An estimated 700 supermarkets across France had their refrigeration systems malfunction this week after a mass hack, Ars Technica reported. The result was untold tons of spoiled perishables and, in some cases, fires. The outlet's assessment of the cause was blunt: basic cybersecurity would have prevented it.

French authorities say the targets were internet-connected controllers that let technicians remotely manage the refrigerators and freezers across an entire store. Translation: one login can control every cold thing in the building, which is convenient for technicians and, it turns out, for strangers. The unknown attackers ran the defrosters long enough that temperatures reached as high as 50° Celsius (122° Fahrenheit). A freezer is not designed to be a sauna, and several electrical systems reportedly caught fire.

AI Stocks Lose $1 Trillion in Four Days, Discover Money Has a Price

Twenty of the world's biggest AI and chip stocks have shed over $1 trillion this week as yields above 5% make building data centers more expensive.

CNBC reported that 20 of the most valuable global AI and semiconductor stocks have lost more than $1 trillion in market value so far this week. The selling spread from Wall Street to Asia and Europe on Thursday. The cause, according to the report, is a combination of rate hikes, AI financing worries and intensifying competition, which is a thorough list for a sell-off that mostly comes down to one thing: borrowing is expensive.

The U.S. fell first. On Wednesday the Nasdaq Composite dropped 1.88% to 26,692.87 and the S&P 500 lost 1.11% to 7,718.94, after inflation data came in hotter than expected. Translation: prices are rising faster than hoped, so the Fed may keep rates high or raise them, and companies that need enormous amounts of credit begin to look less like visionaries and more like borrowers. The latest official figures show the 10-year Treasury yield at 5.19% and annual CPI inflation at 3.74%.

Asia took it harder. CNBC said SK Hynix fell more than 10%, Samsung Electronics 8.73%, and SoftBank 7.9%, while Advantest dropped almost 14%. In the U.S., Dell sank 6.8%, Intel about 2.8%, Nvidia 1.84% and AMD 1.5%. Micron, in a display of independence, closed up 1.87%.
