U.S. and Israel Reportedly Schedule Possible Iran Attack for After the Midterms, Out of Courtesy to the Ballot

An anonymous source told NPR the two allies are planning for a possible early-November strike, while Iran says it is prepared.

The wire said: U.S. and Israel are planning for a possible attack on Iran, source tells NPR · NPR World

By The Daily Spin Desk · Geopolitics2 min read

The United States and Israel are preparing plans for a possible attack on Iran in early November, after the U.S. midterm elections, according to a person briefed on war planning who spoke to NPR. Iran, for its part, said it is prepared. The source was not named, which is standard practice when discussing things governments would rather not be asked about on the record.

The timing is the detail that stands out. Early November falls after the midterms, which means that, if the report is accurate, the schedule leaves the voting finished before the shooting might start. Translation: a possible war is being slotted around the election calendar, the way a company avoids announcing layoffs the week before a shareholder meeting.

Ukraine's Internet Returns After Three Officials Explain Nobody Was in Charge of Turning It Off

Trump says Musk 'put it back' after Starlink speed caps hobbled Ukrainian drones, while Musk, Rubio and the White House each describe a different owner of the switch.

US President Donald Trump said on Thursday that Elon Musk has restored Ukraine's access to Starlink, the satellite internet service its military relies on. The BBC and Al Jazeera both reported the remarks, which followed days of restrictions that hampered Kyiv's forces. Ukraine thus learned that its communications can be switched off and on again, which is the standard first step in tech support and an unusual one in warfare.

The sequence matters. Ukrainian officials announced sudden restrictions on October 13, according to Al Jazeera, and Ukraine first reported an interruption to US intelligence sharing on October 14, according to the BBC. The Wall Street Journal reported that speeds were capped at around 160 km/h, and that Trump was frustrated by this. Translation: the internet had been slowed to the point that Ukraine's offensive drones and interceptors were, in the Journal's account, powerless against Russian attacks.

AI Finds Decade-Old PHP Flaw; Hackers, Reading Same Calendar, Arrive Four Days Early

OpenAI and Google models helped uncover the PHP2Shell vulnerabilities, but Amazon says attackers tried to exploit them four days before the public heard about it.

The developers who maintain PHP released software updates on Wednesday to fix two vulnerabilities that, according to Ars Technica, had gone undetected for more than a decade. The same day, the US National Security Agency told every organization using PHP to update immediately or take its systems offline. Translation: patch it now, or unplug it, which is the IT equivalent of choosing between a fire extinguisher and a smaller building.

The flaws, collectively nicknamed PHP2Shell, were uncovered with help from AI. The Decoder reported that OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol found the issue as part of an effort called Patch-the-Planet, which OpenAI describes as one of the "most impactful security issues in recent memory." Security firm Wiz said it found the same flaws independently, using its own platform alongside Google's Gemini 4 Argon and GPT-5.6 Sol. Two companies and several models arriving at the same hole is a reassuring sign of thoroughness, and also a sign that the hole was quite findable.

Chip Stocks Slide as AI Boom Discovers It Needs Money From Other People

Meta's negative free cash flow, a record credit-risk reading and a shelved Solidigm IPO sent the Nasdaq to its worst session since June.

The Nasdaq Composite fell 2.6% on Thursday, its worst session since June, according to CNBC. Nvidia dropped 4.4%, Advanced Micro Devices fell 8.1%, and MarketWatch said Micron and other chip stocks fell hard. Its three explanations were Tesla's lower spending target, a weaker forecast from Lam Research and a reminder that the AI boom relies on outside funding. The third reason is the interesting one, since it suggests the boom was less self-sustaining than advertised.

Meta supplied the clearest example. Its shares sank nearly 7% after third-quarter results showed free cash flow of negative $2.12 billion, CNBC reported. Translation: free cash flow is the money left after a company pays for its operations and its equipment. Negative means the company spent more than it earned, which is normally what a household calls a problem and a corporation calls an investment cycle.

Revenue was not the issue. Sales rose 26% from a year earlier to $64.60 billion, ahead of the $63.37 billion analysts expected. The trouble is spending: Meta now expects full-year capital expenditures of $135 billion to $145 billion, raising the bottom of its range from $130 billion. Translation: capex is money spent on physical things like servers and buildings. Meta moved its minimum spending up by $5 billion, the sort of adjustment most people make to a grocery budget.
