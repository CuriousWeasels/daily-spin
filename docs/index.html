Eight States Sue OpenAI, Alleging ChatGPT Was Marketed as Safer Than It Is

Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, West Virginia, Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho and Montana filed separate suits accusing the company of putting profits ahead of child safety.

Eight US states have sued OpenAI, alleging that ChatGPT is not safe for children and that the company misled the public about it, The Verge reported, citing earlier Reuters coverage. The suits were filed separately by Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, West Virginia, Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho and Montana. It is rare for eight states to agree on anything without a federal holiday attached, so the coordination is itself a data point.

The legal theory is consumer protection. Translation: the states are not claiming that chatbots are illegal, only that you cannot tell customers a product is safe if you allegedly knew otherwise. According to the complaints, OpenAI presented ChatGPT as a "safe, youth-appropriate tool that puts safety first," when it was in fact "dangerous to vulnerable users."

French Supermarket Fridges Hacked Into Ovens, Proving Password Was Basically the Calendar

An estimated 700 French supermarkets saw refrigeration systems heated to as high as 50°C after attackers reached internet-connected controllers, with some fires reported.

According to Ars Technica, a mass hack of refrigeration systems at an estimated 700 supermarkets in France caused them to malfunction this week, spoiling what the outlet called untold tons of perishables and, in some cases, causing fires. French authorities say the compromised equipment was a set of internet-connected controllers that technicians use to manage whole-store fleets of fridges and freezers. It is a rare cyberattack in which the stolen item is cold.

The attackers, who remain unknown, activated defrosters for long enough that the units reached temperatures as high as 50° Celsius, or 122° Fahrenheit. Translation: a freezer was persuaded to do the job of a mildly ambitious oven. By the time anyone noticed, the food was ruined and some electrical systems had caught fire.

Wholesale Prices Jump 6.3%, Fed Funds Traders Discover Rate Hikes Exist

September's producer price index rose 1.3% on the month, more than double forecasts, as the Iran war's energy shock spread into diesel, goods and the Fed's calendar.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Thursday that its producer price index rose 1.3% in September and 6.3% from a year earlier, according to CNBC. Economists polled by Dow Jones had forecast 0.6% and 5.5%. In forecasting terms, this is what is known as a miss, and in household terms it is what is known as the bill.

Translation: the producer price index measures what businesses receive for goods and services at the wholesale level, before a retailer adds its own markup and a sad little sign about supply chains. When wholesale prices rise, the cost tends to be passed along, with the speed of a rumor and the generosity of a toll booth.

Energy did much of the work, as the conflict with Iran curtailed oil supply. Brent crude stood at 125.44 as of Oct. 6, per the latest official data. The more interesting detail is the spread: the BLS said almost half of September's rise in processed goods for immediate demand was tied to diesel prices. Diesel moves trucks, trucks move everything, and so a war far away ends up in the price of nearly anything that sits on a pallet.
