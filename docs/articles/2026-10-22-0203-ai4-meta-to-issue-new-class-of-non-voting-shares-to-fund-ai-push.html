Meta Invents New Kind of Stock So AI Bills Can Be Paid by Other People

The company will create non-voting shares, tradable from 2027, to fund AI, acquisitions and employee pay, while expecting to spend $135 billion to $145 billion on capital expenditures this year.

The wire said: Meta to create a new class of shares to fund AI push · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 22, 2026 02:03 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Meta announced alongside its third-quarter earnings that it will issue a new class of non-voting shares. The company says the money will fund AI work, possible acquisitions and employee compensation, with trading starting in 2027.

Why it matters

Investors may soon be able to own a slice of Meta's AI spending without getting any say in it, which is how much of the AI boom already works for everyone else. It also signals that even one of the world's richest companies sees AI costs as large enough to need new financing tools.

Meta said on Wednesday, in its third-quarter earnings report, that it is creating a new class of shares to help pay for its AI ambitions. According to The Verge, the stock will be non-voting, will also cover potential acquisitions and employee compensation, and will begin trading in 2027. The company has therefore found a way to ask the public for money while keeping the public out of the meetings.

Translation: a non-voting share is ownership with the decision-making removed. You get a claim on the profits, and the people already running the company keep running it. This is a familiar arrangement for founder-led companies, and it tends to be popular with founders.

The company's own explanation was earnest. Its press release said the scale of investment required to compete amid the AI transformation "is significant." Chief financial officer Susan Li told analysts the shares would "expand our funding tools" and give flexibility to invest behind what she called unprecedented momentum. Translation: the checkbook is large, but the invoices are larger.

The invoices are real. Meta expects capital expenditures of $135 billion to $145 billion this year, according to The Verge, up from a prior range of $130 billion to $145 billion. Translation: capital expenditures means money spent on things like data centers and chips. The company raised the bottom of its range by $5 billion, which is the sort of adjustment that only counts as modest in a context where $5 billion is a rounding error.

The spending buys data centers, talent poached from rivals such as OpenAI, and models meant to compete with those from other tech giants. It also buys products, and Meta has one to show off. Its AI agent, Muse, launched in September, can clean out an inbox, book restaurant reservations and plan trips, and reached 10 million US downloads this week after climbing to the top of app stores. The company also rolled out a model called Muse Wonder.

There have been snags. The Verge reported that a tech YouTuber said the agent gave his address to a stranger on Facebook Marketplace, and that Meta paused Muse's conversation-sharing feature after researchers found chats publicly available on the web. An assistant that books your dinner and also publishes your conversations is, at least, thorough.

The mechanism is straightforward. Building AI at this scale costs more than even a very profitable ad business may comfortably cover, so Meta wants more ways to raise money, and stock that comes with no votes is the cheapest thing it can offer in governance terms. Whether buyers will pay full price for a share of the future with no say in how it is spent is a question that has until 2027 to answer itself.
