AI Giants Slash Prices Up to 80%, Insist They Are Merely Competing

Over four months, OpenAI and Anthropic repeatedly undercut themselves and each other, while cheap open-weight models waited patiently by the exit.

The wire said: OpenAI and Anthropic cut their LLM prices by up to 80% within a few months. Why? · Hacker News

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 26, 2026 01:55 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

OpenAI and Anthropic, the two leading makers of commercial AI models, have released a rapid string of new models and price cuts since June 2026. A Hacker News-hosted analysis lays out the timeline and asks why prices fell so far without apparent loss of quality.

Why it matters

For developers and companies that pay per token, AI coding tools are getting much cheaper, which undermines the popular theory that prices must eventually spike. It also suggests the leading labs sell something closer to a commodity than they would like investors to believe.

According to a timeline published on Hacker News, the price of using flagship models from OpenAI and Anthropic has dropped sharply since June 2026. OpenAI cut its small GPT-5.6 Luna model by 80% on July 30, to $0.20 per million input tokens. Neither company calls this a price war. Both simply keep announcing cheaper things on suspiciously close dates.

Some background. Translation: a token is a chunk of text, roughly a word fragment, and AI companies bill per million of them, with separate rates for input, output and "cache reads." A cache read is the model re-reading the conversation so far, which it does every turn. The author says that in agentic coding, cache reads make up 96% to 99% of token spending, so the least glamorous line on the price sheet is the one that matters.

That explains Anthropic's September 1 launch of Fable 5.1. Its headline price stayed at $10 input and $50 output, but cache reads fell 75%, from $1 to $0.25. By the author's math, that works out to a 70% to 75% cut in real cost. Translation: the sticker price held still while the bill fell by two-thirds, a trick usually performed in the other direction.

The tit-for-tat then sped up. On September 22, OpenAI released GPT-6 Sol at $2 / $10, half the temporarily discounted GPT-5.6 Sol. The same day Anthropic released Opus 5.5 at $4 / $20 and said it "requires less compute to serve than Opus 5." The author puts the total cost reduction at 40% to 60%. A week later, OpenAI followed with GPT-6.1 Sol after the first version turned out, in the author's words, to be a dud, and halved its cache price.

The author's explanation is that models have become interchangeable. Switching takes a toggle in a coding tool or a changed endpoint string, so loyalty is thin. Meanwhile open-weight models, whose parameters anyone can download and host, are closing in. DeepSeek V4 Flash went viral in April at $0.14 / $0.28, and the author suspects the Luna cut was aimed squarely at it. Providers hosting it on OpenRouter rose from 13 in May to 29 for the updated version.

Hosting competition is the key mechanism. When 29 companies can sell the same model, the price drifts toward the cost of electricity and chips. Moonshot AI's Kimi K3.1 Pro, the author says, scores 55 against Opus 5.5's 58 at half the input and output price. Over 20 providers already host its predecessor, and the weights are due on November 4.

The author also takes on a popular theory among AI skeptics: that today's prices are an Uber-style subsidy and will rise once customers are hooked. The excerpt provided cuts off before the full rebuttal, so it is unclear how the author finishes the argument. But the timeline itself is awkward evidence for the theory, since the companies keep lowering prices while quality improves.

The labs' answer is to stay ahead with better models, which is why, as the author notes, billions of dollars go into research. It is an expensive way to remain slightly more expensive than a free download. Watch November 4, when Kimi's weights arrive and a few dozen hosting companies get to set the price themselves.
