Administration Keeps Secret Surveillance Ruling Secret Until Congress Agrees to Renew Surveillance

Two senators say the government missed its own 180-day deadline to release a FISA court opinion, even as officials ask Congress to renew Section 702 by year's end.

The wire said: The US government is hiding secret court ruling about how it spies on Americans · Hacker News

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 27, 2026 16:54 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Section 702 of FISA lets US intelligence agencies collect foreign communications, and in the process sweep up Americans' messages that agents can later search. The program lapsed in June, but collection continues under a March court approval that expires next March.

Why it matters

Congress is being asked to renew a warrantless surveillance authority without seeing a court's findings about how agents search Americans' messages. If the ruling stays sealed through the vote, lawmakers and the public will be debating the program on incomplete information.

Senators Ron Wyden of Oregon and Adam Schiff of California say the Trump administration is illegally withholding a Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court ruling from the public until Congress renews Section 702, according to WIRED and Ars Technica. The ruling, issued seven months ago, objected to search filters that let analysts pull Americans' messages from intercepted data without the approvals, written justifications, or records the law requires. The court approved another year of surveillance anyway, which is the legal equivalent of a restaurant inspector writing up the kitchen and then renewing the license.

The administration's own public documentation says such rulings are reviewed for release "within 180 days of issuance." Translation: the government set its own deadline for telling people what the court said, and then let it pass. That window closed September 13, while intelligence officials were asking lawmakers to reauthorize the program by the end of the year.

The senators have demanded an explanation, and the names of the officials responsible, by November 6. In their letter to Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton and Attorney General Todd Blanche, they wrote that if the administration won't follow this "simple statutory directive," it is unclear why anyone should trust it to follow the other legal limits on Section 702. That is a polite way of asking what else is in the drawer.

The administration has been asked before, and Blanche has said yes before. In April, Republican chair Tom Cotton and Democratic vice chair Mark Warner of the Intelligence Committee pressed for release within 15 days. In July, asked in writing whether he would declassify the ruling by the 180-day deadline, Blanche replied, "Yes." Warner's office says neither former DNI Tulsi Gabbard nor Bill Pulte, who briefly replaced her as acting director, was responsive. Clayton, who is legally responsible for declassifying it, has promised only that the government was "working arduously to expeditiously declassify" it.

Some of the contents have leaked out in pieces. The New York Times reported in April, citing administration talking points, that the court found the tools used to narrow intercepted data to an American's messages amount to searches of Americans, subject to the same limits, and ordered them rebuilt. Senator Alex Padilla said the Justice Department is appealing portions of the ruling. An earlier declassified opinion described an FBI "participants filter" that had been running searches no one counted, which is an efficient way to achieve a perfect compliance record: do not count.

Meanwhile, WIRED reports that seven intelligence agency fact sheets from March, one dated the day of the ruling, cited a 98.9 percent compliance rate for FBI searches of Americans. None mentioned the court's findings. The Justice Department inspector general's October 2025 review put the rate at 98.6 percent and could not conclude that compliance problems were "entirely in the past." The fact sheets also say FBI auditors review every such search, while FBI director Kash Patel closed the bureau's Office of Internal Auditing last May and moved its auditors elsewhere.

Lawmakers who have read the ruling suggest it is worth the trip to the secure room. Senator Dick Durbin said that once colleagues see it, "your opinion of the role of FISA and the rights of Americans will be impacted for sure." Demand Progress and 22 other groups, from the ACLU to a conservative-backed privacy organization, urged congressional leaders to refuse reauthorization while the ruling is withheld.

Advocates expect the administration may try to attach a renewal to the annual defense authorization bill, which Congress usually finalizes in December. Lawmakers did something similar in 2023, extending Section 702 for four months through that year's defense bill. The current versions do not yet include it. Watch November 6: the senators have set a deadline, and the administration's record with deadlines is now a matter of public record.
