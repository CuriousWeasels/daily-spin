Power Event at One London Data Centre Briefly Unplugs Banks, Couriers and Courts

A single Google Cloud site, europe-west2, disrupted Revolut, Lloyds, HSBC, Royal Mail, DPD and court video hearings on Tuesday, exactly a year after a similar Amazon outage.

The wire said: Google says services recovering after cloud outage hits UK banks and websites · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 20, 2026 15:53 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Google Cloud reported an "issue with multiple services" at its London data centre complex on Tuesday morning after what it called a "power event." UK banks, delivery firms and the courts service reported disruption, and Google later said most customers were no longer affected.

Why it matters

When a few cloud providers host much of the internet, one building's electrical problem can stop people paying for parking or small firms shipping perishable vegetables. The incident is another reminder that redundancy is a feature customers must buy and test, not one they can assume.

Google Cloud said on Tuesday that services were recovering after a "power event" at its London data centre complex, known as europe-west2, disrupted banks, businesses and the courts. According to the BBC, Revolut, Lloyds Bank and HSBC confirmed problems with some services that morning, after thousands of user reports online. The modern economy, it turns out, can be paused by a power problem in one part of one city.

Translation: a cloud provider rents out computing power from large data centres so that companies do not have to run their own. This is efficient and cheap, until many companies discover they have all rented rooms in the same building.

The banks were careful with their wording. The BBC reported that none of them directly linked their problems to Google. Revolut's spokesperson did confirm a third-party issue and said "all our customers' money and data are safe." Lloyds, which also owns Halifax and Bank of Scotland, did not confirm a cause but said it understood the root issues had been resolved. Google, for its part, apologised "to all who are affected by the disruption." Everyone apologised, and nobody was quite responsible.

The human cost was small but specific. Rachael Ellen, a Revolut customer in Glasgow, told the BBC she could not pay for parking at a shopping centre, and the app's help bot told her no incoming transactions were pending when a relative had sent one. Other customers reported problems ranging from reaching money abroad to completing a property purchase. Royal Mail, DPD and e-bike service Forest were also flagged by users, and HM Courts and Tribunals Service said the video platform for remote hearings was affected.

Small businesses felt it differently. Guy Baybutt of The Veg Box Company in Preston said his firm could not reach its website to process orders, could not book parcels onto DPD's system, and lost new sales. He told the BBC that firms like his cannot absorb lost revenue, especially with perishable produce. A bank can issue a statement; a lettuce cannot.

By Tuesday afternoon, Google's health dashboard said the issues had been "mitigated for most of the customers," with a small number still affected. The company promised an analysis once its internal investigation is complete. Translation: the explanation of what happened will arrive after everyone has stopped needing it.

The timing carried its own commentary. The BBC noted the outage came exactly one year after an Amazon Web Services failure at its largest cloud site knocked more than 1,000 sites offline, including UK banks. Prof Feng Li of Bayes Business School said the incident shows how deeply economies depend on a handful of providers, adding that it was not yet clear what caused the power problem. He also offered the line that matters most: failures will happen, and what counts is how far they spread and whether customers have workable alternatives.

That last point is the awkward one. Spreading workloads across several data centres or several providers costs more money and effort, and it is hard to justify until the day it is needed. Watch for Google's post-incident report, and for whether the banks say anything about their own backup plans.
