Florida DMV Breach Traced to Police Login Stored Where Anyone Could Find It

Hackers used compromised credentials to reach driver's license records, and the state says it may not know where to send the apology letters.

The wire said: Florida DMV hacked after police dept insecurely stored login info · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 20, 2026 02:16 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Florida's Department of Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles says a malicious actor accessed department records on September 4, 2026, using compromised credentials. Ars Technica reported the headline cause as a police department insecurely storing login information.

Why it matters

Driver's license data, including document images, is the kind of information that makes identity theft easier, and it does not expire when the credit monitoring does. Anyone who has ever held a Florida license, current resident or not, has reason to check their accounts.

Florida's motor vehicle agency has begun notifying people affected by a data breach of driver's license and motor vehicle records, Ars Technica reported. In a notice posted Friday, the department said a malicious actor accessed certain records on September 4, 2026, using compromised credentials. Translation: someone had a valid key, so the lock was not really consulted.

According to Ars Technica, the credentials trace back to a police department that stored its login information insecurely. This is the digital equivalent of writing the vault code on a sticky note and attaching it to the vault, except that the note was apparently attached to a different building.

The department says it found no evidence that the data has been misused so far. It also says law enforcement determined the affected information potentially includes driver's license and motor vehicle data, including document images and other personal information. The phrase 'no evidence yet' is doing the work of a small, overworked intern: it describes what has been found, not what is happening.

Victims are being sent letters offering one year of credit monitoring from Sontiq, Inc. Translation: a company will watch your credit report for twelve months, after which the risk stays exactly where it was and the watching stops. Stolen identity details are famously not subject to a one-year warranty.

Not everyone will receive a letter. The state acknowledged it may lack current contact information for many former Florida residents whose data it was still storing. So the department kept records on people it can no longer reach, which is a retention policy that works well in every scenario except this one. Those people can email customerinquiry@flhsmv.gov to find out if they were affected.

Timing has also drawn complaints. Some residents said they received notice only this week, though the breach happened more than a month ago, and news outlets had already reported it. Ars Technica noted that several Floridians on a state subreddit had already frozen their credit or placed fraud alerts, which makes Reddit the faster notification system.

The department confirmed that an 'international cybercriminal organization' was behind the breach but did not say which group or how long it had access. Ars Technica's reporting linked the incident to ShinyHunters. The department said it promptly mitigated the breach, launched an investigation and notified state authorities as required.

For everyone else, the department recommends monitoring financial accounts, reviewing credit reports and considering a fraud alert or security freeze. That is a sensible list. It is also a list of chores assigned to the victims by the organization that held the data.
