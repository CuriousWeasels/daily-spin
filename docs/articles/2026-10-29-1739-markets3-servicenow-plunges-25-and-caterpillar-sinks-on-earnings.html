ServiceNow Falls 25% After Meeting Expectations, Which Turns Out to Be Insufficient

The enterprise software firm posted $4.11 billion in revenue and raised its outlook, yet had its worst day ever as investors again asked whether AI will eat the software business.

The wire said: ServiceNow shares plunge 25% after earnings revive AI disruption fears · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 29, 2026 17:39 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

ServiceNow sells cloud software that large companies use to run internal workflows. Software stocks have been under pressure on fears, nicknamed the "SaaSpocalypse," that AI tools will undermine the subscription model the industry relies on.

Why it matters

A 25% drop in a major software company on in-line results shows how little room for error investors now allow, which matters for anyone whose retirement account holds tech stocks. It also suggests the market is paying for proof of AI profits, not promises.

ServiceNow shares fell 25% on Thursday and were headed for the worst day in the company's history, CNBC reported, after third-quarter results failed to impress. Revenue grew more than 20% to $4.11 billion, against a $4.10 billion estimate from analysts polled by LSEG. By the standards of ordinary commerce, this is called doing what you said you would do.

Adjusted earnings came in at $1.00 per share, against a forecast of $1.02. Translation: the company earned two cents per share less than predicted, a gap roughly the size of the change at the bottom of a couch, and the market valued it at a quarter of the company.

The company also slightly raised its full-year 2026 subscription revenue outlook to between $15.78 billion and $15.79 billion, and net new AI average contract value jumped 65%. Translation: the average size of newly signed AI deals grew sharply. This did not help. Citi's Tyler Radke told CNBC the quarter showed "the lack of upside" that ServiceNow customarily delivers.

The deeper problem is the "SaaSpocalypse," the fear that new AI capabilities will upend traditional software business models. SaaS means software as a service, the practice of renting programs by subscription. If AI agents can do the work that employees once needed software to manage, the logic goes, fewer companies will need as many subscriptions. ServiceNow's results did little to settle that anxiety, according to CNBC.

Radke also pointed to the lack of immediate payoff from ServiceNow's newly formed forward-deployed engineering team, which embeds with customers to build AI solutions. Translation: the company is sending engineers to live inside client firms and build things, which costs money now and earns it later. "I think you got to wait until next year to really see that inflection," Radke said. Inflection, in this context, means the point when spending finally turns into growth.

The wider tape was not a sea of calm either. MarketWatch reported that Caterpillar was heading for its biggest loss in years, even though its data-center business was described as fine and other areas disappointed. Official data show the S&P 500 tracker SPY at 768.14 as of Oct. 29 and the VIX, the market's fear gauge, at 16.91 as of Oct. 28, a level that does not suggest panic. Investors, it seems, are calm in general and ruthless in particular.

The lesson for the software industry is that growing 20% and raising guidance is now the entry fee, not the prize. Investors want evidence that AI adds profit before they will stop imagining it subtracting some. Radke's waiting game runs until next year; the stock market's patience, as Thursday showed, runs until roughly the next earnings call.
