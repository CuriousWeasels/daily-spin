Housing Market Enters Deep Sleep as Fed Weighs Rate Hikes, Which Are Known Sleep Aids

Pending home sales hit a record low in data going back to 2001, mortgage rates sit near 7.4%, and the Fed's next move may be up.

The wire said: Housing is flashing warning signs just as the Fed weighs more interest rate hikes · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 21, 2026 16:10 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Mortgage rates have climbed sharply over the past year while inflation has stayed stubborn, leaving the Federal Reserve with little room to help housing. New data from the National Association of Realtors and the Commerce Department show the market weakening further in September.

Why it matters

Anyone hoping to buy, sell, or refinance a home faces borrowing costs more than a full percentage point above last year's, with no relief scheduled. Because the Fed's attention is on inflation, housing is unlikely to get help soon.

The National Association of Realtors reported Tuesday that its pending home sales index, which counts homes under contract but not yet sold, fell 7.3% in September to the lowest level in records going back to 2001. It was down 11.3% from a year earlier. Wall Street had expected a decline of 0.4%, which is the sort of miss usually described as a surprise by people who were not trying to buy a house.

Translation: pending sales are a forward-looking measure, so they show what closings will look like in the coming weeks, not what already happened. A record low here is less a snapshot than a weather forecast, and the forecast is fog.

The Commerce Department added to the mood. According to CNBC, housing starts fell 5.2% in the month against an expected drop of 0.4%, and building permits slipped 0.2% when economists had penciled in a 2% gain. Single-family permits totaled 845,000, the lowest since 2023. Builder sentiment, CNBC noted, is at its weakest in 14 years.

The main culprit is the mortgage rate. CNBC reported that Mortgage News Daily had the 30-year rate above 7% for most of September and ending near 7.6%. Five years ago it was closer to 3%. The Mortgage Bankers Association's weekly survey showed a slight dip to 7.41% from 7.50%, its first decline in more than two months. Applications fell anyway: total volume dropped 5.3%, purchase applications fell 7%, and refinancing fell 2%.

This is where the lock-in effect comes in. Translation: homeowners with cheap old mortgages do not want to sell and trade them for expensive new ones, so they stay put, and fewer homes reach the market. Goldman Sachs economist Ronnie Walker noted that more than 95% of mortgage borrowers have rates below the current level, and 65% are at least 2 percentage points below. Goldman expects existing home sales of 3.9 million in 2027, about 26% below 2019 levels.

Some buyers are adapting. The MBA said adjustable-rate mortgages made up 10.5% of applications, with rates almost a full percentage point lower than the 30-year fixed. Translation: an ARM is a loan that starts cheap and later resets to whatever the market charges, which is a bet that the future will be kinder than the present.

The Fed cannot easily rescue anyone. Wholesale final demand goods prices rose 2.6%, the fastest pace in a data series dating to December 2009, according to CNBC. Fed officials are also watching price pressure from artificial intelligence investment, which is raising the cost of chips and electronics, though they believe that investment could offset weakness in sectors like housing. In other words, the plan for housing is that someone else's boom will cover it.

Markets expect the Fed to hold rates next week, but CME Group's FedWatch tool implies a strong chance of quarter-point hikes in December and March 2027. Chris Rupkey of Fwdbonds wrote that housing is heading for a deep freeze. The NAR's Lawrence Yun offered a counterpoint: because mortgage rates have risen far more than the Fed's own hikes, they may be topping out. Buyers and sellers, he said, may simply be waiting for better rates.

That is a fair description of the entire market at the moment: everyone is waiting for someone else to move first.
