Florida DMV Tells Former Residents to Email Itself to Learn Whether It Lost Their Data

A breach of driver's license records, reportedly linked to ShinyHunters, has prompted letters to current residents and an email address for everyone the state can no longer locate.

The wire said: Huge DMV data breach impacted current and former Florida residents · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 19, 2026 17:49 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Florida's Department of Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles says a malicious actor used compromised credentials on September 4, 2026 to access department records. The agency has since begun sending breach notices, according to Ars Technica.

Why it matters

Driver's license data, including document images, is hard to replace and useful for identity fraud, so anyone who has ever held a Florida license has reason to watch their credit. The case also shows that data kept on people who left a state does not leave with them.

The Florida Department of Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles has begun notifying people affected by a breach of driver's license and motor vehicle data, Ars Technica reported. In a notice posted Friday, the department said a malicious actor accessed certain records on September 4, 2026, using compromised credentials. Translation: someone had a valid login, which is the digital equivalent of walking into the building because the door was held open.

The department said law enforcement determined the potentially affected information includes driver license and motor vehicle data, including document images, and other personal information. It said there is no evidence so far that the data has been misused. Note the phrase 'so far': it is the standard legal way of saying the story has not finished yet.

The state is mailing letters to everyone affected and offering one year of credit monitoring from Sontiq, Inc. Translation: a service that watches for someone borrowing your identity, offered for a period considerably shorter than the time your identity will remain borrowable.

Here the process hits a snag. The department acknowledged it may not have current contact information for many former Florida residents whose data it was still storing. So the state kept their records long after it lost track of the people, and the notification system depends on addresses it may no longer have. Those people can check whether they were affected by emailing customerinquiry@flhsmv.gov. In effect, the agency is asking those it cannot find to find it.

Officials recommended monitoring financial accounts and reviewing credit reports, along with placing a fraud alert or security freeze with the major credit bureaus. According to Ars Technica, several Floridians on the state's subreddit had already done the third item, which suggests the public is somewhat ahead of the notification schedule. Some residents complained they received notice only this week, though the breach occurred more than a month ago and had previously been reported by news outlets.

The department said an international cybercriminal organization was behind the incident, though it did not name the group or say how long the intruders had access. The Ars Technica report's own subhead links the hack to ShinyHunters, a group name the agency itself did not confirm. The department said it promptly mitigated the breach, launched an investigation and notified state authorities as required.

What to watch next: whether any of the stolen data surfaces for sale, and whether a year of credit monitoring turns out to be the right length. Criminals are not known for honoring the terms of a service plan.
