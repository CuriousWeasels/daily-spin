GM Pays $220 Million to Settle Claim It Sold Drivers' Habits to Insurers

The automaker will pay D.C. and 35 states after its Smart Driver data ended up with insurance companies, and an outside party will now check its homework.

The wire said: GM to pay $220 million after selling customer Smart Driver data · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 30, 2026 17:17 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

GM marketed Smart Driver as a tool for monitoring teen drivers or one's own habits, but in 2024 it emerged that the data was being shared with brokers who sold it to insurers. GM already settled with the FTC, which was finalized in January.

Why it matters

The data your car collects about how you drive can end up affecting your insurance bill, and the settlement shows regulators are starting to treat that as a legal problem rather than a feature. For drivers, it is a reminder to read what the dashboard is agreeing to on your behalf.

General Motors will pay $220 million to settle a lawsuit brought by the District of Columbia and 35 states, Ars Technica reported, over its handling of data from the Smart Driver system. The system was pitched as a way for parents to watch teen drivers or for adults to monitor their own habits. It was apparently also a way for other people to watch them, for a fee.

According to Ars Technica, it emerged in 2024 that GM had been sharing the data with brokers. Translation: data brokers are middlemen who buy information from one company and resell it to another, a business model that requires no one to ask the person the information is about. The brokers then sold it to insurance companies, and some drivers found their premiums rose as a result.

Many of those drivers said they had never knowingly agreed to any of this. That is the crux of the case. A driver who clicks through a screen to get a feature is not necessarily consenting to a second, unadvertised career for their braking habits.

This is not GM's first round. Last year GM and its OnStar subsidiary reached a settlement with the Federal Trade Commission, finalized this January, banning them from sharing geolocation or driver behavior data with third parties for five years. GM also discontinued Smart Driver across its entire range. Translation: the product was shut down after the part where it was supposed to help drivers turned out to be the smaller part of the business.

The state deal adds a notable feature, according to TechCrunch: a third party must assess GM's compliance with the new privacy program. In other words, the company that mishandled the data will now be graded by someone who is not the company. That is a modest idea, and in corporate privacy it counts as a bold reform.

GM, for its part, told Reuters that connected vehicle data enables "safer, more helpful and personalized experiences," and that it is committed to being clear about what information is used and what choices customers have. The sources do not say whether the statement was drafted before or after the customers found out what the choices had been.

The sources do not detail how the $220 million will be divided among the states, or whether affected drivers will receive anything. What to watch next is whether the outside assessor ever publishes anything, and whether other automakers with their own driving-score programs decide to read the settlement carefully.
