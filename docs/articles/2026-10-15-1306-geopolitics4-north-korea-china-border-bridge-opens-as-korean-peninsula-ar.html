North Korea and China Open Bridge Finished in 2014, Just as Soon as Someone Built the Road

The New Yalu River Bridge opened Thursday after a 12-year wait, while both Koreas keep testing hypersonic missiles that travel across borders considerably faster than trucks.

The wire said: North Korea, China open border bridge after 12-year delay (DW)

By The Daily Spin Desk · Geopolitics Oct 15, 2026 13:06 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

China and North Korea opened the New Yalu River Bridge, a cable-stayed crossing between Dandong and Sinuiju that was completed in 2014 but never used. Its opening comes as North and South Korea trade missile tests and as Beijing and Pyongyang repair relations after the pandemic border closure.

Why it matters

China is already North Korea's largest trading partner despite UN sanctions, so a larger crossing shapes how much cash and equipment reaches Pyongyang. Meanwhile, faster missiles on both sides push Japan and South Korea toward costly debates about missile defense.

China and North Korea on Thursday officially opened the New Yalu River Bridge, their most significant land border crossing, according to DW, citing Chinese state media Xinhua. The cable-stayed bridge was finished in 2014. It has since waited 12 years for the formality of a road to drive onto, which is a good reminder that the hard part of infrastructure is usually the part after the picture.

Yonhap reported that the bridge sat unused because the North Korean side lacked connecting roads. The pandemic then prompted Pyongyang to close its borders, which delayed matters further. Even on opening day, DW noted, work on the approach road and compound in Sinuiju remained unfinished. Translation: the ribbon was cut on a bridge whose far end is still a construction site, but the flags were in place, and flags are the part that photographs well.

Trucks decorated with Chinese and North Korean flags crossed during the ceremony, and spectators crowded a road on the Chinese side in Dandong. Premier Li Qiang said in a video address that relations were "entering a new phase." He added that the crossing would strongly promote economic and trade cooperation. Xinhua carried the remarks, which is where one would expect to find them.

Analysts quoted by DW and AFP expect real traffic. Ahn Chan-il, a researcher originally from North Korea, told AFP he expects hundreds of vehicles a day, carrying China's abundant goods and resources into the North. Lim Eul-chul of Kyungnam University was more measured: the bridge is unlikely to bring an immediate full return to pre-sanctions trade, but it will help move heavy equipment and raw materials North Korea needs for development. Translation: not a flood, but a bigger tap.

There are limits. Train service between the two countries restarted in March after six years, yet Pyongyang has not resumed issuing tourism visas to Chinese citizens, who once made up the bulk of its overseas visitors. Experts also believe North Korea remains wary of depending too heavily on China. DW noted that China is already by far Pyongyang's largest trading partner, despite UN sanctions banning the flow of goods and workers over its missile and nuclear programs. A bigger bridge to a sanctioned economy is, at minimum, an interesting compliance question.

The same week brought reminders of what the sanctions are about. North Korea said Wednesday it tested two types of submarine-launched cruise missiles off its east coast, a day after state media said Kim Jong Un admired an intercontinental ballistic missile at a new defense industry museum. On October 3, Pyongyang announced a hypersonic intermediate-range missile test that traveled 350 kilometers. South Korean President Lee Jae-myung observed his country's first domestically developed hypersonic missile test five days before Kim's museum visit.

Hypersonic means at least Mach 5, or over 6,100 kilometers per hour, and the weapons can dip or weave in flight, making them harder to intercept. Chris Green of the International Crisis Group told DW the arms race is long-running and hypersonics are the latest chapter. He said it is impossible to confirm North Korea's claims, but that underestimating Pyongyang has repeatedly been a mistake. Retired South Korean general Chun In-bum said there is currently "no good answer" to the threat, though he stressed he was speculating.

Green does not think the balance of power has changed, since each side's systems may cancel out. But if the North deploys them in huge numbers, he said, a conversation about greater missile defense in South Korea and Japan will follow, raising very difficult questions. Those questions are expected to arrive faster than the road to the bridge did.
