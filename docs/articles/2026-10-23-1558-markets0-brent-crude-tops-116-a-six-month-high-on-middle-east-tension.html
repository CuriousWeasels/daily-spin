Brent Crude Hits $116 as Markets Price in Sequel to Iran Conflict

Oil reached a six-month high on Friday as investors bet the U.S. will resume strikes on Iran, while Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia keep the supply picture unsettled.

The wire said: Brent crude oil tops $116, a six-month high, as tensions swell in the Middle East · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 23, 2026 15:58 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Tensions between the U.S. and Iran have been rising, and Iran-allied Houthi militants have been striking targets in Saudi Arabia. Oil traders are now pricing in the possibility of renewed American strikes.

Why it matters

Higher crude prices feed through to gasoline, shipping and airfares, which complicates the fight against inflation that is already running at 3.74%. Investors face a bond market and an energy market pulling in uncomfortable directions.

Brent crude futures rose 2.6% to $116.13 a barrel on Friday, the highest since April 30, according to CNBC. U.S. benchmark West Texas Intermediate traded about 3% higher at $102.56. Brent was on track for a weekly gain of nearly 6%, its third straight weekly rise, which is the market's way of saying it has stopped hoping the news will improve.

The immediate cause is expectation. Investors increasingly believe the U.S. will resume strikes on Iran in the coming weeks. Translation: oil traders are paying today for a disruption that has not happened yet, which is how futures markets work and also how they ruin your commute.

The official messaging has not helped. CNBC reported that President Donald Trump said Wednesday the U.S. was not negotiating with Iran and that something would happen "fairly soon." U.S. officials also told The New York Times that the U.S. and Israel have plans for fresh strikes after the midterm elections. So the plan is reportedly real, the timing is reportedly electoral, and the price of oil is reportedly not waiting for the ballot count.

Iran's army chief, per the state agency Tasnim, said the military is fully prepared for "asymmetric warfare." Translation: fighting by means other than matching the opponent's aircraft carriers, such as drones, proxies and attacks on things that are expensive to defend. Markets read this as a reminder that the other side also has a calendar.

The physical risk is concrete. Houthi militants have kept striking Saudi Arabia, and the State Department ordered all non-essential personnel to leave the country. A drone attack on the Shaybah oil field on Wednesday left two people dead and two injured, according to Saudi state media, and at least two were killed and eight injured in an attack on King Fahd International Airport in Dammam. Reuters reported that Saudi Aramco told staff to evacuate its Dhahran headquarters and work remotely, which suggests the world's most important oil company has concluded that the office is the least safe place to keep an oil company.

For ordinary people, the arithmetic is unforgiving. Crude is the raw material for gasoline, diesel and jet fuel, so a higher price tends to show up at the pump and in the cost of moving everything else. Inflation stood at 3.74% year on year in the latest official reading, and the Federal Reserve's policy rate is 3.88%, so the central bank has little room to call this temporary and watch.

Bond markets are already nervous. The 10-year Treasury yield was 5.13% and the 2-year was 4.81% as of Oct. 21. Translation: the government is paying more to borrow, and so, eventually, will anyone with a mortgage or a business loan. Stocks, meanwhile, have shrugged, with the S&P 500 fund SPY at 762.89 and the VIX, the market's fear gauge, at a middling 19.03.

That calm may be the most interesting number of all. Equities are priced as if the oil problem is manageable, while crude is priced as if it is not. One of them will turn out to be wrong, and the midterm elections have been helpfully named as the deadline for finding out.
