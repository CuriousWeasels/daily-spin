FCC Uses New Chip-Blocking Power for First Time, Targets Camera Component

Six weeks after granting itself authority to block gadgets containing chips from blacklisted firms, the agency has blocked two devices, though it won't say which.

The wire said: The FCC is beginning to block gadgets with ‘foreign surveillance chips’ · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 23, 2026 02:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

On October 13th, the FCC under Brendan Carr gave itself the power to block devices containing chips made by any company on its Covered List of national security threats. The Verge reports it has now used that power for the first time.

Why it matters

Cheap cameras and dashcams often rely on components most buyers never see, so a ban on a chip can quietly remove products from shelves. Whether this improves security or just reshuffles the market is a question the agency has so far declined to discuss.

The Federal Communications Commission has blocked two gadgets on the grounds that they contain what it calls "foreign surveillance chips," according to The Verge. It is the first use of a power the agency granted itself on October 13th. The agency did not say which two gadgets were blocked, which makes this an enforcement action in the way a locked door with no sign on it is a policy.

The chip it did name is the HiVision Hi3516CV610 UHD Smart Vision SoC. Translation: an SoC, or system on a chip, is a single piece of silicon that does the work of several, and this one turns a raw camera sensor into a home security camera, a dashcam, or something similar. The Verge says it is often sold in a bundle with a Sony sensor and sometimes a lens, according to listings viewed on AliExpress.

The FCC's reasoning has two parts. First, HiVision advertises the chip as designed for the surveillance market, with built-in detection algorithms for human faces, the agency explains. Second, HiVision is a subsidiary of Huawei, which was among the first telecom companies the US government labeled a national security risk. Translation: a surveillance camera chip was described by its maker as being for surveillance, which the FCC has chosen to treat as a confession.

Huawei is on the FCC's Covered List, a roster of companies whose equipment the agency considers a threat. The new power is meant to close a loophole in which a banned company's parts arrive inside another company's finished product. The Verge notes the agency did something similar when it gave itself the power to retroactively ban gadgets and to block modular radio transmitters. The pattern is clear: each time a gap appears, the rulebook grows a patch over it.

The practical effect lands on makers and buyers of inexpensive cameras, who may not know whose silicon sits inside the box. A manufacturer that once needed to check its own supply chain now also needs to check its suppliers' suppliers, against a list that can change.

The Verge raises the question it has asked before, about the router ban and the Roomba ban: is the FCC keeping people safer, or picking winners and losers while making a show of enforcement? The outlet says it has asked the agency repeatedly to discuss its efforts, and the agency will not engage. For now, the best evidence of the policy's wisdom is the policy's own assurance, delivered without comment.
