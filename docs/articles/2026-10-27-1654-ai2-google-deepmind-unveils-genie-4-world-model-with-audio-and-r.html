Google's Genie 4 Builds Realistic Worlds That Last Two Minutes, Then Forget Them

DeepMind's new world model adds audio, better physics and real-time edits, but it launches only to trusted testers and Ultra subscribers.

The wire said: DeepMind unveils Genie 4 world model promising more realistic physics and humans · Ars Technica AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 27, 2026 16:54 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Google DeepMind's Genie 3 world model, which generates interactive environments from text prompts, rattled gaming stocks last year. Genie 4 is the successor, released Tuesday as a research preview.

Why it matters

World models are being pitched as a way to train robots, prototype films and build games without hand-made environments, which is why game studios, carmakers and chip companies are all paying attention. For now, the technology's memory still runs out after about two minutes.

Google DeepMind released Genie 4 on Tuesday, according to TechCrunch, a world model that generates interactive virtual environments from text prompts. It is available as a research preview to trusted testers. Translation: a world model is software that invents a playable scene frame by frame as you move through it, with no pre-built levels. The previous version lasted about one minute before things fell apart, and Genie 4 lasts about two, which in the video game industry is known as a speedrun.

The Verge lists four main upgrades: more realistic worlds, real-time changes by typed prompt, generated audio, and Gemini 3.8 Flash working under the hood. TechCrunch adds that the audio includes music, ambient noise and interactive dialogue. Users can change the weather or camera angle, or add people and objects, mid-simulation. Ars Technica notes this works somewhat like reasoning models that write themselves extra prompts to stay on target.

The Gemini link is the structural change. Ars Technica reports that Gemini breaks the user's goal into steps and evaluates each output frame, while Genie draws. TechCrunch describes Gemini as the director. Google's Tejas Kulkarni said the future holds hybrid world models, with Gemini writing code and text while Genie simulates reality at different levels of abstraction. Translation: one AI writes the plan, another paints the scene, and a third meeting is presumably scheduled.

Google's pitch covers pre-visualization for filmmakers, game prototyping, interactive learning and robot training. TechCrunch described an experiment tied to Gemini Robotics in which a Gemini model split tasks into steps that Genie 4 simulated live, a way to test robot scenarios before they happen in the real world. Trusted testers, DeepMind engineer Diego Rivas said, find it most valuable for ideation and storyboarding. He also said it is still early and the end-to-end product is not yet clear.

Then there are the caveats, which Google supplied itself. Details and object permanence can drift as a simulation runs, and Ars Technica says worlds forget how things started after a couple of minutes. The team has not figured out how to guarantee accurate audio or legible in-world text. A tool pitched for training robots in reality has not yet mastered keeping a sign readable.

The commercial context is crowded. TechCrunch notes that Runway released its second world model, GWM Worlds 2, a couple of months ago. Meanwhile the industry is consolidating: World Labs is being acquired by AMD, Amazon is in talks to buy Decart, and Rivian acquired Third Dimension for self-driving simulation.

Access is tiered. Genie 4 is coming soon to Google AI Ultra subscribers in Project Genie, and a waitlist is open. Early access partners of the Gemini API and Google AI Studio are also in line. The Verge's reporter, who has not tried it, previously used Genie 3 to generate what he called bad Nintendo knockoffs, so the benchmark for improvement is clear.

What to watch is whether the two-minute limit stretches. A world that lasts a full film scene would be a product. One that lasts a commercial break is a very expensive demo.
