Boeing Beats Quarter, Misses Future, and Investors Choose to Sell the Future

Boeing's stock fell 10% after it pushed its $10 billion free cash flow target to 2029 and said 737 Max output will not hit 52 a month until late 2027.

The wire said: The big reason Boeing’s stock is falling so hard after earnings · MarketWatch

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 28, 2026 02:42 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Boeing has spent years trying to recover from production and quality problems, and CEO Kelly Ortberg has been steadying the 737 Max line. On Tuesday the company reported third-quarter results and, for the first time, gave investors free cash flow targets for the next three years.

Why it matters

Boeing's cash flow depends on delivering planes, and delivery pace affects airline fleet plans, aircraft availability and the stock held in many retirement portfolios. A slower ramp means a slower recovery, and the company is telling investors so directly.

Boeing shares fell 10% on Tuesday after the company reported third-quarter results and then described what the next three years of cash will look like. According to CNBC, the stock hit a fresh 52-week low and was on pace for its worst day since the April 2025 tariff panic. The quarter itself was fine. The forecast was the problem, which is a common arrangement in markets, where the past is a receipt and the future is the invoice.

The quarter, briefly: revenue rose 4% to $24.19 billion, ahead of the $23.84 billion analysts expected, per LSEG. The adjusted loss was 34 cents a share, worse than the expected 21 cents. Free cash flow was $464 million, up from $238 million a year earlier and above the $330 million FactSet consensus. Translation: free cash flow is the cash left after a company pays to run and build things, and with Boeing not yet reliably profitable, it is the number investors watch most.

Then came the outlook. Boeing expects $3 billion of free cash flow in 2027 against a consensus of $5.7 billion, and $6 billion in 2028 against $9.6 billion. It still reaches its $10 billion annual target in 2029, but CNBC's analysis notes that is a year later than Wall Street had modeled, and the 2029 consensus was $12.9 billion. Part of the 2027 shortfall comes from extra spending to support the Navy's next-generation F/A-XX fighter contract, which Boeing won in September. Winning a large contract means paying up front for the privilege.

The main constraint is wings. Ortberg said Boeing is producing about 47 737 Max jets a month and now aims for 52 in the second half of 2027. He said wing output is improving but must show sustained performance first. Boeing also now expects to fall short of its goal of delivering 500 737s in 2026. This matters because, as CNBC noted, most of an aircraft's price is paid when the customer receives it. A plane that is nearly finished is, financially, a very large decoration.

The commercial airplane unit shows the strain. Revenue fell 3% to $10.72 billion, with an operating margin of negative 6.5%, which Boeing attributed to delivery mix, costs tied to future production rate changes and higher supply chain costs. Boeing delivered 161 commercial airplanes, one more than a year earlier. The defense unit did the heavy lifting, with revenue up 15% to $7.94 billion.

There was good news, delivered at a lower volume. Boeing reported a record $745 billion backlog, and the FAA certified the 737 Max 10 last week after the Max 7 in August, completing the family. Ortberg said customer demand has not changed despite the Middle East conflict pushing up jet fuel prices, and argued that costly fuel makes efficient new jets more attractive. Brent crude was most recently listed at $115.22, so airlines have a strong incentive to retire thirsty old planes, if they can get new ones.

CNBC's analysts called the report a clearing event and kept their buy-equivalent rating, but cut their price target to $225. Their logic is that bad news is now priced in and Ortberg is deliberately under-promising. That is a reasonable view, and also the view of every investor who has ever bought a falling stock.

Ortberg told CNBC that everyone wants Boeing to go faster but that it will do it right. After the company's recent history, that is the correct answer. It also happens to be the answer that costs $2.7 billion less in 2027 than investors hoped.
