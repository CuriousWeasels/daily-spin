Justice Department Finds Thousands of Students Who Attend Class Flawlessly and Do Not Exist

Five people are charged in the first broad federal crackdown on "ghost students," with officials flagging 830,000 suspect aid accounts and more than $32 million in confirmed losses.

The wire said: Thousands of “ghost students” use AI to steal millions in student loans, DOJ says · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 31, 2026 02:52 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The Justice Department announced charges against five individuals accused of using stolen identities and AI to pose as college students and collect federal aid. The Department of Education says it has flagged more than 830,000 potentially fraudulent aid accounts.

Why it matters

People whose identities were stolen may learn of it only when collectors demand repayment on loans for colleges they never attended. The new fraud checks also risk slowing aid for real students, particularly low-income ones without a driver's license.

The US Department of Justice has charged five individuals in what it calls the first broad federal crackdown on "ghost students," according to Ars Technica. Ghost students are fake enrollees built from stolen identities, whose main academic achievement is receiving financial aid. By the standards of modern higher education, this is a student who pays attention, never complains and is never on campus.

The scheme, as officials described it at a press conference, begins with buying personal information of Americans, most often high school graduates who went straight to work and never attended college. Translation: the ideal victim is someone with a clean record and no reason to check whether they are enrolled somewhere. Fraudsters then file the Free Application for Federal Student Aid, or FAFSA, and apply to universities and community colleges.

If accepted, aid goes first to the school for tuition and fees. Whatever is left over lands in bank accounts controlled by the fraudsters, the DOJ said, some of them opened by Americans acting as money mules. Translation: a money mule is a person who lends their bank account to a crime and is later surprised to be involved in it.

Artificial intelligence keeps the enterprise running. Officials said it is used to fill out applications, complete coursework and even create the appearance of a student attending virtual sessions. "It looks like the ghost student is actually completing the class," said Assistant Attorney General Colin McDonald. The goal, he said, is to keep the ghost enrolled "semester after semester after semester," which is also the stated goal of many real students and nearly every registrar's office.

The numbers are large. The Education Department has identified more than 830,000 potentially fraudulent aid accounts, and the DOJ said that if all of them are ghosts, losses approach $130 million. Officials said they have identified more than $32 million in actual loss tied to these specific defendants and believe the true figure is substantially higher. In one case, a defendant allegedly used multiple accounts to receive over 700 fraudulent refund deposits totaling about $1,439,548. The DOJ also seized more than $750,000 in cryptocurrency from a network associated with Kenyan nationals, which McDonald called the first international seizure of its kind in Kenya.

The victims are the awkward part. Many will not discover the fraud until the government comes to collect years later, by which time their credit may be damaged. The DOJ said known victims are shocked by their faked attendance at an institution hundreds of miles away. McDonald conceded that unraveling it is "a mess that no one should have to endure," which is a candid description for a press conference.

Officials also found time for politics. Undersecretary of Education Nicholas Kent said the Biden administration had turned off nearly every fraud detection measure, while the current department has introduced online identity verification. FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson said of the automated approach: "The computers help us find it, and we keep the money in the agency." The DOJ suspects the scam has run since at least 2021, so credit for the discovery may be disputed for some time.

The fix has its own risks. Students must hold government ID up to a camera, and automated systems assign a risk rating, rejecting anyone flagged as high risk. Advocates warn that false flags burden schools and delay aid. MorraLee Keller of the National College Attainment Network told Inside Higher Ed that many low-income young people may not have a driver's license. In August, the department also confirmed a glitch that kept fraud results from reaching certain FAFSA forms for a week, after which it reprocessed them.

So the government has answered AI-generated fake students with AI-reviewed real ones, and is hoping the software can tell the difference. Ghost students never complain about being wrongly rejected. The real ones will.
