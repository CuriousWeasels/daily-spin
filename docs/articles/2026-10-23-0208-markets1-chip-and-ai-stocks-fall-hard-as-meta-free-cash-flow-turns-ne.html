Chip Stocks Slide as AI Boom Discovers It Needs Money From Other People

Meta's negative free cash flow, a record credit-risk reading and a shelved Solidigm IPO sent the Nasdaq to its worst session since June.

The wire said: 3 big reasons Micron and other chip stocks fell hard on Thursday · MarketWatch

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 23, 2026 02:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Big technology companies have committed enormous sums to AI data centers and chips, and investors have mostly cheered. On Thursday, a run of earnings and financing news made them ask who is actually paying for all of it.

Why it matters

If the AI build-out depends on outside financing, then borrowing costs and investor patience matter as much as demand for chatbots. That affects tech-heavy retirement accounts, index funds and anyone whose portfolio is quietly an AI bet.

The Nasdaq Composite fell 2.6% on Thursday, its worst session since June, according to CNBC. Nvidia dropped 4.4%, Advanced Micro Devices fell 8.1%, and MarketWatch said Micron and other chip stocks fell hard. Its three explanations were Tesla's lower spending target, a weaker forecast from Lam Research and a reminder that the AI boom relies on outside funding. The third reason is the interesting one, since it suggests the boom was less self-sustaining than advertised.

Meta supplied the clearest example. Its shares sank nearly 7% after third-quarter results showed free cash flow of negative $2.12 billion, CNBC reported. Translation: free cash flow is the money left after a company pays for its operations and its equipment. Negative means the company spent more than it earned, which is normally what a household calls a problem and a corporation calls an investment cycle.

Revenue was not the issue. Sales rose 26% from a year earlier to $64.60 billion, ahead of the $63.37 billion analysts expected. The trouble is spending: Meta now expects full-year capital expenditures of $135 billion to $145 billion, raising the bottom of its range from $130 billion. Translation: capex is money spent on physical things like servers and buildings. Meta moved its minimum spending up by $5 billion, the sort of adjustment most people make to a grocery budget.

Credit markets noticed. The spread on Meta's five-year credit default swaps hit an all-time high, CNBC said. Translation: a credit default swap is insurance against a borrower failing to pay, and the spread is the price. A record price does not mean Meta is about to default, but it does mean buyers of that insurance feel more nervous than they ever have before.

Meta also announced a new class of nonvoting Class C stock, which it said would preserve its founder-led structure and let it finance growth. In the same filing, it noted that Mark Zuckerberg could leave California if the state's proposed wealth tax passed, since he could face a multibillion-dollar bill on his stock. The filing added that the new stock plan convinced him to stay. Shareholders may take comfort that the governance arrangements and the tax planning were solved by the same document.

The financing theme reached South Korea on Friday. SK Hynix shares fell as much as 3% after it halted a review of a possible U.S. IPO for its Solidigm subsidiary, citing capital market conditions and share price trends. The decision also followed a warning from South Korea's financial regulator, reported by Reuters, that a U.S. listing could violate dual-listing rules. SK Hynix said Solidigm's investment needs are unchanged and that it will look for other ways to pay for a new U.S. fabrication plant.

Elsewhere, CoreWeave fell 10% on Thursday and is down 36% for the month, while Australia's NextDC tumbled nearly 40% on Wednesday after cutting its earnings forecast. Nvidia's Jensen Huang told CNBC he does not expect a memory chip oversupply by 2029. That is a reassuring forecast from the person who sells the most chips, and it is also a long way off.

The backdrop is not generous. The 10-year Treasury yield stands at 5.13% and the 2-year at 4.81%, so the money these companies want to borrow is not cheap. The AI build-out has always run on the belief that demand would outrun the bills. This week's lesson is that the bills arrive first.
