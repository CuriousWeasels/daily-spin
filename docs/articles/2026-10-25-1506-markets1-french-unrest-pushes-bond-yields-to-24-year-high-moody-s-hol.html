France Keeps Its Credit Rating, Loses Its Patience With Everything Else

Moody's held France at Aa3 with a negative outlook while 10-year yields hit their highest since 2002 and protesters planned more marches.

The wire said: What next for France as riots rattle markets? Three questions facing investors ahead of more protests · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 25, 2026 15:06 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

France has endured a week of unrest over living costs and public services, while its government struggles to pass a budget meant to shrink a deficit of roughly 5.4% to 5.5% of GDP. Bond yields have climbed to levels not seen in over two decades.

Why it matters

Higher French borrowing costs ripple through euro zone debt, bank lending and the cost of financing the government's own budget. Prolonged unrest could also weigh on tourism, retail and growth.

Moody's on Friday left France's credit rating at Aa3, which it still calls "high grade," and kept its negative outlook, CNBC reported. Translation: the grade is fine, but the teacher has written "concerning" in the margin and has not ruled out a phone call home.

The agency said the outlook reflects the risk that political fragmentation "will continue to impair" the work of the legislature. In plain terms, Moody's is less worried about whether France can afford its budget than about whether anyone can agree to vote for one. It said it could restore a stable outlook if institutions manage to narrow the deficit.

The market did its own grading. According to CNBC, the yield on 10-year French bonds, known as OATs, rose 11 basis points on Thursday to its highest since 2002, passing 5%. Translation: a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, and the yield is the interest rate investors demand to lend to the government. The gap over German Bunds, the euro zone's safe-bond benchmark, is the widest since November 2011.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. France's deficit is about 5.5% of GDP according to CNBC's Moody's report, against a 3% EU limit, and the finance ministry has warned that debt will reach 121.7% of GDP in 2027. More than $1 trillion of debt matures by 2030, which means France will be borrowing at today's prices for some time. The government aims to cut the deficit to 5% in 2027 from a projected 5.4% in 2026, and the National Assembly resumes debating the budget's tax measures on Oct. 27, with opposition lawmakers resisting.

The streets add another variable. The Interior Ministry counted 410,000 protesters on Saturday; unions said the real number was twice that. Last weekend, the other CNBC report cited more than 500,000 marchers. Clashes broke out in Paris and Marseille despite a large security operation. More demonstrations are planned for Oct. 24 and a union-backed day of strikes for Nov. 5.

Analysts quoted by CNBC see two ways this hurts. ING's Charlotte de Montpellier noted that past disruption was modest: the Yellow Vest movement may have shaved 0.1 percentage point off growth in late 2018, and the 2023 pension strikes about the same in the first quarter. But she warned that a lasting loss of confidence can matter more, and ING already expected growth of only 0.3% in 2026.

Companies are feeling it. Carrefour shares fell more than 14% Wednesday after it cut its profit outlook and investment, citing the unrest and rising energy costs. CEO Alexandre Bompard called it an "unprecedented situation." ING flagged retail, hospitality and transport as most exposed, while Carmignac's Kevin Thozet pointed to tax-free tourist shopping, around 20% of LVMH's French revenue. The CAC 40 is down 4.5% over the past month.

Natixis's Alain Durré said the government could use Article 49.3, which pushes a bill through without a final vote unless a no-confidence motion passes, or executive ordinances. Translation: the budget could be passed by procedure rather than persuasion, a method that calms bond investors and does little for the mood outside. Ebury's Matthew Ryan cautioned that any budget that survives may carry so many concessions that the deficit stays unaddressed.

Both S&P Global and Fitch cut France's rating in 2025, so Moody's is now the last of the big three to hold the line. Watch the Oct. 27 budget debate and the Nov. 5 strikes: one decides whether the deficit shrinks, the other whether anyone is willing to let it.
