Britain and Japan Discover Inflation Is Also Available in Their Currencies

UK inflation hit 3.5% and Japan's core rate reached 2.1%, and both central banks now appear ready to raise rates, though for different reasons and with different tax plans.

The wire said: UK inflation jumps to 3.5% — cementing rate hike expectations · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 23, 2026 02:08 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Energy prices have surged since the U.S.-Iran war disrupted oil shipments through the Strait of Hormuz. That shock is now showing up in consumer price data in Britain and Japan, two countries that import much of their energy.

Why it matters

If the Bank of England raises rates to 4% as markets expect, mortgages and business loans in Britain are likely to get more expensive. In Japan, higher inflation and a weak yen mean that imported fuel and goods cost more, even as the government cuts taxes.

UK consumer prices rose 3.5% in the 12 months to September, the Office for National Statistics said on Wednesday, above the 3.4% economists polled by Reuters had expected. CNBC reported that the figure follows 3.1% in August and 2.9% in July, and is the highest annual reading in almost a year. Motor fuel prices rose 29.8% over the year, a pace not seen since August 2022, so drivers are once again learning that a pump is a very effective way to read macroeconomic data.

Britain is a net energy importer. Translation: it buys more energy than it sells, so when the world price jumps, it pays the difference personally. The cause cited is the U.S.-Iran war and the struggle to move oil through the Strait of Hormuz, which is a long way from a Tesco forecourt but not from its price board.

The Bank of England is not expected to stop at the headline number. Scott Gardner of J.P. Morgan Personal Investing said services inflation has accelerated, which raises concern about "second round inflationary effects beyond energy prices." Translation: the worry is that higher fuel costs leak into wages, rents and everything else, and prices begin rising simply because everyone expects them to. Markets price a 98% chance of a 25 basis point hike to 4% next month, according to LSEG data. Translation: a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so this is a quarter-point increase, and traders consider it nearly settled.

Chancellor John Healey has to present his first Autumn Budget next week, and the numbers are not helping. Separate data showed national debt exceeded £3 trillion for the first time in September, while the cost of servicing it has risen sharply and growth is weak. Healey promised to maintain "fiscal discipline" while giving families and businesses "breathing space." Those two goals are in tension, which is why Suren Thiru of the Institute of Chartered Accountants in England and Wales called the Budget "a perilous high-wire act." Higher inflation adds pressure for cost-of-living support and also pushes up borrowing costs, shrinking what Treasury officials call headroom, which is the gap between the government's plans and its self-imposed limits.

Japan's version is milder in size but similar in discomfort. Core inflation, which excludes fresh food, reached 2.1% in September, up from 1.7% in August, its highest this year. The Bank of Japan's preferred gauge, which also strips out energy, came in at 2.4%, above the central bank's 2% target. Capital Economics' Abhijit Surya noted that this happened even as rice prices fell 20.6% and energy prices fell 0.6%, thanks to subsidies from the Takaichi administration.

The pressure comes from imports. A weak yen makes oil and other goods more expensive, and Japan is buying energy from costlier U.S. sources as it moves away from Middle Eastern suppliers. Surya sees another rate hike in December after September's quarter-point increase to 1.25%, a three-decade high. For a country that spent decades trying to produce inflation, getting it must feel like receiving a long-awaited package with the wrong contents.

Meanwhile, Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama told parliament that Japan was "on the cusp of shifting to a period of sustained inflation driven by rising wages." Asked whether a plan to cut the consumption tax on food from 8% to 1% for two years clashes with rate hikes, he said he saw "no gap" between the government and the central bank, Reuters reported. One side is making food cheaper while the other makes borrowing dearer to cool prices, and they have apparently agreed this is coordination.

Watch the Budget next week, then the Bank of England's November meeting. Both governments want to cushion consumers from higher prices, and both central banks are preparing to do the opposite.
