Ship Attacks Near Hormuz Hit Record as Saudi Arabia Learns Allies Come in Symbolic Sizes

Twelve vessels reported attacks in one week while Houthi strikes on Saudi targets test a kingdom that planned to be a tourist destination.

The wire said: Ship attack reports near Hormuz reach record high as oil exports slow · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 16, 2026 15:38 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The US-Israeli war with Iran began in February, and Iran and its allies have since targeted shipping and Gulf states. The US has been escorting tankers through the Strait of Hormuz, and the Houthis in Yemen are now striking Saudi Arabia.

Why it matters

Fewer tankers getting through Hormuz means tighter oil supply and higher risk for anyone who buys fuel or ships goods. Attacks on Saudi airports and oil sites also threaten the stability of the world's largest oil exporter.

Twelve ships reported being attacked in and around the Strait of Hormuz between 5 and 11 October, the most in a single week since the war began, according to BBC Verify analysis of data from the UK Maritime Trade Operations agency. The previous week had ten. Six of the attacks came on a single Sunday, which is a record for a day, and a reminder that records are not always something you want to set.

The BBC reported that experts see a pattern. The US military has been escorting more tankers through the southern part of the strait, and Iran has responded by attacking more ships. Translation: when one side builds a safer lane, the other side redefines where the lane is. Iran calls the route near Oman's coast unauthorised and wants ships to use one closer to its own shore, which happens to be the one where it can reach them more easily.

The escorts have been working, up to a point. US Navy data cited by the BBC shows assisted transits in the first ten days of October were up 85% compared with the same period in August. Most Gulf oil now moves by a shuttle system: smaller tankers carry cargo through the strait and hand it to larger tankers in the Gulf of Oman. Translation: a relay race in which the baton is crude oil and the track is under fire.

The numbers show the effect. Kpler data put flows at about 12.6 million barrels per day on 13 October, down from 15 million at the end of September and well below the pre-war level of roughly 17 million. Iranian exports have stopped entirely because of the US blockade of Iranian ports and ships. US Central Command said commercial traffic continues to flow with coordinated protection, which is true in the way a river still flows when someone is throwing things into it.

The human cost is real and is not a punchline. The tanker Petro Dolphin was hit by a drone near Oman, more than 250km from the strait, and while eleven Indian seafarers were rescued, two remain missing, according to the Forward Seamen's Union of India. The container ship GFS Pride sank on Tuesday after being struck by an unknown projectile, and all 21 crew were evacuated. Iran's Revolutionary Guard said the ship carried contraband and took an unauthorised route. Orbital Risk Intelligence's Martin Kelly said Iran's aim is to stop ships and deter others, and the attacks now reach as far as 500km from the strait.

Meanwhile, DW reported that Saudi Arabia faces its own problem. The Houthis hit Aramco's headquarters in Dhahran on Tuesday, and attacks at Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport last weekend killed more than a dozen people and injured more than 300. Airlines suspended flights, the airport was expected to stay closed through at least 17 October, and the Future Investment Initiative summit will offer remote participation. Translation: a conference designed to attract investors to the kingdom is now being held for investors who would like to stay elsewhere.

This collides with Vision 2030, the Saudi plan to reduce dependence on oil by becoming a hub for tourism, aviation and artificial intelligence. Analyst Timothy Kaldas told DW that diversification depends on visitors seeing the kingdom as safe. That is hard to sell while a cruise missile is intercepted over the Medina region, though DW notes it is unclear who launched it.

Riyadh's allies offer a mixed package. Turkey and Pakistan signed the Mecca Joint Defense Agreement with Saudi Arabia in August and have offered intelligence and defense support, but will not send troops to Yemen. France and the UK offered limited help, and Egypt offered words. The US has offered intelligence sharing and targeting assistance but not direct strikes, apparently to avoid opening another front. Kaldas said Riyadh is prioritizing the symbolic value of these partners, which is a diplomatic way of saying the alliance is mostly for display.

What to watch: whether the shuttle tankers keep using the Omani route, and whether the Houthi campaign keeps hitting airports. The oil market will price both, and ordinary buyers will eventually pay the invoice.
