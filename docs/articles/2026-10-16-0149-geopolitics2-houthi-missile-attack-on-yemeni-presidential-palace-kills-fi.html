Saudi Arabia Calls Houthi Attacks War Crimes as Yemen's Ceasefire Finishes Unraveling

A missile strike on Aden's presidential palace killed five, the US warns of threats to Saudi airports, and a four-year informal truce is now mostly a memory.

The wire said: Iran war live: Saudi blasts Houthi ‘war crimes’; US warns on Saudi airports · Al Jazeera

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 16, 2026 01:49 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Yemen's civil war began in 2014 and drew in a Saudi-led coalition in 2015. An informal ceasefire held for about four years before beginning to fray in July, when the Houthis announced a "maritime embargo" against Saudi Arabia.

Why it matters

The Houthis now hold part of the coast beside the Bab al-Mandab Strait, a chokepoint for global shipping, and BBC reporting links their advance to higher oil prices. Civilians in Yemen bear the heaviest cost, with cholera cases surging and 201,000 more people displaced, according to the UN.

Five people were killed and 16 injured when five ballistic missiles hit al-Maashiq Palace in Aden, the seat of Yemen's internationally recognised government, the BBC reported, citing officials. Information Minister Moammar al-Eryani said the prime minister and other ministers were unhurt. Health Minister Qasim Buhaibeh said the casualties included guards and civilian employees.

Houthi military spokesman Yahya Sarea described the target as "the Saudi enemy's military operations headquarters in Aden." Translation: the Houthis say a government palace is a military site, and the government says it is a palace. Both descriptions cannot be correct, though only one comes with a casualty list. Prime Minister Shaya Zindani called the attack "cowardly" and vowed that operations against the Houthis would continue.

Al Jazeera reports that Saudi Arabia has called Houthi attacks "full-fledged war crimes," accusing the group of endangering Muslim holy sites and pilgrims. The charge follows a Saudi-led coalition claim that it intercepted a cruise missile in the Medina region, home to one of Islam's holiest shrines. The Houthis denied attacking Saudi Arabia on Thursday, so the missile is currently an object with a trajectory and no sender.

Meanwhile, the US embassy in Saudi Arabia has warned of potential attacks on Saudi airports and restricted staff travel, according to Al Jazeera. This is not abstract caution. On Saturday, a Houthi missile strike killed 13 people at Riyadh's international airport, which the BBC described as the deadliest attack in Saudi Arabia in more than a decade.

Riyadh's deputy foreign minister, Rayed Krimly, said Iran continues to arm and train the Houthis in violation of UN Security Council resolutions. Iran has previously denied supplying weapons and says it supports the group only politically. Its foreign minister has also offered "any assistance necessary" to start talks between Saudi Arabia and the Houthis. Translation: Iran denies lighting the fire and offers to help negotiate its end, which is a coherent position only if nobody checks the shipping manifests.

The mechanics matter beyond Yemen. In September the Houthis seized part of the south-western Red Sea coast near Bab al-Mandab, and the BBC says their advance contributed to a rise in oil prices after six months of war between the US, Israel and Iran. Last week, pro-government forces said they retook territory overlooking the strait with Saudi air support. A chokepoint, in other words, is a place where a small piece of land gives someone a large vote on the price of fuel.

The human toll is the least negotiable figure here. Yemen's health ministry says at least 1,282 people have been killed and 5,890 injured since 6 August. The UN says another 201,000 people have been displaced and warns of an alarming surge in suspected cholera cases. Watch whether the offered talks acquire a venue, a date and a Houthi delegation. So far they have only acquired offers.
