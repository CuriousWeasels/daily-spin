Fed Expected to Do Nothing Loudly, While Declining to Say What It Will Do Next

Chairman Kevin Warsh is expected to hold rates this week after September's hike, with forecasters split on whether he should be tightening at all.

The wire said: Fed expected to hold rates steady, but Warsh may point way to more hikes · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 27, 2026 16:54 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The Fed raised rates by a quarter point in September as inflation climbed, with CPI at 3.7% and energy prices surging. Its new chairman, Kevin Warsh, has said he dislikes forward guidance, the habit of telling markets what the Fed plans next.

Why it matters

Higher rates mean mortgages above 7% and costlier car and consumer loans, while the Fed's own silence leaves borrowers and investors guessing. A mistake in either direction would land hardest on housing and household budgets.

The Federal Reserve is widely expected to leave interest rates unchanged when it announces its decision Wednesday, CNBC reported. Futures markets put the chance of a hike this week at about 10%, and 93% of respondents in CNBC's Fed Survey of 30 fund managers, strategists and economists also expect a pause. The central bank will thus hold a closely watched meeting in order to do nothing, which is a lot of attention for a rounding error.

The harder part is what Warsh says, or declines to say. He has pushed the Fed away from forward guidance. Translation: forward guidance means the Fed tells markets where rates are going, and Warsh would prefer that markets figure it out from the data, like everyone else with a mortgage. Deutsche Bank's Brett Ryan expects him to signal that more tightening will likely be needed over time, while skipping October buys more time to assess the data.

The data are not helping him relax. Annual CPI was 3.7% in September, driven largely by energy prices. Core CPI, which strips out gas and groceries, was 2.6%, up 0.3 percentage point from August and still above the Fed's 2% target. Translation: even the version of inflation designed to ignore the scary parts is going the wrong way.

Meanwhile the economy is sending mixed notes. Mortgage rates above 7% are squeezing housing, and CNBC described a low-fire, low-hire labor market with layoffs near their lowest since the late 1960s. Unemployment is 4.2%. Futures see better than 80% odds of a December hike, and Evercore ISI's Krishna Guha reads the Fed's baseline as roughly one hike per quarter, with the right to change its mind.

Economists disagree on whether any of this is wise. Janus Henderson's Richard Bernstein called the Fed's hesitancy to raise rates unfathomable. Moody's Mark Zandi says inflation is high but not entrenched and this is not the time for more hikes. Allianz's Mohamed El-Erian expects hikes but calls them the wrong tool, arguing for fiscal containment instead. He expects the pain to land on housing, car loans and consumer loans rather than on tech.

The survey adds a list of other worries: the war in Iran, an AI bubble, and the congressional election next week. Half of respondents blame government debt over AI borrowing for high bond yields, and the 10-year Treasury yield stands at 5.0%. Respondents expect two hikes next year on average, and a 26% chance of recession.

The likeliest outcome Wednesday is a hold, a carefully worded statement and a chairman explaining at length why he will not explain. Watch for dissents: survey respondents expect at least two voters to object to keeping rates unchanged.
