Anthropic Books $16.8 Billion Quarter, Investors Ask Why Not More

Revenue jumped about 45 percent in Q3 and the company is profitable on an adjusted basis, but slowing growth is putting pressure on a November IPO valuation.

The wire said: Anthropic's Q3 revenue hits $16.8B but growth slows ahead of planned November IPO · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 16, 2026 01:49 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Anthropic, the maker of the Claude AI models, is planning a stock market debut in November, before Thanksgiving, according to The Information. It privately valued itself at $965 billion in May, and bankers had floated an IPO valuation as high as $2 trillion.

Why it matters

Anthropic's IPO is the biggest test yet of whether public investors will pay AI-boom prices for AI-boom revenue. If the valuation is cut, it may signal how much the market believes in the rest of the sector.

Anthropic told potential investors at a private meeting in San Francisco that its third-quarter revenue was $16.8 billion, according to multiple US media reports cited by The Decoder. That is roughly 45 percent above the second quarter and more than 3.6 times the company's entire 2025 revenue. By most standards this would be called a triumph. In IPO season it is called a disappointment, because the previous quarter was better.

In the second quarter, demand for the coding tool Claude Code reportedly pushed revenue up more than 140 percent over the first quarter. Growth of 45 percent is therefore a slowdown, in the way that a car going 65 miles an hour is a slowdown from 140. The Decoder suggests some of it is seasonal: people work less in summer and use fewer tokens. Translation: tokens are the chunks of text AI models read and write, and they are how the meter runs. A second factor was competition. OpenAI launched a strong rival called Astra in September, and Anthropic only countered it in late September with Opus 5.5.

There was good news in the margins. The Information reported an adjusted operating margin of 11 percent for the year through Q3, and the Financial Times said it was the second straight quarter of adjusted profitability. Translation: adjusted means the company left out some large costs, such as stock-based compensation, which is paying employees in shares and then agreeing not to mention it. The company also earned $1.70 in revenue for every dollar it spent on compute, and is cash-flow positive. The Decoder notes this suggests trained models can make money despite months of price wars, though training costs, staff costs and cheaper open-weight rivals remain caveats.

Then there is distillation. Anthropic estimates 5 to 7 percent of its revenue comes from it, meaning other companies use Claude's outputs to train their own models. Anthropic has repeatedly accused Chinese labs of copying its models this way. It has now deployed effective countermeasures, which The Decoder notes raises the question of why it lobbied so long against the practice if it could simply block it. Anthropic told investors that distillation revenue will likely fall and weigh on the current quarter, which is the cost of protecting your product from customers who were also copying it.

The investors who heard all this were reportedly not thrilled. Several expect Anthropic's banking advisers to lower valuation expectations to possibly under $1.5 trillion, partly because the company did not discuss its September annualized revenue rate or a full-year outlook. Translation: annualized rate means taking one month and multiplying it by twelve, which flatters a company right up until it declines to share the month. In July, that rate was reportedly $65 billion.

The timing does not help. According to The Decoder, inflation remains high, borrowing costs are rising, public sentiment on AI is more negative than positive, and unsolved cybersecurity risks loom. OpenAI, Google and Anthropic have also been in a price war for months while open models close the performance gap.

The Decoder's own conclusion is that people will almost certainly use more AI, not less. The open question is whether the market will pay close to $2 trillion for that, or merely one and a half.
