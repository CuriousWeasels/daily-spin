France's Borrowing Costs Hit 5%, a Number Last Seen in 2002

The 10-year yield briefly topped 5% as Paris tries to pass a budget through a parliament that cannot agree on lunch, let alone 54 billion euros in cuts.

The wire said: French 10-year bond yield hits 5% for first time since 2002 amid unrest · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 21, 2026 16:10 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

France has been running a wide deficit and piling up debt while its parliament stays divided and its governments keep falling over budget fights. Protests that began last month as a Parisian student movement turned into weekend rioting.

Why it matters

When a government's borrowing costs rise, that expense eventually shows up in taxes, public services and the rates charged on loans across the economy. France is a core euro-zone economy, so doubts about its finances matter well beyond Paris.

The yield on France's benchmark 10-year government bond briefly crossed 5% on Wednesday for the first time since 2002, CNBC reported. It was later around 4.9794%, about 6 basis points higher on the day. Translation: a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so the market moved a small amount in a direction that finance ministers dislike.

A bond yield is the annual return investors demand for lending to a government. When the yield rises, investors are asking for more money to hold the debt, usually because they think it is riskier. France is therefore being told, in the polite language of markets, that its customers would like a better deal.

The reasons are not obscure. France ended last year with a deficit of 5.1% of GDP, while the EU, which has placed the country under its excessive deficit procedure, recommends something closer to 3%. Translation: Brussels has opened a file on France for spending more than it should, and the file is getting thicker.

Prime Minister Sebastien Lecornu says he will target 54 billion euros ($60.6 billion) in spending cuts in the 2027 budget. The difficulty is that cuts must pass a divided parliament, and CNBC noted that investors doubt such a body can agree on them. Past budget fights have toppled governments, and Lecornu himself resigned less than a month into his tenure, only to be reappointed by President Emmanuel Macron days later. This is one way to demonstrate continuity.

The government has a workaround. Finance minister Roland Lescure told RTL that ministers have what he called "guardrails," including Article 49.3 of the constitution, which lets the Prime Minister force a bill through the National Assembly without a vote. Lecornu used emergency legislation to push this year's budget through in February. Translation: if parliament cannot agree, the budget will proceed anyway, which solves the vote problem and leaves the confidence problem for another day.

Lescure said he was convinced the budget would pass because, in his words, "we have no choice." That is a candid summary of the bond market's position as well.

The street adds complications. Protests that started as a student movement last month turned violent, and officials estimate weekend rioting and looting caused damages worth hundreds of millions of euros. Unrest is awkward timing for a government trying to persuade citizens to accept austerity.

Next spring's presidential election looms as another source of uncertainty. Frontrunner Marine Le Pen said earlier this month she could bring the deficit below 3% within 18 months of taking office, though critics question whether her proposed savings are credible. Bond traders, who have heard many deficit promises, will presumably want to see the arithmetic.

The 5% line is a round number rather than a cliff, and the yield slipped back under it. But it gives headline writers something to point at, and the budget talks now have a very visible scoreboard.
