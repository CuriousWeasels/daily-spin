Ukraine Learns Its Battlefield Internet Has a Landlord, and the Landlord Has Opinions

Kyiv says Washington is behind lost access to Starshield, the military version of Starlink, as reports of speed limits raise fresh worries about dependence on private satellites.

The wire said: Ukraine and the U.S. are at odds over Starshield. Here's what to know · NPR World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 19, 2026 01:58 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Since Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022, Ukraine's forces have relied heavily on SpaceX's Starlink satellite service for communications and drone operations. Recent disruptions have prompted Ukraine and the U.S. to trade accusations over who controls what.

Why it matters

A modern army that depends on a private company's network can have its operations changed by a software setting or a policy decision it does not control. The dispute shows why European governments are looking at alternatives, though DW notes those remain untested at scale.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has said he believes the U.S. government is behind his military's loss of normal access to Starshield, the satellite communications service used by Ukraine's armed forces, NPR reported. The dispute has opened a practical question for any country at war: who owns the network your army uses to fight?

Some vocabulary helps. Starlink is SpaceX's commercial satellite internet service. Starshield, according to DW, is the version designed for government and military use, built on the same technology. Translation: same dish in the sky, different customer, and apparently a different person holding the off switch. DW reports that Elon Musk recently said Starshield systems are controlled by the US government, while Starlink is a commercial service operated by SpaceX. That answer is clear, and it is also not a comfort to Kyiv.

The stakes are not abstract. The Center for Security Studies at ETH Zurich estimates that around 50,000 Starlink terminals were in use in Ukraine in 2025. They relay orders between command levels, carry drone video, and move images and GPS coordinates of Russian positions to artillery units. Starlink became central partly because the KA-SAT system Ukraine had favored was hit by a cyberattack hours before the invasion, according to a CSS analysis.

Then there is the speed limit. According to Ukrainian media reports cited by DW, SpaceX temporarily capped Starlink terminals in motion at about 160 kilometers per hour (100 mph). Some Ukrainian drones travel at up to 300 kilometers per hour (186 mph). Translation: a drone faster than the cap can lose its connection, stop sending live video, and stop taking real-time commands, leaving it to finish its mission flying blind. A policy that sounds like a highway rule becomes a combat problem.

The satellite link is also only half of a pair. DW notes that Ukraine gets reconnaissance data from Western partners on air attacks, missile launches, troop movements and anti-aircraft positions, and that reports of the US limiting access to that data are as worrying as any throttling. Britain and France can provide intelligence, but not to the extent the United States can. One side supplies the map and the other supplies the delivery route, and both currently have American fingerprints.

Alternatives exist. DW points to Eutelsat/OneWeb in Europe, but says its ability to match Starlink in wartime conditions has not been tested on a large scale. That is the polite way of saying the backup plan has never been asked to do the job.

Experts quoted by DW say reliance on private technology firms marks a fundamental change in warfare. Relying on one provider simplifies operations, as DW puts it, right up until the provider becomes a variable. Watch whether the restrictions stay temporary, and whether any government decides that critical military communications should come with a contract that survives a bad week.
