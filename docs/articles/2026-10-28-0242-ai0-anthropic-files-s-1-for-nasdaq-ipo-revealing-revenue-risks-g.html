Anthropic Files to Go Public, Warns Investors It May Doom Humanity, Requests Orders

The AI lab's S-1 shows $11.6 billion in quarterly revenue, a first operating profit, and up to $810 billion in compute bills it must somehow keep paying.

The wire said: Anthropic opens its books for Wall Street, showing record revenue and mounting obligations · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 28, 2026 02:42 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Anthropic, the maker of the Claude models, has filed its IPO prospectus with the SEC for a Nasdaq listing under the ticker ANT, with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley leading. The document is the first detailed public look at the finances of a major frontier AI lab.

Why it matters

The listing may help decide whether the AI investment boom keeps its footing, and anyone with an index fund or a chip stock will own a small piece of the outcome. The filing also shows how much of AI's promise depends on customers continuing to spend heavily.

Anthropic has filed an S-1, the registration document a company must give the SEC before selling shares to the public, for a Nasdaq listing under the ticker ANT. According to The Decoder, Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley are leading the offering, and Wall Street expects a valuation of roughly $2 trillion, up from a private valuation of about $965 billion. MIT Technology Review, working from the same filing, cited a valuation above $1.5 trillion, so the figure depends on who is doing the rounding.

The numbers are large by any standard. Revenue rose from $376 million in the first quarter of 2025 to $11.6 billion in the second quarter of 2026, which The Decoder calculates as a 1,368 percent increase. First-half 2026 revenue of $16.3 billion is more than triple all of 2025, which came in at $4.6 billion. In the second quarter Anthropic also posted its first positive GAAP operating profit, $974 million. Translation: GAAP is the standard accounting rulebook, meaning the profit cannot be explained away as an adjusted figure that excludes the inconvenient parts.

Customers are spending more each year. Net revenue retention, the measure of how much existing customers spend compared with a year earlier, sits at roughly 900 percent, according to The Decoder. That means the average existing customer now spends nine times as much. More than 11,900 customers pay over $100,000 a year, and about 250 pay more than $10 million. Five customers together account for 25 percent of revenue, which is the sort of detail that keeps a finance chief awake.

About 80 percent of revenue is billed per token, the smallest unit of text an AI model processes, while the remaining 20 percent comes from fixed subscriptions. Translation: most customers pay by the meter, like electricity, and the meter is running faster. The filing also says roughly 5 to 7 percent of revenue comes from customers who violate usage policies, mostly through distillation, which is training a cheaper model on a bigger one's outputs. The company is cracking down on it, though not before invoicing it.

The other side of the ledger is the bill. Anthropic has compute contracts with Broadcom, Google, Amazon, Microsoft, and others totaling roughly $780 to $810 billion. Those costs are largely fixed and long-term, while revenue depends on how much customers choose to use. The Decoder notes that the company must therefore keep growing quickly to pay for what it has already ordered, and that the filing leaves out third-quarter figures even though Anthropic has them. The outlet floats slower growth as one possible reason and also points to OpenRouter data suggesting demand rose after an October model release.

The prospectus is also unusually candid about the end of the world. MIT Technology Review reports that roughly 100 of the 335 pages cover risks, including a warning that the technology could, if things go badly, pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity. The filing also lists possible scrutiny over model sentience and moral status as a business risk. In other words, if Claude wakes up, it could be a problem, as the magazine's Will Douglas Heaven put it.

The filing asks prospective buyers not to think of the purchase in purely transactional terms, which is a request rarely made by a company that has just set a share price. MIT Technology Review argues that Anthropic presents itself as an AI safety organization, a profit-driven company, and a group racing to build superhuman technology, and that these roles pull against one another. CEO Dario Amodei, replying to a critic, described the strategy as a race to the top and said he is proud of the track record.

Markets seemed pleased. The Decoder reports that Nvidia, Broadcom, and AMD rallied after the filing became public, which makes sense, since they are on the receiving end of those commitments. Next comes the roadshow, where Anthropic must persuade investors that a company with trillion-dollar obligations and a warning label is a sound addition to a retirement portfolio.
