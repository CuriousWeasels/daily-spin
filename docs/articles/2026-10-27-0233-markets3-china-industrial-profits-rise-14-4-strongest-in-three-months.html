China's Factories Report 14.4% Profit Jump, Mostly From Selling Shovels to the AI Gold Rush

Industrial profits grew at their fastest pace since June, but the same data shows Chinese households are mostly not invited to the party.

The wire said: China industrial profits post strongest growth in three months, rising 14.4% · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 27, 2026 02:33 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

China's National Bureau of Statistics released September industrial profit figures on Tuesday. They show earnings recovering after three straight years of declines, driven by AI-related equipment, even as consumer demand remains weak.

Why it matters

The data shows that China's growth is leaning heavily on global AI spending and exports rather than its own shoppers, which leaves it exposed if that spending slows. For everyone else, it suggests the AI buildout is feeding real factory profits well beyond the chip companies themselves.

Profits at large Chinese industrial firms rose 14.4% in September from a year earlier, the strongest pace since June, according to data released Tuesday by the National Bureau of Statistics, as reported by CNBC. For the first nine months of the year, profits are up 15.5%. This is a considerable recovery for a sector that, until last year, had spent three consecutive years shrinking.

The explanation is that the world is building AI data centers, and someone has to make the parts. Chief Statistician Yu Weining pointed to electronics and advanced manufacturing, where makers of electronic special materials saw profits more than triple, and semiconductor equipment makers saw a 51.7% jump. Translation: when everyone is panning for gold, the shovel factory has a very good quarter.

The shovels go further down the aisle than one might expect. Profits at optical fiber manufacturers rose more than sixfold over the nine months, and cable makers more than doubled, according to Yu. Copper and aluminum producers gained nearly 45%, helped by demand from AI and renewable industries. CNBC also notes that higher commodity prices stemming from the war in Iran have lifted earnings at miners and raw-material suppliers.

That last point comes with a catch. Downstream manufacturers, the ones who buy those materials and turn them into things, are facing rising input costs. Their customers, meanwhile, are not exactly queuing up. Earnings growth in AI and robotics is outpacing clothing, autos and furniture, which is the economic equivalent of the cafeteria having excellent steak and no one at the salad bar being paid.

The weak link is the Chinese household. Retail sales grew just 1.1% in September, which beat forecasts but was only marginally above August's weak reading. Shena Yue, senior economist at Oxford Economics, said the property downturn continues to weigh on housing purchases and related durables, and that "China's export strength isn't able to fully offset its domestic weakness." Translation: selling to the world is working, and selling to the neighbors is not.

The broader numbers are respectable on paper. The economy grew 4.5% in the third quarter from a year earlier, slightly faster than the previous quarter, bringing nine-month growth to 4.6%, within the government's full-year target of 4.5% to 5%. Profit growth also owes something to Beijing's efforts to curb price wars, which is the policy of asking companies to stop selling things at a loss in the hope that they will stop.

As for help, CNBC reports that Beijing has so far held back from major new stimulus for domestic demand, citing concerns over local government debt and the fading effect of consumer trade-in subsidies. So the plan, for now, is to let the AI boom carry the load. What to watch is whether global data center spending keeps its pace, since the factories are currently depending on customers abroad who, like all enthusiasts, may eventually run out of room for more cable.
