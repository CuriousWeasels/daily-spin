AI Buildout Discovers Money Must Come From Somewhere, Neoclouds and Meta Wobble

CoreWeave's default insurance costs past 11%, Meta burns cash and SK Hynix shelves a Solidigm IPO, as Nvidia's role as the industry's lender faces its first real test.

The wire said: Turmoil hits AI buildout as neoclouds face pressure, credit risks mount and Solidigm pulls IPO · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 25, 2026 01:53 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

For two years, the biggest tech companies have spent record sums on AI data centers, increasingly with borrowed money. This week, investors began asking who gets repaid if the demand forecasts miss.

Why it matters

The AI boom has been a main driver of stock indexes and retirement accounts, and it is now leaning on credit markets. If borrowing gets more expensive, the buildout slows, and the market's favorite story slows with it.

The Nasdaq fell again on Friday and ended the week down 0.7%, according to CNBC, as the AI infrastructure trade had what CNBC called a rocky week. The S&P 500 slipped 0.5% and the Dow rose 0.4%, which means the defensive sectors had a good week by owning toothpaste-adjacent companies. Kimberly-Clark rose 4.3%, because nothing says confidence like buying paper towels.

The pressure was sharpest at the neoclouds, smaller companies that rent out AI computing power. Translation: they buy expensive Nvidia chips with borrowed money and hope customers pay more in rent than the lenders charge in interest. CoreWeave, Nebius and IREN are each down well over 30% in October, CNBC reported. CoreWeave's credit default swap spread, the price of insuring its debt against default, passed 11%, a level generally read as distress, and yields on its 2031 and 2032 notes topped 15%.

Meta supplied the large-company version. It reported negative free cash flow of more than $2 billion for the quarter, raised the floor of its full-year capital spending to $135 billion from $130 billion, and said it has about $825 billion in leases and contracts ahead, over $450 billion non-cancellable. Translation: a take-or-pay agreement means you pay whether or not you needed the thing. A filing also said it may need to raise hundreds of billions of dollars over several years starting as early as fall 2026, and the stock dropped 7% on Thursday.

The filing also described a plan for a new non-voting Class C share, which would help raise money without diluting Mark Zuckerberg's voting control. Investors are invited to fund the future of the company while holding the same amount of say over it as the people who buy tickets to the lobby. Meta's five-year default swaps hit a record above 124 basis points (a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point), and Nvidia and Oracle also set records, at 126 and 343.

Which brings us to Nvidia, which has become the industry's lender as well as its chip supplier. According to CNBC, it had $36 billion in off-balance-sheet backstops for neoclouds as of its July filing, plus a guarantee of up to $105 billion tied to a proposed OpenAI data center in Ohio. Translation: if the tenants do not pay, Nvidia does, which makes the chip company a customer's customer's insurer. Nvidia generally pays nothing unless a project defaults, a condition that has the rare virtue of being easy to state and hard to rule out.

Jensen Huang remains upbeat. He told CNBC that demand for neoclouds is off the charts and that the demand for capital is much greater than the supply of it. This is a coherent argument, and also a description of a market in which people are short of money. David Trainer of New Constructs put the other side plainly: if demand for compute misses lofty expectations, there will be a problem.

Elsewhere the exits were busy. SK Hynix pulled the IPO of its Solidigm unit, citing market conditions. South Korea's Elice Group fell 30% on debut, and Australia's NextDC fell 39.5% after cutting its earnings forecast. Gartner predicts that 80% of neocloud companies will be shut down or absorbed by 2030, a forecast that would make the next few years a good time to own a moving company.

The worry is not that nobody wants AI. It is that the bill arrives before the revenue does. Next week brings earnings from Alphabet, Microsoft and Amazon, who will each be asked the same question in different accents: how are you paying for this?
