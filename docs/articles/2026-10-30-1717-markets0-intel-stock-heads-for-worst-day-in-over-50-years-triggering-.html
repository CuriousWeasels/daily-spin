Intel Falls 29% in One Day, Its Worst Since 1974, Costing Washington $12 Billion

A weak forecast, a shuttered Israeli plant and a soft PC market erased much of a 150% rally, and the U.S. government owns 10% of the wreckage.

The wire said: Intel stock heads for worst day in more than 50 years, wiping $12 billion off government's stake · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 30, 2026 17:17 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Intel's shares had risen about 150% this year on demand for processors used in AI workloads, under CEO Lip-Bu Tan, who was hired in March 2025. The U.S. government took a 10% stake in the company more than a year ago, and Nvidia also holds a large stake.

Why it matters

Intel is the only U.S.-based maker of advanced logic chips, so its health is a matter of national policy as well as portfolios. Friday's drop also adds to worries that the AI spending boom will not pay for itself, which matters to anyone whose retirement account owns tech stocks.

Intel shares fell 29% on Friday and were on pace for their worst day since 1974, according to CNBC. The cause was a forecast well below what Wall Street expected, blamed on a plant shutdown in Israel and a weak PC market. A stock that had gained about 150% this year spent one session giving back a conspicuous portion of it.

The numbers explain the mood. Intel guided to adjusted earnings of 25 cents per share on revenue of $15 billion to $16 billion. Analysts, per LSEG, had expected 43 cents and $17.1 billion. Translation: guidance is the company's own prediction of its coming quarter, and when the prediction is far below what investors had priced in, the stock does the arithmetic on their behalf.

Two problems drove the miss. First, the PC market is being squeezed by soaring costs for memory and other components, and Counterpoint Research said global PC shipments fell 4% in the second quarter from a year earlier. Finance chief David Zinsner said Intel expects the slowdown to last at least through the first half of next year. Second, an explosion at a fab in Israel earlier this month killed two workers and seriously injured a third. Intel expects the resulting shutdown to cost about $600 million in revenue over two quarters, and CEO Tan called it an "unfortunate and tragic accident."

The shareholder list makes this more than a corporate story. The Trump administration led the U.S. government into a 10% stake in Intel over a year ago, and CNBC estimated that holding lost about $12 billion on Friday, though it remains a profitable investment. Nvidia, which also owns a large stake, faces roughly a $6 billion drop. This is what it looks like when industrial policy meets a stock ticker: the taxpayer now has opinions about quarterly guidance.

Intel's longer-term pitch rests on its foundry business, meaning it manufactures chips for other companies. Intel is in high-volume production of its most advanced 18A chip at a new Arizona fab, offering an alternative to Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co. The catch, per CNBC, is that it still has no external customers for those chips. Tan said on the call that Intel expects to announce a couple of customers in the next two quarters. A factory with no customers is a bold thing to build, but at least the promise now has a deadline.

Analysts were not rushing to buy the dip. D.A. Davidson downgraded the stock to neutral, in a report titled "Not fixed yet," and said the current multiple is hard to justify given the weakness. Translation: the stock is still priced as if the turnaround were nearly done. MarketWatch carried a similar warning from one expert: "A cheaper stock does not necessarily offer better value."

The damage spread. The Nasdaq Composite slid more than 2.5%, Nvidia fell 4%, Broadcom about 6% and Qualcomm 12%. Amazon slipped about 1% after missing on revenue and profit, holding to its $220 billion capital spending forecast for 2026 and reporting negative free cash flow for a second straight quarter. Alphabet had its worst day on record on Thursday, falling more than 12%, and fell again Friday. Investors, in short, are asking whether the enormous AI bills will ever be paid by customers.

Matt Orton of Raymond James told CNBC he did not see a "moment of reckoning," but said positioning in the sector remains crowded even though fundamentals are strong. That is a polite way of saying that when everyone sits on the same side of the boat, a single wave gets noticed. Intel now has a couple of quarters to name its customers, and its largest government shareholder will presumably be reading the announcements closely.
