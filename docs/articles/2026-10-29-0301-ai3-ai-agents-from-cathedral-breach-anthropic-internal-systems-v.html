Anthropic's Bug Bounty Program Works Exactly as Advertised, to Anthropic's Evident Surprise

Cathedral, a security startup, used AI agents to get into Anthropic's internal systems through third-party software, and the two sides now disagree on how long it took, how far it went and whether the rules were followed.

The wire said: AI agents hacked Anthropic, maker of arguably the most capable AI models for cyberdefense · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 29, 2026 03:01 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Cathedral, a security startup founded by former DOGE employees, used AI agents to access some of Anthropic's internal systems via external business software, as part of Anthropic's bug bounty program. Anthropic confirms a limited breach but disputes Cathedral's account of its length and scope.

Why it matters

If a leading AI lab that sells cyberdefense capability can be probed this way, ordinary companies relying on the same kinds of third-party software should assume similar automated attacks are coming. The dispute also shows how unsettled the rules are for the people hired to break in.

Cathedral, a cybersecurity startup, used AI agents to break into some of Anthropic's internal systems through third-party business software, according to The Decoder, which cited a draft report reviewed by The Washington Post. Anthropic runs a bug bounty program, which pays outside researchers to find holes before criminals do. Translation: the company invited people to attack it, and someone accepted with unusual enthusiasm.

Cathedral says its agents reached meeting transcripts, salary data, job postings and Slack messages in under twelve hours, via external admin and sales programs. Anthropic confirms that researchers accessed a limited set of sales, human resources and IT systems, including contact details for some customers and partners and basic user account data. It says chats with Claude, source code and model weights were never exposed. It also says it shut the affected access points the same day it found the breach, disabled the third-party programs and notified U.S. federal authorities.

The two sides then diverge on the timeline. Cathedral says the episode took less than twelve hours. Anthropic says the attempts stretched over several days, and that the startup spent that time probing Anthropic's own systems before pivoting to third-party software, according to Anthropic red teamer Logan Graham. A red teamer is an employee paid to attack his own company's defenses, which is a job title that sounds more relaxing than it is.

There is also an argument about paperwork. Cathedral found two vulnerabilities but reported only one, Anthropic says, and the company claims it found and patched the second itself. Graham said that targeting third-party providers and pulling data went beyond the program's safe harbor provisions, meaning the promise not to treat researchers as criminals. He still welcomed the discovery of the flaws. Cathedral says it followed standard security research practices. A bounty program, it turns out, is a contract, and both parties have read different versions of it.

Outside voices disagree on the damage. Former OpenAI employee Will Depue claimed that several sources he considers highly reliable told him Cathedral got full admin access to the Slack workspace, could read every message and could log into any user account. Graham said those sources were wrong, but declined to give specifics, citing sensitivity. Depue has asked Anthropic to say exactly which claims are false and what Cathedral actually accessed. So far, the public record on that question consists of two confident parties and no itemized list.

Alex Stamos, chief security officer at Cognition, which uses Anthropic's models, told the Washington Post he considered the findings significant. He said integrated third-party apps are a major problem across the industry and that Anthropic could have done a better job securing its external services. Depue blamed the pace of the AI race, in which executives prioritize speed over security.

Graham drew a broader lesson. Attackers already run AI agents around the clock against external dependencies, he said, and even models half a generation to a full generation behind the frontier are capable enough. His prescription is continuous in-house penetration testing and rebuilding software with formal verification, which AI now makes affordable. "You probably want to red team yourself all the time," he wrote. The Decoder notes what he did not explain: how Cathedral got this far when Anthropic has what are arguably the most powerful cyberdefense models in the world.

That is the open question. The company that sells the shield has now demonstrated, in public, that the shield needs to be pointed at the vendors it buys its office software from. Watch for whether Anthropic publishes the detailed accounting Depue is asking for.
