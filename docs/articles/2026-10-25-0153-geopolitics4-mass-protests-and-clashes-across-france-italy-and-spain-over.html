France Permits Only Union-Approved Protests, Receives Large Quantity of Protests Anyway

Almost 1,000 were arrested as hundreds of thousands marched over living costs and schools, with 70,000 police deployed to avoid a repeat of last weekend's looting.

The wire said: Almost 1,000 arrested as clashes follow another day of mass protests across France · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 25, 2026 01:53 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Last weekend's French protests over fuel prices and the cost of living, which followed weeks of student demonstrations, turned into looting and arson and led to more than 2,500 arrests. This Saturday brought another round of marches, with a far larger security presence.

Why it matters

With presidential elections six months away, street unrest over living costs is becoming a campaign issue that the government cannot easily police away. Businesses that were hit last week are also watching to see whether the damage, put at hundreds of millions of euros, grows.

Nearly 1,000 people were arrested in France on Saturday after hundreds of thousands joined protests over the cost of living and poor school conditions, officials said. Most of the day passed peacefully, but police fired tear gas and water cannon in several cities after clashes late in the evening. The government counts this as a success, which tells you what last weekend was like.

The turnout was disputed in the traditional manner. The interior ministry estimated 410,000 marchers, while the unions said about 800,000. Translation: the side that organizes a march and the side that polices it each have a strong professional interest in different arithmetic, and both are reliably confident.

The authorities approached the day with considerable preparation. Some 70,000 police officers were deployed and restrictions were placed on several cities, after last weekend's disorder left officers outnumbered, hundreds of businesses looted and buildings torched. President Emmanuel Macron had also decreed that only demonstrations organized by recognized trade unions would be allowed. This is a bit like handling a crowd by announcing that it must be a different crowd.

The strategy worked in part. According to the BBC, there was no repeat of the rioting and looting on last week's scale. But in Paris, clashes broke out at the end of a march from Porte d'Italie toward Porte de Vincennes, where police included an elite unit that usually handles organized crime and counter-terrorism. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez said "several thousand radical activists" gathered at the rear of the march, including about 500 identified as rioters, AFP reported.

Elsewhere the pattern was familiar. Rubbish bins were set ablaze and streets blocked in Marseille, boards were pulled off a building in Bordeaux to make a barricade, and police in Lyon used tear gas on a crowd that had gathered in an area the local prefecture had declared off-limits. Nuñez said two people were seriously injured, one in Toulouse and one in Clermont-Ferrand, and that the arrest total would rise. Hundreds of people have already been jailed for vandalism, arson and looting after fast-track court hearings.

The protesters' stated grievances were less technical than the policing. A 26-year-old in Paris named Zelie told the BBC people were "really frustrated" and had been suffering since Macron took office. One masked teenager condemned the looting but tied it to poverty and police violence, and said protests would end when there is a response to their demands. That is a fairly clear negotiating position, and a harder one to meet with a ban on non-union marches.

France was not alone. According to the BBC, riot police fired tear gas at thousands of anti-government demonstrators in Tirana, clashes were reported in Brussels, and Milan deployed hundreds of soldiers alongside police to keep protesters from gathering at all. Al Jazeera reported that demonstrators across Europe were angry about living costs, cuts to public services and military spending. Governments have found that a continent-wide grievance cannot be solved by a single curfew.

Meanwhile, about 13,000 people marched peacefully in Orléans against the hard-right National Rally, a reminder that there is a political contest underway. Leading presidential contenders have blamed Macron and his ministers for the unrest. The next test is whether 70,000 officers can be kept on the streets every weekend until the election.
