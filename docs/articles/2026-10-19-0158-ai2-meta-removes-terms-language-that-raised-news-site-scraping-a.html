Meta Deletes Sentence Letting It Use Your Website, Says Sentence Meant Nothing

After experts read new commercial terms as a license to scrape publishers for AI training, Meta removed the language and said nothing about its crawling had changed.

The wire said: Meta removes language that raised scraping and AI training concerns · TechCrunch AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 19, 2026 01:58 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Meta updated its commercial terms to say it could use content from a business account's domains, web pages or URLs to develop and train its products. Legal experts and Nieman Lab read this as possible permission to scrape publishers for AI training, and Meta has now taken the language out.

Why it matters

Publishers rely on the robots.txt file to tell crawlers what to leave alone, and any contract language that might override it is a live concern for anyone whose content feeds AI systems. Meta says nothing changed, but the episode shows how much of the rules around AI training sit in fine print few people read.

Meta has removed a passage from its commercial terms of use that critics said could let it scrape websites to train artificial intelligence, according to The Verge and TechCrunch. The passage said Meta could use any content from a business account's domains, web pages or URLs to "provide, develop, improve, research, train, and maintain the integrity of" its products and services. The company says the passage was never meant to do what people thought it did, which is a strong argument for not having written it that way.

The alarm began with Nieman Lab, which spoke to legal experts who called it a "remarkable provision." The concern was simple: any publisher with a Meta business account, which is to say most of them, might have agreed to let Meta use its site content for AI training. James Grimmelmann, a professor of digital and information law at Cornell, was among those who read it that way, according to The Verge. They also worried it could supersede robots.txt.

Translation: robots.txt is a small text file on a website that tells automated crawlers which pages to stay away from. It is not legally binding, and works on the honor system, a system that AI companies have been testing with enthusiasm. A contract clause that overrode it would have turned a polite request into a signed concession.

Meta's first response was denial. Spokesperson Daniel Roberts and communications chief Andy Stone said there was no change to how Meta uses web crawlers, and that "any suggestion to the contrary is false." That position had a slight structural problem, which is that the suggestion was printed on Meta's own terms page.

Meta then deleted the language and issued a fuller statement. "We've heard you and want to clarify," the company said, adding that the crawling terms it consolidated applied only to its ads and business products, and that this is now spelled out in the terms. Roberts also said content owners can use robots.txt to signal their preferences, and that the updates do not give Meta new rights that supersede them. He said it was "not about scraping news sites."

The company is not exactly a stranger to this fight. TechCrunch notes that Meta won a high-profile copyright lawsuit last year, while other cases against AI companies, Meta included, continue. In that setting, every sentence about what an AI company may do with other people's content gets read by lawyers for approximately the length of a deposition.

The practical result is a tidy one. Meta says it did not change its behavior, did not gain new rights, and has now removed the words that suggested otherwise. Publishers can take comfort in this, and then check their robots.txt files, and possibly their contracts, and possibly the contracts again next month.
