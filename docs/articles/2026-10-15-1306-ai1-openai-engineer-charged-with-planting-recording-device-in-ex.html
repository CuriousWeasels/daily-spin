OpenAI Engineer Allegedly Bugs Boardroom With Toilet Paper and Chatbot Advice

Abdulrahman Alfozan, 35, faces up to five years in prison after the FBI said he hid a recorder in an executive conference room at OpenAI.

By The Daily Spin Desk · Oct 15, 2026 13:06 UTC · 3 min read
Catch upThe FBI and Justice Department say a Saudi engineer who joined OpenAI in July 2025 planted a hidden audio recorder in a restricted leadership meeting room. He was arrested on October 9 and fired the same day.
Why it mattersThe case shows that AI labs holding strategic secrets are now counterintelligence targets, and that their own products may leave a record of how a suspect built the tools. OpenAI says nothing sensitive like model weights, source code or customer data appears to have been taken.

OpenAI engineer Abdulrahman Alfozan was arrested on October 9 for allegedly hiding an audio recording device in a conference room where company leadership discusses research, product plans and business strategy, according to the Justice Department and FBI. He faces up to five years in prison and a $250,000 fine if convicted of violating laws against unlawful recordings. The company that builds some of the world's most advanced software was, in this instance, undone by a television.

The FBI alleges the device was wrapped in toilet paper and plugged into the back of the room's TV, likely on September 28. Translation: the plan was to hide a surveillance device in plain sight by making it look like something that had fallen behind a TV. Ars Technica reported that OpenAI found it on September 30, two days later, and determined its contents were encrypted. The Decoder reported that Alfozan, who had worked at the company since July 2025, did not have access to the room, and that a second recording device was found in his bag.

The room is described as restricted to those with special clearance, and it is where executives talk about technical progress, government engagements and commercial partnerships. Ars Technica reported the FBI alleges that chatbot logs and CCTV footage suggested he meant to spy on the company and risked leaking trade secrets in a way that threatened national security. That framing is notable: the bureau is treating a conference room as a national security asset, which says something about how the industry now sees itself.

OpenAI did not fire him right away. According to Ars Technica, it installed a camera in the room on October 5, and the footage allegedly shows Alfozan trying to retrieve the device that same day. The company then gradually cut his access to its systems while helping the FBI analyze the device, and timed his firing to coincide with the arrest. Translation: it kept the suspect employed just long enough to gather evidence, a practice normally associated with spy films and, apparently, human resources.

The most modern detail comes from the indictment, as relayed by The Decoder. A chatbot account linked to Alfozan's wife was allegedly used between September 24 and 30 to ask how to build a continuous audio recorder, adjust Wi-Fi connectivity, improve audio and live listening, and encrypt the SD card. The account shared several identifiers with his own, and some prompts referenced the recovered device's MAC address, a unique hardware ID. The Decoder noted that the filing does not specify which chatbot was used. It summarized the matter as the engineer apparently vibe-coding his spy gear, which is to say the evidence trail was assembled by the assistant.

OpenAI said there are no indications of the man inappropriately accessing or exfiltrating model weights, source code or customer data, according to The Decoder. FBI Director Kash Patel called protecting American superintelligence innovations one of the bureau's highest counterintelligence priorities. Both outlets note the suspect is a Saudi national, and The Decoder places the case against the backdrop of OpenAI's growing financial and infrastructure ties to Gulf states, including the UAE-based MGX and the Stargate UAE project announced in May 2025. Neither source says the Saudi government was involved, and the filings as described do not allege it.

What to watch is whether prosecutors identify who, if anyone, the recordings were meant for. For now, the known facts are a USB device, some toilet paper, and a search history. OpenAI has presumably added a line to its security training about checking behind the television.
