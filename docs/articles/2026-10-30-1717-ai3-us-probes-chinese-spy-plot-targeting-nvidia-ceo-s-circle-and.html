US Spied on Alleged Chinese Spy Dining With Nvidia's Chief, Told No One at Nvidia

The New York Times reports that US intelligence suspected a Megaspeed executive of passing information to Beijing, while Nvidia says she was just another customer.

The wire said: US investigated Chinese spy plot aimed at Nvidia CEO · TechCrunch AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 30, 2026 17:17 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The New York Times reported that US agencies spent about a year watching Alice Huang, a Singapore-based executive at Megaspeed, which the Commerce Department had already investigated over Nvidia chip resales to China. Nvidia denies that its CEO's contact with her was unusual.

Why it matters

The story shows how export controls on AI chips collide with the commercial interest in selling those chips, and who pays when the two conflict. It also lands as US and French regulators examine whether Nvidia itself has too much market power.

According to the New York Times, as relayed by TechCrunch and The Decoder, US intelligence agencies believe a Megaspeed executive named Alice Huang, also called Huang Le, passed information to China. She was reportedly photographed at several business dinners with Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang in the Singapore region. The two Huangs are not known to be related, which at least spares the investigators a family tree.

Megaspeed is a Singapore-based AI cloud and data center firm. Translation: it rents out computing power built on Nvidia chips, which are the most sought-after and most export-restricted commodity in the technology business. In 2025, the Times reported, the Commerce Department investigated whether Megaspeed helped Chinese companies get around export rules by reselling restricted chips to them. The Decoder adds that a Malaysian sister company bought at least $4.8 billion of AI hardware containing Nvidia chips between June 2024 and February 2026.

The concern, sources told the Times, was that Alice Huang had grown close enough to the CEO to learn Nvidia's plans, including his lobbying of the Trump administration to allow more advanced chips to ship to China. In other words, the worry was that the person tracking the lobbying effort might be reporting on it to the country being lobbied about.

The Decoder reports that President Trump was briefed about a year ago and did nothing, likely to avoid disrupting chip sales or straining trade relations with China. Jensen Huang was not warned, because officials believed he did not know. This is a coherent policy of sorts: watch the suspected spy carefully, and leave the person she is allegedly spying on to find out from the newspaper. Note that the Decoder attributes the likely motive to reporting, not to any official statement.

Nvidia rejects the framing. A spokesperson told TechCrunch that meetings with customers are part of a CEO's job and that Jensen Huang talks with thousands of customer executives every year. The company also said regional cloud customers receive ordinary business information, not trade secrets, and nothing relevant to national security. Megaspeed has announced an internal investigation, and a Chinese embassy spokesperson called the allegations "groundless smears and slanders."

Meanwhile, Nvidia has other visitors to worry about. The Decoder, citing Reuters, reports that the Justice Department is examining Nvidia's planned $12.93 billion purchase of Hugging Face, the open-source hub where AI models are shared. Officials are asking whether it would give Nvidia control over a key distribution point and hurt rivals. Nvidia expects the deal to close in the first half of 2027 and says Hugging Face will keep working with a range of chip, cloud, and AI providers.

That inquiry is part of a broader antitrust probe that began under Biden and has continued under Trump, which is one of the few subjects on which the two administrations have achieved continuity. Reuters sources say investigators have also looked at the SchedMD and Run:ai acquisitions and a licensing deal with Groq. They are checking whether Nvidia offered customers more chips if they also bought its networking gear and Groq processors. France's competition authority is separately examining Nvidia's CUDA software and its stakes in cloud providers such as CoreWeave.

So the picture is this: one government is accused of sitting on a briefing to protect chip sales, and several are asking whether the chip seller has become too central to the industry. Nvidia, for its part, says it has done nothing unusual. The thing to watch is whether Megaspeed's internal investigation finds anything, and whether anyone outside the company is allowed to read it.
