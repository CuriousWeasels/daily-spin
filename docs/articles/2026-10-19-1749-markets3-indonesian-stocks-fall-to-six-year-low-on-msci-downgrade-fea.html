Indonesian Stocks Hit Six-Year Low After Index Provider Floats Demotion to 'Stand-Alone' Status

The Jakarta Composite fell more than 5% Monday after MSCI proposed moving Indonesia out of the emerging-market category, a decision due by June.

The wire said: Indonesian stocks plunge to six-year low as MSCI downgrade fears mount · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 19, 2026 17:49 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

MSCI, a firm whose indexes guide trillions of dollars of passive investing, proposed on Friday reclassifying Indonesia from an emerging market directly to stand-alone status. Indonesian equities were already in a heavy sell-off, with foreign investors pulling money out all year.

Why it matters

If Indonesia is dropped from major benchmarks, funds that simply copy those indexes would be forced to sell, adding pressure to a market that has already lost nearly half its value this year. For investors and Indonesian savers alike, the outcome depends on whether regulators can convince a index provider that the market is transparent enough to trust.

Indonesia's benchmark stock index fell more than 5% on Monday to its lowest level since early October 2020, according to CNBC. The drop followed MSCI's Friday proposal to reclassify the country from an emerging market to stand-alone status. Stand-alone, in this context, is a polite phrase for a market that is no longer in the club.

Translation: MSCI builds the indexes that many large funds track. Passive funds do not form opinions about a country; they hold whatever the index holds. When the index removes a country, those funds must sell it, regardless of how they feel about its prospects, which is why a classification memo can move a market faster than a quarter of earnings.

Goldman Sachs put numbers on the risk in a Sunday note. A full removal from MSCI's emerging-market indexes could trigger about $5.6 billion in passive outflows, which the bank said equals 18 days of average daily trading volume for the relevant stocks. In plain terms, the market would be asked to absorb nearly three and a half weeks of normal trading in a single exercise, and the buyers are not obviously lined up.

There is a second scenario. If FTSE Russell, another index provider, took similar action, a further $2.7 billion could be at risk, though Goldman stressed that would be a separate event. Goldman also estimated that active funds benchmarked to MSCI and FTSE indexes hold more than $8 billion in Indonesian equities. Those managers would not be forced to sell, only free to, which investors will recognize as a different kind of reassurance.

The year has already been unkind. Goldman's data show the MSCI Indonesia Index down 47% as of Friday, with foreign investors withdrawing a net $5.1 billion. Foreign ownership slipped to 25% in October from 27% in January, while the value of foreign holdings roughly halved to about $130 billion from $250 billion. Note that most of that decline came from falling prices rather than departing investors, a distinction that is of limited comfort to anyone holding the prices.

MSCI is expected to decide by June, and the outcome reportedly hinges on whether Indonesian authorities can show meaningful improvements in market governance and transparency. That gives regulators roughly eight months to persuade an index provider that the market is easier to read than it currently appears. Transparency, it turns out, is a thing you can be graded on.

Goldman kept its underweight rating, meaning it recommends holding less Indonesian stock than the benchmark does, citing index uncertainty, weak corporate earnings and macroeconomic headwinds. The bank's strategists, led by Alvin So, wrote that valuations have compressed significantly but remain above historical troughs. The forward price-to-earnings ratio is 7.3 times, the lowest since the global financial crisis. Translation: stocks are cheap, but Goldman is declining to say cheap enough.

The bank also expects Indonesia's central bank to raise rates twice more, by 25 basis points each in the fourth quarter of 2026 and the first quarter of 2027. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so the increases are small, but higher borrowing costs tend to weigh on growth and on what investors will pay for shares. Goldman's conclusion is that the market may not have hit bottom, which is the sort of finding that is reliably true until the day it isn't.

What to watch: whether regulators announce governance reforms before June, and whether FTSE Russell follows MSCI. Until then, Indonesian equities remain a rare asset whose price depends heavily on an index provider's reading of the paperwork.
