Meta Updates Business Terms So Publishers' Websites Can Be Read, Learned From, and Not Invoiced

New commercial terms taking effect October 30 let Meta crawl any site linked to a business account, and critics say that includes train-on-it rights, while Meta says nothing is changing.

The wire said: Meta may use sweeping new commercial TOS to avoid millions in AI licensing fees · Ars Technica AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 17, 2026 01:44 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Meta is updating its commercial terms for business accounts across Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, and Messenger. Nieman Lab reported that the language could let Meta scrape linked websites and use the content to train AI, which Meta disputes.

Why it matters

News publishers are trying to get paid for AI training while also fighting in court over whether payment is required at all. Terms that grant broad access to a publisher's site as a condition of having a business account could undercut that leverage, whichever way the courts rule.

Meta will update its commercial terms on October 30, and according to Ars Technica and The Verge, the new language lets the company use automated software to crawl websites associated with any business account. The terms say Meta may use that content to "develop, improve, research," and "train" its products. Businesses that decline may "stop all access or use" of Meta's products for commercial purposes, which is a contract's way of saying the exit is over there.

Translation: a crawler is a program that reads web pages automatically, and robots.txt is a file where a website politely asks crawlers to stay out. It works like a sign on a lawn. Legal experts told Nieman Lab the new terms could let Meta argue its commercial contract outranks that sign, including any licensing agreement a publisher has negotiated. The sign remains, but the contract says the lawn is already part of the deal.

Notably, the passage never uses the words "artificial intelligence." Ars Technica says the AI reading is inferred from a single use of the word "train" and the references to scraping and crawling. This is a fairly relaxed standard of ambiguity for a company that has spent years building AI products and would presumably know the term.

Meta disputes the reading. Spokesperson Daniel Roberts said the company is "consolidating" its commercial and self-serve ad terms, and that "There is no change to how Meta uses web crawlers." He added that content owners can still use robots.txt flags to state their preferences. Andy Stone, Meta's vice president of communications, wrote on X that the update is meant to pull accurate information about products for sale on third-party sites for ad and content recommendations, not to scrape news sites.

The publishers are unconvinced, and the stakes are plain. Meta has followed OpenAI in signing AI licensing deals with CNN, Fox News, USA Today, and People Inc. Those deals exist because access to journalism has a price. Nieman Lab noted that virtually every news organization has at least one Meta business account, and Ars Technica says the terms offer no opt-out. James Grimmelmann, a Cornell professor of digital and information law, told Nieman Lab the terms give Meta permission to train on any content from publishers' sites and possibly use it in competition with them.

There is also a pricing detail. Last month Meta launched a Meta One subscription that requires businesses and creators to pay to include links in posts and reels. Nieman Lab summarized it this way: Meta has begun charging users to post links to the same content it will be permitted to crawl for free. As business models go, it is admirably complete: the customer pays to deliver the material, and the material then arrives pre-licensed.

Meanwhile, news organizations are arguing against OpenAI over whether training on articles without a license counts as fair use. Ars Technica observes that Meta's terms could grant broad permissions regardless of how that case turns out. If a court says licenses are required, a signed checkbox may serve as one. Watch for whether any publisher declines the terms, which would mean giving up a business account on the platform where its readers are.
