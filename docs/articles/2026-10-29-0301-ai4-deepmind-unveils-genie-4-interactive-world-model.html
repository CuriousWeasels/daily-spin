Google's Genie 4 Builds Whole Worlds on Demand, Then Forgets Them in Minutes

DeepMind's new world model adds sound, dialogue and Gemini supervision, but access is limited to select testers and consistency still fades in longer sessions.

The wire said: DeepMind unveils Genie 4 world model promising more realistic physics and humans · Ars Technica AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 29, 2026 03:01 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Google DeepMind introduced Genie 3 in August 2025, a world model that generates interactive environments frame by frame, and it rattled gaming stocks at the time. Genie 4 is the follow-up, now with audio, dialogue and Gemini models steering the action.

Why it matters

If world models work, they could supply cheap simulated training grounds for robots and AI agents, and a fast prototyping tool for creative industries. For now it is a research preview, and the game industry can keep its nerves for another day.

Google DeepMind has unveiled Genie 4, a world model that generates an interactive environment frame by frame in real time as a user moves through it with the keyboard and steers events with text prompts. According to The Decoder, it is the company's first real-time world model to generate sound directly. Ars Technica noted that Genie 3 sent gaming stocks tumbling last year, which is a lot of influence for a product most people could not use.

Translation: a world model is not a finished video. Nothing is pre-rendered and nobody programs the doors. You describe what you want, and the model improvises the next frame, then the next one, like a very expensive improv troupe that never breaks character, except when it does.

The headline additions are audio and talk. DeepMind says each world gets its own background sound, such as wind in the trees, and characters are meant to speak to each other and react to the user in real time. The company also says the biggest realism gain is in how people are rendered: they are supposed to move naturally, respond to their surroundings, and be directly controllable.

The less obvious change is supervision. Both outlets report that Gemini 3.8 Flash acts as a guiding agent, breaking a goal into steps and checking new frames to see whether each step worked. If the user changes the weather or steps in, it adjusts the plan. Translation: one AI dreams the world, and a second AI stands behind it with a clipboard asking whether that was what the user meant.

DeepMind also showed a robotics tie-in. According to The Decoder, a test version called Gemini 4 Argon split checkers into steps, and Genie 4 used those instructions to simulate Apptronik's Apollo humanoid robot playing the game, with no real robot involved. This is the pitch behind the whole exercise: infinite training environments where machines can practice without breaking anything expensive.

There are caveats, and DeepMind lists them itself. Ars Technica says worlds stay consistent for only a couple of minutes before the model forgets how things started. The Decoder adds that visual details can shift, objects do not always persist, and complex physics, accurate real-time sound and readable text are still under research. A simulated world that loses track of its own furniture is charming in a demo and less so as a robot's training ground.

Access is narrow. Only select testers can try Genie 4 for now, with Google AI Ultra subscribers due to get it soon in the Project Genie web app, along with partners who have early access to the Gemini API and Google AI Studio. Ars Technica put it plainly: if you pay Google enough, you might get to use it before too long. Google says all videos and audio will carry its SynthID watermark, which invisibly marks AI-generated content.

The kicker is the timeline. The world lasts a few minutes, the subscription tier is premium, and the gaming industry's alarm was triggered by the previous version. Watch whether the minutes get longer before the stock market's attention span does.
