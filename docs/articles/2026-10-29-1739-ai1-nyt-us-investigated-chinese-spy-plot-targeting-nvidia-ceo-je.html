US Spied on Executive Dining With Nvidia CEO, Then Left Him Out of the Loop

The New York Times reports US agencies suspected a Singapore cloud executive of passing information to Beijing, while Nvidia says she was just another customer.

The wire said: US investigated Chinese spy plot aimed at Nvidia CEO · TechCrunch AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 29, 2026 17:39 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Megaspeed, a Singapore AI cloud firm, was previously investigated by the Commerce Department over suspicions it helped Chinese companies get around US chip export rules. A new New York Times report says one of its executives was also under intelligence surveillance.

Why it matters

The story shows how hard it is to enforce chip export controls when the products are sold through layers of regional resellers. It also shows the tension between selling more chips and keeping them out of Chinese hands, which affects Nvidia investors and trade policy alike.

US intelligence agencies spent about a year watching an executive at a Singapore AI cloud company because they suspected she might be a Chinese spy, according to anonymous sources cited by the New York Times. The executive, Alice Huang (also known as Huang Le) of Megaspeed, had been photographed at several business dinners with Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang. The two share a surname and, apparently, a taste for regional networking.

The concern, per TechCrunch's summary of the Times report, was that she had grown close enough to the Nvidia chief to collect information about the company's plans. That included his efforts to persuade the Trump administration to let Nvidia ship more advanced technology to China. Translation: the worry was that someone who might be reporting to Beijing was sitting near the person lobbying Washington to sell Beijing more chips.

The Decoder, also citing the Times, goes further. It reports that US authorities concluded while monitoring her that she passes information to Beijing. It adds that President Trump was briefed roughly a year ago and did nothing, likely to avoid disrupting Nvidia's chip sales or straining trade relations with China. Jensen Huang was not warned, because officials believed he had no idea. The two outlets differ in tone: TechCrunch says she could have been a spy, while The Decoder presents the finding as established. Both are relaying anonymous sourcing.

Megaspeed was already known to American officials. The Times reported in 2025 that the Commerce Department had investigated whether the firm helped Chinese companies sidestep AI chip export rules by reselling restricted chips. According to The Decoder, a Malaysian sister company bought at least $4.8 billion worth of AI hardware containing Nvidia chips between June 2024 and February 2026. That is a lot of hardware for a business that, in Nvidia's telling, is an ordinary customer.

Nvidia rejects the suggestion that anything improper happened. A spokesperson told TechCrunch that meetings with customers are part of a CEO's job and that Jensen talks with thousands of customer executives every year. The company added that regional cloud customers receive ordinary business information, not trade secrets, and nothing relevant to national security. Megaspeed has announced an internal investigation, which is the corporate equivalent of asking the person in question to check the building for the person in question. The Chinese embassy called the allegations "groundless smears and slanders."

The mechanics matter more than the dinners. Export controls limit which chips can be sold to China, but cloud resellers in third countries can rent or resell computing power, which makes the final destination hard to verify. Nvidia profits from every sale along the chain, and the government wants both the revenue and the restrictions. Reportedly, one of those goals won the briefing.

What to watch next is whether Megaspeed's internal investigation produces anything public, and whether Washington acts on a year-old briefing now that it has become a newspaper story.
