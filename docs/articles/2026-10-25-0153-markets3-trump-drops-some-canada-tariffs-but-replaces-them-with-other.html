White House Removes Some Canadian Tariffs, Adds Others, Keeps Total Tariff Count Healthy

Trump signed a proclamation swapping exempted Canadian goods for valves, carpets and waste products, hours after Sen. Susan Collins claimed credit for the exemptions.

The wire said: Trump dropping some Canada tariffs but 'replacing' them with other products · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 25, 2026 01:53 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Trump imposed 50% tariffs on a list of Canadian goods over the summer under Section 338, a 96-year-old trade law. Maine, which imports more from Canada than from any other country, has felt the strain, and the tariffs are a top issue in its Senate race.

Why it matters

Tariff exemptions for some products do not lower the overall tariff burden if new products are added, so businesses and consumers may see little net relief. The changes take effect Oct. 30, four days before the Nov. 3 election.

The White House said Friday it would remove tariffs on some Canadian goods and replace them with tariffs on other Canadian goods. According to CNBC, Trump signed a proclamation taking certain products off the list of items hit with 50% duties, while adding what a White House fact sheet called "certain valves, carpets, waste products, and others." The tariff list has not shrunk so much as been rearranged.

The timing is where the story gets interesting. Sen. Susan Collins, R-Maine, announced that she had secured exemptions on nine categories of products she described as crucial to Maine's agricultural industries. The Wall Street Journal first reported her announcement. CNBC reported that the White House announced the new tariffs less than an hour later, and that her press release did not mention them.

Translation: a politician announced a win before the other half of the deal was published. Collins is fighting for reelection in a state where voters have already begun casting ballots and where the tariffs are unpopular. The exempted goods, according to Collins, include corrugated paper, plastic boxes and packing bags, refrigeration equipment, multiple forms of machinery, and seaweed and algae products.

The White House fact sheet says the changes "further refine the scope" of the tariffs. It does not mention Maine. A spokesman, Matthew Middleton, told CNBC the action responds to Canada's "continued discrimination" against U.S. commerce, with the aim of protecting American farmers, manufacturers and workers. Collins' spokeswoman, Phoebe Keller, told CNBC the senator requested exemptions important to Maine and will keep doing so. She did not say what Collins had agreed to in return, which was the question asked.

Collins' Democratic opponent, Troy Jackson, a fifth-generation Maine logger, said she enabled Trump's trade policies and now wants credit for "bringing a cup of water to put out the fire." At the Oct. 6 debate, Collins took credit for removing items such as road salt. Jackson replied: "We can't eat road salt." It is a fair point, though seaweed is at least edible.

The broader backdrop is not calm. By the latest official data, CPI inflation stands at 3.74% year over year, the 10-year Treasury yields 4.96%, and Brent crude is at $115.22 a barrel. Tariffs are taxes paid at the border by importers, who tend to pass the cost along. Swapping which goods are taxed changes who pays, not whether someone does.

The new list takes effect Oct. 30. Voters will go to the polls four days later, which gives them just enough time to check whether their carpets are on it.
