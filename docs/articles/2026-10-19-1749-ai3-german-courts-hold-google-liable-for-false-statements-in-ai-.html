German Courts Inform Google That Its AI Answers Are, Legally Speaking, Google's Answers

A second regional court has held Google responsible for false AI search text, after the first case ended in a quiet settlement before any higher court could rule.

The wire said: Two German courts now hold Google liable for what its AI overviews say · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 19, 2026 17:49 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

In late May, a Munich court ruled that Google is directly liable for what its AI Overviews say, after the feature falsely linked two publishers to fraud. Google announced an appeal, then settled, according to The Decoder.

Why it matters

If courts keep treating AI summaries as the company's own statements, search engines may have to check their answers before publishing them, or pay when they are wrong. Anyone falsely described by an AI search tool gains a faster route to getting it removed.

The Aachen Regional Court has barred Google from spreading false claims about a doctor through its AI Mode search feature, The Decoder reported. The decision is dated September 24, 2026, and relies explicitly on the earlier Munich ruling. It is a preliminary injunction, and Google can still challenge it.

Translation: a preliminary injunction is a court order issued quickly to stop harm before a full trial. The Aachen court issued it without first hearing Google, because the false claims could seriously damage the doctor's professional reputation.

The facts are uncomfortable. The doctor had been a chief physician and professor of neuroradiology from 2010 until late June 2026. On August 28, a colleague told him that Google's AI was explaining his departure by citing an alleged sexual assault against his secretary. Follow-up searches with terms such as "suspension," "termination," and "court proceedings" produced multi-paragraph accounts that even invented a legal aftermath.

None of it concerned him. According to the court, which checked its own records, the criminal case involved a different former university professor. The AI had, in effect, taken one man's story and issued it to another man as a biography.

The doctor emailed Google asking for removal. In mid-September the company said it would take no action. A formal legal demand sent the same day was acknowledged, but the false texts were still live, according to the court. This is the stage at which a company's reasonable-sounding process meets a judge.

The legal heart of the ruling is a classification. Google argued, in effect, that it merely helps people find what others have written. The court disagreed: "The respondent provides AI-generated search and is therefore also responsible for the answers generated in this context." Quoting the Munich decision, it said the AI produces "independent, new, and substantive statements" built from various third-party websites.

The judges also suggested the fix was not exotic. Google could compare AI output against the source websites, they said, and had it confronted its own AI with the doctor's objections, the system would have caught the errors itself. In other words, the court proposed that the machine be asked whether it was right. Violations carry fines of up to 250,000 euros each.

Then there is the Munich case, which is arguably the more strategic story. Google announced an appeal, but The Decoder learned it was never decided; the case ended in a settlement. Whether money changed hands is unknown, and neither Google nor the affected publisher would comment. The result is a landmark ruling that no higher court has ever confirmed, and no higher court can now overturn.

The Decoder argues this creates an odd incentive. Binding precedent arrives only when someone fights to the end, so a company that settles every time can keep the question open indefinitely. The outlet also warns that law firms could probe AI search until it produces a defamatory answer, then seek an injunction and a settlement. It says these systems are easy to manipulate.

The larger risk for Google is the principle. If AI answers count as Google's own content, publishers whose texts feed those answers might eventually assert their own claims. That was not part of the Munich case, but The Decoder calls it a logical next step. In the most extreme scenario, it writes, Google would owe the creators behind its sources for every generated answer.

For now, the score is two regional courts to zero for the theory that a company is responsible for what its product says. Google's next move is either to challenge the Aachen order and risk a precedent, or to find another settlement.
