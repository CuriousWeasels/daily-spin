US Approves 500 Patriot Missiles for Ukraine, Delivery Date Listed as 'Eventually'

The $2.1bn sale is paid for entirely by European allies and Canada, and Zelensky says the missiles could take several years to arrive.

The wire said: US approves sale of 500 Patriot missiles to Ukraine · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 31, 2026 02:52 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Russia has intensified aerial attacks on Ukraine's energy grid ahead of winter, and Ukraine says it is running critically low on Patriot interceptors. Relations with Washington were strained this month after a row over eased sanctions on Russia and Donald Trump's call for a new Ukrainian leader.

Why it matters

Patriots are the only weapon Ukraine says can reliably shoot down Russian ballistic missiles, so the gap between approval and delivery decides how much of the power grid stays on this winter. The deal also shows who now pays for American-made defence: not the American taxpayer.

The United States has approved the sale of 500 PAC-3 Patriot interceptor missiles to Ukraine, the BBC reported, at a cost of $2.1bn. The State Department said the purchase would be funded entirely by Kyiv's allies in Europe and Canada. It is a large order, which is a different thing from a delivered one.

Translation: approval means Washington has agreed that Ukraine may buy the missiles. It does not mean the missiles exist. According to the BBC, it is understood the order is for new builds rather than stock already on shelves, so nobody should expect a quick handover.

President Volodymyr Zelensky welcomed the announcement on X but said the deliveries could take "several years" to arrive. He said Ukraine needs the missiles "right now", and that Russia has carried out more than 30 strikes on critical parts of the Ukrainian power grid in the past two weeks. Winter, unlike the production schedule, keeps to its timetable.

The arithmetic is unhelpful. The BBC cites the US Department of Defence as saying only 600 Patriots are produced per year, so this single order is most of a year's output. There is also a global shortage, which the BBC attributes to heavy use of the interceptors in the US war against Iran. Everyone wants the same product from the same factory.

The financing is the tidiest part. The State Department said Ukraine will pay with donations from Canada, Germany and Norway through the JUMPSTART program, plus national funds financed by the European Union's Ukraine Support Loan. Lockheed Martin is the principal contractor, and the department stressed that "no US funds are involved in this sale". In effect, Europe and Canada supply the money, America supplies the paperwork and the factory, and Ukraine supplies the need.

The politics have been less orderly. Earlier this month the US eased sanctions on Russia as part of a deal to secure Russian diesel, and Trump said it was time for Ukraine to get a new leader. Tensions eased after Zelensky met US envoys in Oslo on 20 October. In July, Trump said Washington had "not agreed" to license Ukraine to produce Patriots, three weeks after offering such a right. Ukraine built a grid worth defending and was told it could build the defence, then that it could not.

Meanwhile, at the G20 foreign ministers' meeting in Atlanta, hosted by Secretary of State Marco Rubio, European diplomats refused to stand for the official photograph over the presence of Russian Foreign Minister Sergei Lavrov. The BBC reported the family photo was cancelled. UK Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband told Lavrov: "We all know who the aggressor in this war is." It was a protest conducted by declining to be photographed, a diplomatic tool whose range is limited to the lobby.

What to watch next is the production schedule, which the BBC says is unclear. Until it is published, the sale is best understood as a very expensive place in a very long queue.
