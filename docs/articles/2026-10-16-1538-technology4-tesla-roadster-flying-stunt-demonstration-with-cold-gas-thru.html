Tesla Roadster Flies Off Ramp, Remains Unavailable on Roads, Shelves, or Calendars

Tesla's rocket-assisted Roadster demo in Waco thrilled viewers, but pricing, range, and road legality were left for a future event, with production now pushed to 2028.

The wire said: The incredible flying Tesla Roadster is just for show · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 16, 2026 15:38 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Tesla first promised a new Roadster supercar roughly nine years ago, and early buyers put down $50,000 deposits. Last night in Waco, Texas, the company showed a version that launches off a ramp, flips in the air, and lands using compressed-gas thrusters.

Why it matters

Anyone who paid a deposit for a car that exists mainly as a presentation now has a clearer picture of the timeline, which is 2028 and a version that cannot legally use the road. For investors, it is a reminder that a stunt can fill social media feeds without adding a product to sell.

Tesla held a late-night demonstration in Waco, Texas, in which a Roadster drove up a multi-story ramp, launched off the edge, performed a mid-air kickflip over a row of Teslas, and landed with the help of cold-gas thrusters, according to The Verge. The car reportedly carries 600 liters of compressed air, 10 directional thruster nozzles, and a 2G suction-fan system inspired by SpaceX. It is, by all accounts, an impressive thing to watch, and the only thing it does not do is arrive in a customer's driveway.

The Verge reports that this "Rocket" configuration will be "invite only," removes the passenger seat to fit the high-pressure gas tanks, and will not be approved for public road use. Translation: it is a car in the sense that it has wheels, and a product in the sense that someone will presumably be invited to own it. Where the owner takes it remains, in the words of The Verge, a mystery.

Ars Technica describes the rear exhaust-style openings as the outlets for 100,000 psi jets of compressed nitrogen, and notes that its writer had not yet found the part of the Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Statutes explaining when such a thing is legal on a road. That is a reasonable research question for a vehicle whose main feature is making a very loud, very pressurized exit.

The commercial details were thinner than the nitrogen was dense. The Verge says Tesla skipped pricing and battery range specifications, that the range estimate was cut from 600 miles to 300 miles, and that Musk pushed production to 2028, more than a decade after early adopters handed over $50,000 deposits. Ars Technica adds that the car uses Tesla's 4680 cells, which the company has had trouble scaling, with a special chemistry developed for this model.

The performance claims have grown, as claims tend to do between presentations. Ars Technica says Musk now claims a planned top speed of 383 mph and a 0-60 mph time of 0.8 seconds. Both outlets note that existing cars already compete: The Verge cites BYD's Yangwang U9 Extreme at 308 mph in testing and the McMurtry Spéirling's 1.35-second 0-60 time, while Ars says the Spéirling manages up to 3.5 lateral Gs against Tesla's claimed 2 Gs, and will drive upside down.

Musk himself has called the Roadster a "small potato" project that will not move the needle financially next to the Model Y, The Verge reports. He also called it "the last best driver's car," reflecting his belief that autonomous driving is Tesla's future. Some analysts, per The Verge, see the re-release as groundwork for a rumored Tesla-SpaceX merger, which would likely dilute existing shareholders, including the fans on X who are cheering for it.

The Verge's verdict is that the demo is a successful PR event and a useless product, while rivals such as Rimac, Porsche, and BYD keep shipping real cars. Independent testing, a price, and a road-legal version would settle the question. Until then, the Roadster's most reliable feature is the deadline.
