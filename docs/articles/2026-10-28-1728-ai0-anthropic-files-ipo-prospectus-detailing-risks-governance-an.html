Anthropic Files 335-Page IPO Prospectus, Lists Possible End of Humanity Under 'Risks'

The AI company's S-1 pairs soaring revenue and a reported $1.5 trillion valuation with roughly 100 pages of warnings, including catastrophic and existential harm.

The wire said: Will the real Anthropic please stand up · MIT Technology Review

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 28, 2026 17:28 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Anthropic, maker of the Claude AI models, has filed its IPO prospectus with the Securities and Exchange Commission after weeks of speculation. The 335-page document includes a risk section that Ars Technica counted at 93 pages and more than 70,000 words.

Why it matters

The listing could determine the future of the AI investment boom, according to CNBC, and it asks ordinary investors to price in risks no standard stock offering has had to describe. It also tests whether a company can be both a safety organization and a profit machine once shareholders arrive.

Anthropic has filed its IPO prospectus, a 335-page document that describes the company's technology, its finances and the ways its products might go wrong. According to MIT Technology Review, about 100 pages concern risk. Ars Technica counted the Risk Factors section at 93 pages and over 70,000 words, which is the sort of length usually reserved for tax code and apology letters.

Translation: an S-1 is the registration form a company files with the SEC before selling shares to the public. Companies must disclose anything that could hurt the business, and lawyers tend to interpret 'anything' generously. Most firms list competition, customer churn and regulation. Anthropic also lists the possibility that its technology could, in its words, 'pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity.'

The filing is specific about the mechanism. Ars Technica reported that the worry is not any current model but future ones that could 'substantially automate AI research and development' and operate with a high degree of autonomy. In that case, Anthropic says, progress could outrun its ability to evaluate and safeguard new models. The company adds that this could also 'adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations,' which is true, in the way that a meteor strike is bad for foot traffic.

Sentience gets its own risk factor. Anthropic says that given how it approaches model sentience and moral status, it could face scrutiny and might adopt policies that go beyond legal requirements, constraining operations and raising costs. MIT Technology Review's summary: if Claude wakes up, it could be a problem. Ars Technica noted that most IPOs do not have to address whether their products 'possess or could develop forms of sentience.' The filing also flags model behavior 'resembling blackmail,' and warns that even the perception of such behavior could bring regulatory scrutiny and reputational harm whether or not anyone is actually hurt.

Some of the risks are less cinematic and more familiar to anyone who has read a liability clause. Anthropic notes that customers could sue over actions taken by AI agents even where the customer configured or supervised the agent. It also warns that another company's AI causing harm at scale could produce a public backlash and sweeping regulation that hit Anthropic too. And it worries about public perception that AI causes job losses, which could lead authorities to slow adoption whatever the actual employment effects.

The tone is the part critics noticed. The prospectus asks buyers not to think of the purchase 'in purely transactional terms' but as a chance to be part of something greater. MIT Technology Review's Will Douglas Heaven called this a master class in narrative capture, pointing to cofounder Jack Clark telling an IMF audience that the singularity had begun, and to cofounder Chris Olah telling the Vatican that the company keeps finding things 'mysterious, even unsettling.' The same piece noted that an IPO valuing the company above $1.5 trillion would net its cofounders vast wealth.

Anthropic has a stated answer to the contradiction. CEO Dario Amodei wrote on X that the strategy has always been a 'race to the top,' meaning that showing responsible development is possible raises the bar for the industry, and that this works best if the company is at the frontier. Critics such as Nathan Calvin of Encode AI asked what Anthropic actually is, after the company's secretly circulated draft bill leaked. The Download also summarized coverage that the filing shows soaring revenues alongside staggering losses, safety failures and a month-long training pause, and that the founders will keep voting control after the IPO. Sources differ in emphasis, but none suggests the risk section was short.

So prospective investors are being offered a stake in a company that says it may be building something dangerous, that it is the right party to build it, and that being the right party requires being first. That is a coherent pitch. It is also the first time a prospectus has asked shareholders to hold both the upside and the apocalypse in the same brokerage account. Watch the pricing: the market will decide how many pages of warning a trillion-dollar valuation can absorb.
