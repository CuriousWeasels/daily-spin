UN Panel Concludes That Bombing Heating Plants in Winter Is, Legally Speaking, a War Crime

A UN commission says Russia's attacks on Ukraine's energy grid since 2022 were war crimes, as another cold season arrives and the strikes continue.

The wire said: Russian attacks on Ukraine’s energy infrastructure a war crime: UN · Al Jazeera

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 26, 2026 17:43 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Russia has attacked Ukraine's power and heating systems every winter since its full-scale invasion in February 2022. A UN-backed investigative body has now put its conclusions about those attacks in a formal report.

Why it matters

The finding adds a formal legal judgment to a pattern that leaves millions without heat and water in freezing weather. A report cannot restore power, but it does build a record that could matter in later prosecutions.

The Independent International Commission of Inquiry on Ukraine told the UN General Assembly on Monday that Russia's attacks on Ukrainian energy infrastructure constitute war crimes, according to Al Jazeera. The commission said the strikes weaponise winter cold, which is a notable use of weather as a tool of policy. Translation: nature supplies the freezing, and the bombing of the heating plants supplies the rest.

The report covers attacks on heating plants, power lines and gas facilities since autumn 2022. It says the waves have grown in frequency, intensity and geographic range, with hundreds of drones and missiles launched at a time. The commission singled out the winter of 2025-2026, when temperatures fell to minus 21 degrees Celsius (minus 5.8 Fahrenheit) and millions of people faced long outages of heating, electricity, water and gas.

The legal finding is narrower than the headline suggests, and more pointed. The commission concluded that Russia committed the war crime of intentionally directing attacks against civilian objects. It assessed that the commanders ordering the strikes knew the heating facilities were indispensable to civilians. It also noted that Russian authorities had made official declarations about further attacks and referred to a plan to carry them out. Translation: the commission did not have to guess at intent, because officials said some of it out loud.

Commission chair Erik Mose said that as the cold season approaches, the attacks have intensified again, raising what he called serious concerns about the threats to civilians' lives and health. The fighting shows no sign of pausing. A Russian strike on Zaporizhzhia injured four people overnight, according to regional governor Mykhailo Semikin, and Moscow's defence ministry accused Ukraine of repeatedly striking the Russian-controlled town of Oleshky ahead of a planned local ceasefire this week.

Oleshky features in the other story of the day. NPR reported that Russia says it will open a humanitarian corridor to the occupied town, where people have been eating weeds and pickles to survive while trapped by minefields, shelling and drones. The corridor is welcome news if it opens. It is also a reminder that the party announcing humanitarian access is a party to the fighting.

The commission also accused Moscow of human trafficking in connection with recruiting foreign fighters, in violation of international human rights law. It said most of those recruited have been killed or gone missing, with others injured or captured. Al Jazeera noted numerous reports of men from India being duped into fighting on the front lines.

What happens next is mostly procedural. The commission was created by the UN Human Rights Council and can document and conclude, but it cannot arrest anyone or switch on a boiler. For now, the report is a thorough account of what the winter looks like, delivered just as the next one begins.
