Governments Switch Off Websites to Protect Them From Flaw in Language Running Most of Them

Two critical PHP vulnerabilities, found by AI after a decade unnoticed, led agencies from Finland to the NSA to advise taking sites offline until patched.

The wire said: Websites taken down worldwide as critical cyber-security update issued · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 23, 2026 15:58 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Two critical flaws were found in PHP, the server-side programming language behind WordPress, Wikipedia and many online shops, and a fix was issued on Wednesday. Details leaked on social media before the planned release date, so organisations had to respond at once.

Why it matters

If your council, garage or favourite TV programme website vanished this week, this is why. Attackers who exploit the flaws could steal data or plant backdoors, and experts say the full impact may take months to show.

Governments and companies around the world took websites offline this week after two critical vulnerabilities were disclosed in PHP, a programming language that runs behind the scenes of a large share of the web, according to the BBC. The PHP Foundation issued a fix on Wednesday afternoon. Cyber-security agencies then advised that anyone unable to apply it in time should consider switching their sites off. Translation: the official remedy for a website that might get hacked is to make it unavailable to everyone, which does solve the problem in the narrowest sense.

The affected list was broad. Finland shut down its government's public web services, the Dutch cyber-security agency removed its own site, and parts of Wikipedia went dark while the Wikimedia Foundation patched systems. The UK's Office for Budget Responsibility took its website down, and Edinburgh and Oxford council sites went offline before returning. Baroness Lloyd of Effra told the House of Lords that 32 government services were unavailable and four partially so, which she attributed to precautionary measures. The government said this did not mean the services had been hacked.

The flaw itself is the unnerving part. Wiz, a security firm, says the bugs let an unauthenticated attacker run code remotely on a server, which is the technical way of saying a stranger can give your computer orders. Professor Alan Woodward of the Surrey Centre for Cyber Security said an attacker could steal data, move through a network or plant a backdoor for later. The NSA noted that the flaws are in PHP itself, not in the hosted application or its configuration, so careful website owners get no credit for tidiness.

Scale explains the scramble. Wiz estimated that roughly 70 percent of the internet is affected, a figure the BBC said it could not verify. Woodward put it at some 30 million websites. Translation: nobody agrees on the exact size, but everyone agrees it is the kind of number that cancels your weekend. Sophos's Don Smith said the hard part is not installing the patch but finding every place PHP is running, since it can sit beneath hosting platforms and third-party products without the organisation knowing.

The bugs were found by AI. Researchers at Calif and Wiz, working through OpenAI's Patch the Planet programme, used advanced models to find flaws that had gone unnoticed for more than a decade. Wiz said the models made it practical to develop working exploits across multiple applications in hours. Defenders and attackers have access to the same sort of tools, and the difference is who uses them first. Amazon's security chief, CJ Moses, said AI means more actors can act within hours or even minutes of technical details becoming public.

The timing made things worse. The PHP Foundation had planned to publish its findings later this month along with a complete fix, but details leaked on social media on Tuesday and forced an early announcement. Amazon said it saw exploitation attempts in its decoy systems on 17 and 21 October, before the embargo lifted. Cloudflare's Matthew Prince wrote that attempts went from thousands in the 10 hours before the announcement to millions afterwards. Embargoes, it turns out, are only as strong as the least discreet person who knows about them.

So far, officials say, there are no confirmed successful attacks. The NCSC's Olly Whitehouse called the event unprecedented but reported no signs of successful breaches. Smaller costs are already showing. A garage owner in Chepstow told the BBC he could not print MOT paperwork and had gone back to writing it by hand. NCC Group's Matt Hull noted that security staff several days in may be burnt out and urged them to rest.

Watch the patch rate. Researchers say organisations are applying fixes, but not as quickly as hoped, and PHP versions 8.1 and older must upgrade rather than simply update. Until the long tail of forgotten servers is dealt with, the internet will rely on the same defence it always does, which is that most people eventually click update.
