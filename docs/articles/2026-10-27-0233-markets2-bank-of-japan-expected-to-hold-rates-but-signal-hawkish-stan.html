Bank of Japan Expected to Do Nothing Very Carefully on Friday

All 16 economists in a CNBC survey expect rates to hold at 1.25%, with the real suspense reserved for a single verb in the policy statement and a hike in December or January.

The wire said: Bank of Japan is expected to hold rates this week — but stay hawkish: CNBC survey · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 27, 2026 02:33 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The Bank of Japan raised its policy rate last month to its highest level in 31 years, after decades of near-zero or negative rates. It meets for two days this week, with a decision due Friday.

Why it matters

Japanese rates influence the yen, global bond yields and the cost of the cheap yen borrowing that investors have long used elsewhere. Another hike would add to the pressure on borrowers and bond markets at a time when U.S. yields are already high.

The Bank of Japan is expected to leave its policy rate at 1.25% when its two-day meeting ends on Friday, according to a CNBC survey. All 16 economists and analysts polled expect no change, which in central banking counts as a landslide. The bank raised rates last month, and it appears to feel that one hike per season is a respectable pace.

The more interesting question is what the bank will say, not what it will do. According to reports by Reuters and The Japan Times, cited by CNBC, the BOJ is expected to declare that its 2% underlying inflation target has been met. Ryutaro Kimura of BNP Paribas Asset Management said he is watching whether the statement changes from inflation "has been approaching 2 percent" to "has reached 2 percent." Translation: markets will spend Friday parsing the difference between "almost there" and "there," a distinction roughly one verb wide.

The economic backdrop explains the fuss. Core inflation, which excludes fresh food, rose 2.1% in September from a year earlier, its highest this year, as a weak yen raised import costs and energy prices fed through. Government subsidies are absorbing part of the energy bill for consumers, which means the official number is flattering the real pressure a little. Inflation-adjusted wages rose 1.5% in August, the eighth straight monthly gain. Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama reportedly told lawmakers Japan is "on the cusp of shifting to a period of sustained inflation driven by rising wages."

All respondents expect the bank to set the stage for a hike in December or January. Takuji Aida of Credit Agricole favors January, when the bank publishes its quarterly outlook report. Thirteen respondents see the terminal rate, meaning the level at which the tightening stops, at 1.75% to 2% by around mid-2027. Masato Koike of SOMPO Institute Plus expects the bank to stop at about 1.75% to avoid excessive downward pressure on growth.

Not everyone is convinced the board can keep going. Homin Lee of Lombard Odier said there may not be enough consensus for successive hikes given fragility in the domestic bond market. Sosuke Nakamura of Citi Research sees no clear sign inflation is accelerating enough to justify back-to-back increases. In short, the case for hiking is strong, and so is the case for being nervous about it.

The bond market supplies the nervousness. Japanese government bond yields fell last week on safe-haven demand during a sell-off in AI stocks, reversing earlier pressure from Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's spending plans and a global bond sell-off. Nine respondents expect the 10-year yield at 3% to 3.1% by year-end, up from 2.809%. Seven see the 30-year at 4% to 4.1%, from 3.829%. Translation: the government's borrowing costs are expected to keep climbing, which is awkward for a government that also wants to spend.

The yen is the other open question. Nine respondents expect it to trade between 157 and 158 per dollar over the next month, while three-month forecasts range from 150 to 160, a spread wide enough to suggest forecasting is optional. State Street's Masahiko Loo said BOJ normalization should gradually pull capital back toward Japan, but that elevated U.S. yields should prevent a straight-line yen rally. With the U.S. 10-year Treasury at 5.0% and the Fed funds rate at 3.88%, the dollar side of that trade is not exactly standing still.

The bank is therefore expected to do nothing on Friday, say something slightly different, and let markets do the rest of the work. The next real test is January, assuming the verb survives.
