France Protests Over Crumbling Schools and Prices; Town Hall Burns, Blame Is Distributed Evenly

About 550,000 people marched on Saturday, 1,200 were arrested and 150 police were hurt, while politicians used the day to rehearse April's election.

The wire said: France: Protesters clash with police in nationwide protests, amid rising social polarization · DW

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 18, 2026 01:40 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Weeks of student protests over teacher absences and run-down lycées widened on Saturday into a national day of action joined by unions and remnants of the Yellow Vests. The government has offered 3,000 extra supply teachers, but the protests have continued.

Why it matters

A protest about school budgets and the cost of living is turning into a contest over who owns the anger before the April presidential election. Residents of France may see more bans on marches, more arrests and fewer functioning town halls.

Protests across France on Saturday drew about 550,000 people, according to the Interior Ministry, and ended with clashes, looting and fires in multiple cities. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu said 150 police officers were injured and 1,200 people arrested. The ministry's first injury count was 60, which is a reminder that the first number in any crisis is a draft.

The demonstrations were nominally about high prices, social inequality and the state of schools, where pupils have complained of teacher absences and dilapidated facilities. DW and the BBC both reported that students were joined by trade unionists and veterans of the Yellow Vests movement. Translation: the people who were upset about fuel prices in 2018 are still upset, and have now been joined by their children's teenage cohort.

Most of the day was peaceful. NPR reported that most of the hundreds of protests passed without incident, and the BBC said the main Paris march was largely calm in the afternoon. DW's correspondent said many of the people he met were teachers, nurses, students and workers who wanted no part of the unrest but understood its causes, and who felt a government was not listening to them.

As the day went on, things got worse. Police used tear gas in Paris and Marseille, and the BBC reported water cannon too. AFP reported dozens of shops looted in cities including Paris, Marseille, Nice, Bordeaux and Lille, where an Apple store was emptied. The tourism office in Lyon was set alight, and the town hall in Limoges burned, with the roof partly collapsing. Protesters also smashed their way into Lille's city hall and left. The windows of the broadcasters CNews and Europe 1 were broken in Paris.

There was a human cost as well. Saint-Étienne's mayor said a young man had his hand blown off by a grenade, and called it a tragedy that should never have happened. The BBC also reported seeing riot police charge a crowd in central Paris and hit several people with batons, while DW's correspondent said police seemed in places to be trying to show restraint. The accounts differ, which is common when a day has several hundred separate sites.

The politics arrived faster than the fire brigade. The Interior Ministry blamed the "hard-left", and the government accused Jean-Luc Mélenchon's La France Insoumise of pouring fuel on public discontent, which the party denies. Mélenchon accused police of "unleashing" on protesters while condemning looting and arson, and Lecornu called his comments "dangerous and cynical". Marine Le Pen said protesters' "legitimate demands" had been "held hostage by the far left".

Notice the arrangement. Macron leaves office next year, and the BBC reports that polls point to an April runoff-style fight between Le Pen and Mélenchon. Lecornu said some groups were using the protests as a "warm-up" for the election. The same description fits the politicians commenting on them, who have all begun campaigning with considerable discipline.

Macron promised that those who attacked prefectures, businesses and town halls would be prosecuted and punished. The teachers, whose absence started all this, remain a separate item from the 3,000 extra supply teachers promised on 8 October. Watch whether the government's next offer is about classrooms or about crowd control.
