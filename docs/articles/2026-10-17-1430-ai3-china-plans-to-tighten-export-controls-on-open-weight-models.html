China Drafts Export Rules for AI Models, Exempting Anything Already Given Away

A draft list covers 23 AI technologies and sets thresholds for restricting model exports, while offering automatic approval to open-weight releases.

The wire said: China plans to tighten export controls on open-weight models and 23 key AI technologies · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 17, 2026 14:30 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

China's commerce and science ministries have released a draft update to the country's catalog of banned and restricted export technologies, adding AI to the list in detail. Until final rules take effect, companies must register affected exports through the commerce ministry's portal.

Why it matters

Companies and governments that rely on Chinese open-weight models, especially in Europe, may find that access to future models depends on Beijing's discretion. The rules also mirror existing US controls, so the best open models now come from two governments that both keep an export switch.

China's commerce and science ministries have published a draft rule tightening export controls on 23 key AI technologies, according to The Decoder. The list spans large models, agents, compute clusters, and data processing. The ministries appear to have concluded that AI is important enough to regulate and also important enough to keep selling.

The firmest line is drawn at autonomous malware. AI tools that can write or alter malware on their own to attack critical infrastructure, the military, or government agencies face a total export ban. Tools for finding software vulnerabilities escape a ban but will face new limits. Translation: you may sell the lock-testing kit, but with paperwork.

Large models can be caught in two ways. The first is training on China's "important data," as defined by its Data Security Law, while offering services based on it. Lawfully public data is exempt, and up to 0.1 percent is allowed if it was cleaned beforehand, never appears in outputs, and doesn't substantially boost the model's R&D capacity for core tech in that field. That is a very specific amount of permitted contamination.

The second trigger covers the core technology behind models: weights, checkpoints, source code, architecture, and training or inference methods. Controls apply once a model reaches 10^26 FLOPs of training compute, at least 2 trillion parameters, or a global top-10 ranking. Translation: FLOPs are a count of arithmetic operations, so 10^26 means a great deal of arithmetic. Parameters are the adjustable numbers inside a model, so 2 trillion means a great many of those. Top-10 status is judged from public rankings by major domestic and foreign evaluators, which makes a leaderboard position a regulatory event.

Beijing is also trying not to hurt its own labs. Publishing restricted technology and weights as open source qualifies for automatic licensing, meaning exports are cleared the moment the paperwork is filed. The same applies to Chinese firms running their own data centers abroad, taping out chips overseas, doing research with foreign partners, or helping set international standards. Anything already public through open-source releases or research papers is exempt entirely.

Access methods matter too. Using restricted models through APIs, apps, websites, SaaS tools, or lightweight versions on local devices stays outside the controls. Installing them on local servers abroad requires a standard export license, though companies can use the streamlined process if the software runs on their own overseas cloud.

Dean W. Ball, head of strategic futures at OpenAI, wrote that the move does not mean China is shutting down open-weight models. He said it shows the government creating the option to shut down future ones at will. He also called the framework a "mirror image" of US model-weight export controls. The Decoder expects US companies using open-weight models to lean further toward domestic ones.

Europe's position looks harder, according to the outlet. The EU has been counting on running open-weight models in its own clouds to stay independent. That plan weakens if the best open models come from the US or China and can be cut off by export controls. The sovereignty strategy is sound as long as someone else keeps shipping the product.

Beijing also pledges easier exports to the Global South for capacity building, tech sharing, and joint rulemaking within the World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organization, a bloc China launched to counter Western AI bodies. The draft is not final, so the main thing to watch is whether the thresholds survive and how often the word "optionality" turns out to mean something.
