DHS Learns Its Staff Directory Can Be Public After All, Just Not Through Official Channels

A leak dubbed Icepick exposes details of more than 41,000 Homeland Security employees, arriving as the department works to hide those same names.

The wire said: Massive leak exposes 41,000 DHS workers and undercover ICE agents · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 29, 2026 17:39 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Distributed Denial of Secrets published an archive of Department of Homeland Security personnel records on Wednesday, which it says came from a hacking group calling itself the Department of Peace. The department has spent the past year and a half restricting public identification of its employees.

Why it matters

The leak raises safety questions for the named employees and tests how far the government can go in shielding its workforce from public scrutiny. It also shows that a single login system, if the hackers' account is right, can hold a great deal of an agency's internal map.

More than 41,000 employees or contractors of the Department of Homeland Security have had their details published online, according to Ars Technica and the BBC. The archive, called Icepick, was released by the transparency nonprofit Distributed Denial of Secrets, or DDoSecrets. It contains names, email addresses, phone numbers and office addresses, plus references to internal systems. In short, DHS has finally produced a comprehensive staff directory, though not on its own initiative.

The records span DHS and seven component agencies, including Immigration and Customs Enforcement and Customs and Border Protection. The BBC reported, citing the data, that more than 21,000 of those listed work for CBP and nearly 6,600 for ICE. Others come from Homeland Security Investigations, the Federal Protective Service and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. Translation: the agency responsible for cybersecurity infrastructure appears in a leak about cybersecurity.

The material reportedly goes beyond contact details. The BBC said it includes dozens of previously unknown operation names, the agents who led them, and some technology used, including tools from Cellebrite and Palantir. Ars Technica described references to undercover and surveillance programs. DDoSecrets co-founder Emma Best said the aim was to replace "anonymity with accountability" and that the release was not meant as a directory of every agent.

The hackers say they got the data from DHS's Okta login system. Translation: Okta is a single sign-on service, the digital equivalent of one key that opens every door in the building, which is convenient until someone else has a copy. Okta told both outlets that it has no evidence its data or systems were compromised. That leaves two accounts, the hackers' and the vendor's, and the sources do not resolve them.

A DHS spokesperson said the agency is aware of a potential breach and is working to address it, adding that it takes threats against employees seriously. The Department of Peace pointed to a July 17 statement by Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin that he was "turning up the heat". The group wrote that this is what happens to ICE when you do that.

The timing is awkward for the department's own position on names. Ars Technica reports that since the start of the second Trump administration, DHS and the federal government have moved to hide employees' names and faces. DHS has also broadened its definition of doxing, normally the malicious publication of someone's home address and personal details, to cover basic facts about public employees like names and titles. By that definition, the department is now the subject of a very large doxing, which it did not have to wait long to learn about.

The BBC places this alongside a pattern of recent incidents. The FBI was hit last month by a group called ShinyHunters, which took personal information on thousands of agents, and the Federal Election Commission was taken offline over a suspected hacking attempt. Ars Technica adds that law enforcement has arrested several suspects tied to the FBI breach. The BBC also notes that ICE agents often wear masks and rarely identify themselves despite a legal requirement to do so, which gives the leak an interesting symmetry.

What to watch next: whether Okta and DHS reconcile their accounts, and whether the department's promised public update says how 41,000 records left the building. Until then, the department's most complete staff list belongs to someone else.
