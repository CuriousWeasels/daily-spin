Driver Says He Was Asleep While 'Full Self-Driving' Hit Patrol Cars

California Highway Patrol says two officers were seriously injured, and reminds the public that 'full' and 'self' are not binding terms.

The wire said: Tesla driver says he was asleep and using FSD when he crashed into highway patrol cars · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 17, 2026 01:44 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

A Tesla driver in California told authorities he was asleep and using the company's Full Self-Driving mode when his car hit parked highway patrol vehicles on I-80. Federal regulators have been scrutinizing Tesla's driver-assist software since 2021.

Why it matters

Anyone sharing a road with a car running driver-assist software is relying on the human behind the wheel actually being awake. The legal and practical burden still sits with the driver, whatever the product is called.

The California Highway Patrol said a Tesla driver who told officers he was asleep and using Full Self-Driving crashed into parked patrol vehicles on I-80, according to The Verge. The officers had been responding to an earlier collision. CHP said two of them were seriously injured and taken to a hospital.

Video released by CHP, as described by The Verge, shows the Tesla passing flares, cones and emergency lights before striking the back of a parked patrol car. Those are the items a roadway uses to say, in several different dialects, that something is wrong ahead. The warnings were apparently not read by anyone in the vehicle.

CHP responded with a statement that doubles as a product review: 'Despite its name, full self-driving is not fully autonomous.' It added that technology does not relieve drivers of the responsibility to operate their vehicles safely. Translation: the name is marketing, the liability is yours.

The crash fits a pattern regulators have already studied. After repeated collisions with parked emergency vehicles, the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA), the federal auto safety regulator, opened an investigation into Tesla's Autopilot in 2021, The Verge reported. Its eventual report found the driver-assist features did not do enough to keep drivers engaged, and linked them to hundreds of crashes and dozens of deaths in which both the system and the driver failed to respond.

Tesla issued an update in 2023 adding more controls and alerts to prompt drivers to take full control when Autosteer is on. NHTSA then opened a second investigation into whether those changes went far enough. Translation: the fix was a more insistent reminder to supervise a system sold on the premise that you need not.

The mechanism is a tidy one. A system that handles most driving well enough encourages attention to drift, and attention that drifts is exactly what the system depends on to catch its mistakes. Sleep is the logical endpoint of that incentive, though not one the owner's manual endorses.

The sources do not say whether the driver faces charges or what the investigation found about the system's state at impact. Those answers will matter, because the legal question is whether responsibility lies with the person who dozed off or the product that made dozing off feel reasonable. Until it is settled, the safest autonomous feature remains a conscious adult.
