Microsoft Invites 60,000 Employees to Leave, Stressing That Nobody Is Being Pushed

The company is offering voluntary exit packages to up to 60,000 staff in four countries, months after laying off 4,800 and after a separate retirement program.

The wire said: Microsoft offers buyouts to 60,000 employees · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 27, 2026 02:33 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Microsoft has about 223,000 employees and has been trimming headcount in stages. It laid off 4,800 people months ago, mostly in Xbox and commercial sales, and earlier this year offered a voluntary retirement program to about 9,000 long-serving staff.

Why it matters

Buyouts let a company shrink without the headlines and morale damage of layoffs, but they also let the employees most able to find other jobs decide to leave. For workers at big tech firms, an invitation to depart is increasingly a sign of how the company sees its future headcount.

Microsoft is offering voluntary buyouts to up to 60,000 employees in the US, UK, Canada and India, The Verge reported. That is roughly a quarter of a workforce of about 223,000. In a message to staff, chief people officer Amy Coleman described it as a chance to leave "with financial support and career planning resources."

Translation: a voluntary exit offer is a layoff in which the company asks politely first. The employee chooses to go, takes a payment, and the company reduces costs without having to pick names from a list. Microsoft's offer, according to The Verge, is meant to trim staff without another round of layoffs.

Eligibility depends heavily on where you sit. Sources told The Verge that staff in AI experiences, Copilot, CoreAI, Windows + Devices, Xbox, finance and human resources are eligible. Cloud + AI, MicrosoftAI, and corporate, external and legal affairs are partially participating. Marketing, commercial business, discovery and quantum, and security are not. The company, in other words, has quietly published a map of which departments it considers load-bearing.

The offer is also new in kind. Microsoft's internal HRweb note, seen by The Verge, says it is not based on age, length of service or performance. That sets it apart from the earlier retirement program, which was limited to about 9,000 US employees whose age and years of service added up to 70 or more. This time, in principle, anyone in an eligible group can raise a hand.

That is where the incentives get interesting. In any voluntary program, the people most likely to accept are those with the best prospects elsewhere, which is not always the group the company would pick to lose. Microsoft's answer appears to be the careful carve-outs by team, though the sources do not say how it will handle the staff who take the deal but turn out to be hard to replace.

Coleman's memo is candid on one point: "The Voluntary Exit Offer is new for us," she wrote, adding, "We'll learn from it." It is not often that a memo about 60,000 jobs announces itself as a pilot. Microsoft also says it may consider another offer "where there is a business and workforce need," which is the corporate way of saying this may not be the last invitation.

The timing sits alongside other cuts. Fortune reported that Xbox CEO Asha Sharma is planning new rounds of layoffs, according to The Verge. So the company is simultaneously asking some people to leave voluntarily and preparing to ask others involuntarily. Employees in eligible teams now have a decision to make, with the useful context that declining the offer does not appear to be a guarantee of anything.
