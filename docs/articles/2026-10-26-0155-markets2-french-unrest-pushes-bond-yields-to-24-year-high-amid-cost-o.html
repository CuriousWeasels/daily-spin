France Riots Rattle Bond Market, Which Has Been Quietly Worried About Budget Anyway

With 10-year yields above 5% for the first time since 2002, investors are asking whether a divided parliament can pass a budget before the protests do more damage.

The wire said: What next for France as riots rattle markets? Three questions facing investors ahead of more protests · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 26, 2026 01:55 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Protests that began last month with high school students complaining about school conditions have grown into nationwide unrest over living costs and inequality. Violence flared for a second straight weekend, while France's borrowing costs climbed to levels not seen in over two decades.

Why it matters

Higher French borrowing costs raise the price of running the euro zone's second-largest economy, and some investors fear the strain could spread to other countries. For ordinary people, a weaker economy and a stalled budget mean more uncertainty over jobs, spending and taxes.

Hundreds of thousands of people marched across France on Saturday, and by nightfall some of the rallies had turned to clashes in Paris and Marseille, CNBC reported. The Interior Ministry counted 410,000 participants. Unions said the real number was twice as high, which is the traditional French method of settling a crowd estimate: each side picks the figure that suits its argument and defends it until the next march.

The bond market has been counting too. The yield on France's 10-year government bond, known as the OAT, rose 11 basis points on Thursday to its highest since 2002, and it settled around 5.03% on Friday. Translation: a basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point, and a yield is the interest rate the government pays to borrow. When it climbs, lenders are saying they would like more money for the same risk. The gap over German Bunds, the euro zone's safe benchmark, is the widest since November 2011.

The protests did not create the problem so much as sit down in the middle of it. The government wants to cut its budget deficit to 5% of GDP in 2027, from a projected 5.4% in 2026. The National Assembly is due to resume debating the tax measures meant to deliver the savings on Oct. 27, and opposition lawmakers are resisting. Ebury's Matthew Ryan said it remains unclear whether the budget will pass, and that if it does it may be so full of concessions to left and right that the deficit stays largely unaddressed. Translation: everyone gets something, and the arithmetic gets nothing.

Moody's on Friday kept France at Aa3, which it still calls high grade, but left its outlook negative. It cited the risk that political fragmentation will continue to impair the functioning of the legislature. It also said the outlook could return to stable if institutions manage to narrow the deficit. That is a polite way of saying the grade is fine and the grown-ups in the room are the issue.

Natixis's Alain Durré said the government could try Article 49.3, which pushes a bill through without a final vote unless lawmakers pass a no-confidence motion, or use executive ordinances. Either could calm investors, he said. He also noted that recent selling has come mainly from tactical portfolio reallocations, with traditional buy-and-hold investors still sitting on the sidelines. In other words, the patient money has not left yet, and it is watching the door.

The economic damage so far is measured in worry rather than wreckage. ING's Charlotte de Montpellier said it had expected growth of just 0.3% in 2026 and 0.6% in 2027, and that unrest could make it weaker. Past episodes offer a rough scale: INSEE estimated the Yellow Vests may have trimmed fourth-quarter 2018 growth by 0.1 percentage point, and the Banque de France put the hit from the 2023 pension strikes at around 0.1 point in the first quarter. Markets can look past short disruptions, she said, but a lasting loss of confidence is another matter.

Companies are already feeling it. The CAC 40 has fallen 4.5% over the past month. Carrefour shares dropped more than 14% Wednesday after it cut its profit outlook and investment, citing the unrest and higher energy costs; CEO Alexandre Bompard called the situation unprecedented. ING said more than 2,000 businesses had been directly affected by Tuesday. Luxury is less exposed, since France is 8% of LVMH's 2025 revenue, though Carmignac's Kevin Thozet noted tax-free tourist purchases make up around 20% of that French slice.

More protests are planned for Oct. 24, and a union-backed national day of strikes and protests is set for Nov. 5. Thozet warned that another six months of political vacuum is a very long time for markets and entrepreneurs. Bond investors, who rarely wait six months for anything, will be watching the Assembly on Oct. 27.
