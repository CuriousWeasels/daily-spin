Albania's Students Skip Class to Teach Prime Minister a Lesson in Resignation

After five days of clashes in Tirana, Edi Rama says governments change through elections, which are due in 2029, and which the students would like moved up.

The wire said: Albania: Students demand political change and resignation of Prime Minister Edi Rama · DW

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 18, 2026 01:40 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Albanians have protested since the summer, first over a tourism development in the protected coastal area of Zvernec linked to Jared Kushner, and later over corruption allegations and Prime Minister Edi Rama's long hold on power. This week student-led demonstrations in Tirana turned violent.

Why it matters

Albania wants to join the European Union by 2030, and the EU prefers candidates whose capitals are not on fire. How Rama handles the unrest, and the corruption investigations around him, could shape that bid and the country's next election.

Demonstrators and riot police fought in central Tirana for a fifth consecutive day on Saturday, the BBC reported, with masked protesters, many of them high school and university students, throwing rocks and petrol bombs at officers guarding the education ministry. Police answered with water cannon and tear gas, and there were reports that rubber bullets were used for the first time. The education ministry, it should be noted, is the building the students were already boycotting.

The protests began months ago and were largely peaceful. DW reported that they started in late May over a multimillion-euro tourism project in Zvernec linked to Kushner, and grew into what is called the Flamingo Revolution. The BBC dates the start to early June. Either way, one 19-year-old medical student told the BBC, "We were peaceful for 135 days." Translation: the government had a long window in which to respond to peaceful protest, and it was not used.

The tally so far is grim and bureaucratic. DW said 152 people were arrested and 324 placed under investigation over three days, with police reporting 71 officers injured. The BBC said 80 people had been charged with disorder. Both outlets note injuries on both sides. Al Jazeera reported that protesters accuse police of brutality.

Rama stayed silent for the first two days, then appeared in a video recorded inside the fire-damaged College of Europe building, which is a strong visual argument if not a conciliatory one. He blamed an opposition hiding behind masked people, told Reuters he would not resign because governments are changed in elections, and invoked "foreign hands" and "foreign actors" seeking to halt Albania's EU progress. He did not name them. Opposition leader Sali Berisha, 82, mocked the claim, asking whether it was Greece, Iran, Serbia or someone else. Translation: an accusation of foreign interference works best when it includes a foreigner.

The students are not simply asking for a change of party. DW quoted a 20-year-old journalism student who wants the political class of the past 35 years gone, which covers both Rama and Berisha. A 22-year-old economics student said she earns €540 and cannot imagine affording a flat in the luxury towers rising over Tirana. That is a housing policy critique delivered at tear-gas range.

Rama's position is stronger on paper than on the street. His Socialist Party holds 83 of 140 seats, and elections are not due until 2029. But the BBC reported that Albania's special prosecution service recently arrested secret service chief Vlora Hyseni, accused of helping Rama's friend, businessman Ergys Agasi, evade justice. Agasi denies corruption, Hyseni says she was trying to recruit him as an intelligence asset, and Rama says he knows Agasi but knew nothing of illegal dealings.

Analysts see no tidy exit. One civil society activist told DW that the only solution is a caretaker government led by a non-partisan prime minister and elections in May 2027. Another analyst warned that a protest must produce a platform, because "you cannot vote for a protest." Brussels has urged calm and dialogue, which is what Brussels says in most situations. Watch whether Rama's offer remains "wait for 2029" or whether the elections get closer than the calendar says.
