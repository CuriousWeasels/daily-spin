Israel Votes, Counts, and Discovers Arithmetic: 53 Seats Is Not 61

With 99% of ballots counted, Netanyahu's bloc leads Eisenkot's 53 to 48 in the Knesset, but neither side has a majority, and Arab-led parties holding 19 seats may decide who governs.

Catch up

Israel held its first general election since the Hamas-led attacks of 7 October 2023 and the wars that followed in Gaza, Lebanon and Iran. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu faced former military chief Gadi Eisenkot, and exit polls had projected a near tie.

Why it matters

Whoever can assemble 61 seats will decide Israel's wartime leadership, its judicial and military conscription policies, and its international standing. If nobody can, the country faces another election, which is the one outcome everyone says they want to avoid.

Partial results from Tuesday's election show Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc narrowly ahead of Gadi Eisenkot's, but short of a majority, the BBC reported. With 99% of ballots counted, Likud and its far-right and ultra-Orthodox allies were on track for 53 of the 120 Knesset seats. Eisenkot's Yashar party and three other Jewish opposition parties were set for 48. A majority requires 61, a number that neither side has and both have promised to reach.

The remaining seats belong to Arab-led parties, which are on course to win an unprecedented 19. Translation: after a decisive night of voting, the people with the most leverage are the ones both camps spent the campaign pledging not to call. According to the BBC, all but one of the Jewish parties have said they will not work with them. That is a firm position for as long as it is not the only route to a majority.

The turnout explains much of this. Herzog praised the 77.7% turnout, the highest in more than two decades, as a badge of honour for Israeli society. The BBC reported that the two Arab parties, the United Arab List and the Joint List, are set to almost double their representation thanks to a surge in Arab Israeli voting. Arab citizens make up about a fifth of the population. Joint List head Yousef Jabareen said voters were motivated partly by a chance to remove National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir from office.

Netanyahu's bloc did better than the exit polls suggested, which had ranged from a three-seat lead to a tie. Even so, 53 seats is 15 fewer than it had controlled in the last Knesset. Netanyahu told supporters his bloc had won a great majority, and that it was forbidden to form a government relying on what he called anti-Zionist Arab parties. A majority, in this usage, is a number that may be rounded in the speaker's favour.

Eisenkot's side has its own arithmetic problem. He declared that the public had spoken in favour of change, but the BBC noted he has attached conditions to any partnership with Arab parties, including recognising Israel as Jewish and democratic. Right-wing parties in his bloc have flatly rejected it. Yair Golan of the Democrats urged partners to form a change government with the Arab parties, and United Arab List leader Mansour Abbas said he hoped the numbers could reach 61. Jabareen replied that the opposition cannot dictate conditions. Everyone wants a coalition, and nobody wants the coalition partners.

Meanwhile, Likud has accused the Arab parties of stealing the election. It told the Central Elections Committee they had used bribery, threats, violence, fraud and prohibited software, though the BBC said it offered no evidence. The committee said it would examine the claims in a post-election review. Its chairman, Justice Noam Sohlberg, also instructed the Shin Bet to investigate serious suspicions of improper foreign funding. Haaretz cited a source saying the claim concerned Qatar.

The count is not quite finished. Officials began tallying as many as 480,000 absentee ballots from soldiers, diplomats and hospital patients, which could shift seat allocations under Israel's proportional system. After official results, President Isaac Herzog will consult every faction and choose the leader most likely to form a government, a process that could take weeks. If that fails, Israel holds another election. Herzog said everyone should arrive with an open mind, which is the one item in short supply on either side.
