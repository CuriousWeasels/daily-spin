German Courts Inform Google That Its AI Answers Are, Legally Speaking, Its Answers

A second regional court has held Google responsible for false AI search text, even as the first case vanished into a settlement before any appeals court could weigh in.

The wire said: Two German courts now hold Google liable for what its AI overviews say · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 18, 2026 14:56 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

In late May, a Munich court ruled that Google's AI Overviews are the company's own statements, after the feature falsely tied two publishers to fraud. Google announced an appeal, then settled, according to The Decoder.

Why it matters

If courts keep treating AI summaries as the company's own speech, anyone wrongly described by a search engine gains a faster route to a takedown, and Google gains a new category of legal exposure. The bigger question is whether publishers whose work feeds those answers can eventually claim a share.

The Aachen Regional Court, in a decision dated September 24, 2026, barred Google from spreading three variants of false text about a physician through its AI Mode search feature, The Decoder reported. The court relied on the earlier Munich ruling and treated the AI output as Google's own statements rather than links to someone else's website. The decision is a preliminary injunction, so it is not final, and Google can challenge it.

The facts are unpleasant for the defendant. The doctor had been chief physician and professor of neuroradiology from 2010 until late June 2026. On August 28, a colleague told him that Google's AI was explaining his departure with an alleged sexual assault against his secretary. Follow-up searches using words like suspension, termination and court proceedings produced detailed, multi-paragraph accounts, including an invented legal aftermath.

None of it involved him. According to the court, the criminal case concerned a different former university professor, which the judges could confirm from their own records. The AI had, in effect, taken one man's case file and handed it to another, which is a remarkable service for a product marketed as saving people time.

The doctor emailed Google to remove the content. In mid-September, Google said it would take no action. A formal legal demand sent the same day was acknowledged but, per the court, changed nothing, and the false texts stayed live. Translation: the company received two requests to stop saying a man committed a crime he did not commit, and filed both under received.

Because the claims could badly damage his professional reputation, the court acted without first hearing Google. Violations carry fines of up to 250,000 euros each. The court also noted that Google could check its output against the source websites it used, and that had the company put the doctor's objections to its own AI, the system would have caught the errors itself.

The court's central point was one Google has resisted: the company runs AI-generated search and is therefore responsible for the answers it generates. Aachen quoted Munich in describing the output as independent, new and substantive statements built from content on various third-party websites. A neutral middleman passes information along. A system that writes new sentences is, in this view, a author.

The Munich case never produced a higher-court ruling. The Decoder learned that Google's publicly announced appeal was never decided because the case ended in a settlement. Why it settled, and whether money changed hands, is unknown, and neither Google nor the publisher would comment. Settling is a tidy way to avoid precedent, though it does not delete the reasoning already written down, and Aachen just proved judges can read.

The Decoder also raises two larger risks. One is an incentive problem: if Google keeps settling, specialized law firms could probe AI search until it produces something defamatory, seek an injunction and negotiate. The other is bigger. If a top court ruled that AI answers are Google's own content, publishers whose texts feed them might claim compensation, which would upend the economics of AI search.

For now, the watch item is whether Google challenges the Aachen injunction and lets a higher court rule, or settles again. Settling is cheaper each time, until a case arrives that nobody can afford to make disappear.
