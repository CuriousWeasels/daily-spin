Bank of England Official Notes Bond Market Now Largely Owned by Leveraged Hedge Funds, Says Shock Likelier

Sarah Breeden tells CNBC that hedge funds, holding a record 7% of Treasurys, have raised the odds that one market's problem becomes everyone's.

The wire said: Why hedge funds could be making a bond market shock more likely · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 19, 2026 17:49 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Hedge funds have grown into major holders of government bonds, often using borrowed money in strategies like the basis trade. Bond yields hit multi-decade highs earlier this month in the U.S. and U.K., while France's borrowing costs have also risen.

Why it matters

Government bond yields set borrowing costs for mortgages, companies and governments themselves, so instability in this market reaches ordinary budgets. If leveraged funds are forced to sell at once, a local wobble can spread across markets.

Bank of England deputy governor Sarah Breeden told CNBC on Thursday that the likelihood of a broader market shock is "greater than what it was," because hedge funds now play a larger role in global government bond markets. She spoke on the sidelines of the IMF-World Bank annual meetings in Bangkok. In short, the safest asset in the financial system increasingly has a tenant who rents it with borrowed money.

Breeden, who handles financial stability and sits on the Monetary Policy Committee, said the investor base has shifted. Bond markets were once dominated by what she called real money investors, meaning insurance companies and pension funds. Translation: buyers who purchase bonds with their own cash, intend to hold them for decades, and are not thrilled by daily price moves. Increasingly, she said, the holders are hedge funds that are "price-sensitive, leveraged, international."

Each of those adjectives matters. Price-sensitive means they sell when prices fall. Leveraged means they borrowed to buy, so falling prices force them to sell more. International means the selling does not stay politely within one country. Put together, Breeden said, the likelihood of a shock in one market transmitting to another is higher than before.

The IMF made a similar point in its Global Financial Stability Report, published Tuesday. It put hedge fund gross assets at about $13 trillion and acknowledged that the funds support market liquidity and efficiency. Translation: when they trade, there is usually someone to trade with, which is useful right up until they all want the same exit. The IMF also warned that leverage and crowded trades could turbo-charge stress in turmoil, with spillovers to areas such as banks' prime brokerage businesses.

The strategy under the microscope is the basis trade. Per CNBC, a fund buys cash Treasurys and shorts the matching futures contracts, aiming to capture a small price gap between the two. Translation: the profit per trade is tiny, so funds borrow heavily to make it worth the effort. Small margins multiplied by large debts is a tidy recipe for returns, and also for the phrase "unwinding."

The scale is not trivial. According to U.S. Office of Financial Research data published Aug. 19, hedge funds held a record 7% of the $30 trillion Treasury market at the end of 2025. That works out to roughly $2 trillion, a sum that is large enough to matter if it needs to leave quickly.

The backdrop is already jumpy. CNBC reported that 10-year and 30-year Treasury yields hit their highest since 2002 earlier this month, and U.K. gilt yields touched multi-decade highs. French OAT yields have also risen sharply on fiscal worries, stoking fears of a fresh euro zone debt crisis. The latest data show the 10-year Treasury at 5.1% and the 2-year at 4.77%, as of Oct. 15. Translation for households: the government's cost of borrowing is high, and the pricing for mortgages and business loans tends to follow.

Breeden did not predict a specific event, and the sources do not say one is imminent. Her point is structural: the shock absorbers in the bond market have been partly replaced by participants who are paid to be nimble, not patient. Regulators are watching it, which is the central banker's way of saying they would prefer to be wrong about this one.
