Maduro Pleads Not Guilty to Torture Charges, Judge Does It for Him Anyway

A rewritten indictment adds torture allegations to the drug case against Venezuela's deposed president and his wife, with trial lawyers asking to move the date from 1 June to 14 June.

The wire said: Nicolás Maduro tells New York court new torture allegations are ‘false’ · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 17, 2026 01:44 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Nicolás Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores, were seized from their Caracas home by the US military in a night-time raid in January and now face a drug trafficking case in New York. Prosecutors have since rewritten the indictment to add allegations of a state-run torture system.

Why it matters

Prosecuting a foreign leader in a US court on torture charges is exceedingly rare, so the case could set a template, or a cautionary tale, for how far American courts reach into other countries' conduct. The defense's immunity argument will test whether being a head of state protects anyone who is later captured by the military of another country.

Nicolás Maduro told a New York court on Thursday that torture allegations against him were "false accusations," according to the Guardian. He appeared shackled at the ankles to face a rewritten indictment that accuses him and his wife, Cilia Flores, of leading a state-sponsored campaign of repression. The indictment says the campaign tortured scores of victims, including three Americans.

The procedure got slightly unusual at the plea stage. Judge Alvin K Hellerstein asked Maduro to enter a plea, and Maduro twice responded by criticizing the indictment instead, saying it contained "false accusations and false testimony." Translation: a plea is supposed to be one word, and Maduro delivered a short closing argument twice. The judge, having given him two chances, entered a not-guilty plea on his behalf, which is the court's way of saying the answer was understood.

Flores, 70 and a former attorney general, was more economical. Speaking through an interpreter, she said: "I am completely innocent. I am not guilty." The indictment alleges she helped develop the repressive system and ordered the torture of some specific people.

The allegations themselves are grim and are written plainly in the indictment. Prosecutors say Maduro oversaw a system that beat, asphyxiated and delivered electric shocks to its victims. They say he personally recommended torture methods, received updates on the treatment of specific victims, and threatened to kill one prisoner's child as a form of psychological torture. His lawyer, Barry Pollack, said last week that Maduro denies the charges and "looks forward to his day in court." Maduro has always denied wrongdoing.

There is a legal logic to the new charges. Torture is a crime under international law and can be prosecuted outside the country where it occurred, regardless of the victims' nationality. Translation: the law is built so that a torturer cannot escape simply by being the one who runs the building. In practice, the Guardian notes, US prosecutions of foreign officials for this are exceedingly rare, with only a few precedents, including the son of former Liberian president Charles Taylor, Russians responsible for war crimes in Ukraine, and a former prison warden in Bashar Assad's Syria. Those are mostly people who were not, until recently, presidents.

That brings up the defense. In the earlier drug case, in which Maduro and Flores pleaded not guilty to conspiring to flood the US with cocaine, their attorneys argue the case should be dismissed on immunity grounds because the couple were the leader and first lady of a foreign country. Translation: heads of state generally cannot be hauled into another country's courts, and the defense would like that principle applied to a head of state who was hauled in by the military. Whether the court agrees will matter well beyond Venezuela.

The revised indictment reaches back to 2013, when Maduro took over from his mentor Hugo Chávez, who died of cancer that year. Maduro has also been under investigation for years by the International Criminal Court over alleged crimes against humanity tied to his crackdown on opponents. Prosecutors told the judge that both sides want the trial moved from 1 June to 14 June, a rare point on which everyone in the room agrees.
