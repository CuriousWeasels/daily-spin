Europe Plans Internet Cable to Greenland, Politely Routing Around Washington

The EU's Pan-Arctic Cable would link Europe to Greenland, possibly Canada, and eventually Asia, while a new report says Pacific cables owned by US tech giants face the highest sabotage risk.

The wire said: As US ties fray, Europe plans first undersea cable link to Greenland · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 29, 2026 03:01 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The European Commission published an Arctic strategy on October 20, 2026 naming a direct subsea cable to Greenland as a flagship project. Separately, a September 2026 report for a US congressional advisory commission ranked Pacific cables by their vulnerability to Chinese gray zone tactics.

Why it matters

Nearly all internet traffic between continents travels through cables on the seabed, so where they run and who can reach them shapes the cost, speed, and security of everyday online life. Governments are now treating them as strategic assets rather than plumbing.

The European Union plans to build what would be its first undersea internet cable linking Europe directly to Greenland, according to Ars Technica. The Pan-Arctic Cable is described as a flagship project in a European Commission joint communication published on October 20, 2026. The plan could later extend to Canada and eventually to Japan and South Korea, a route that notably avoids the United States.

The document calls for "a sovereign, secure and resilient submarine cable connection linking Greenland directly to the EU." Translation: a cable is a wire, and a sovereign wire is one whose owners do not need to call anyone else before using it. The Commission also describes Canada as a possible stepping stone for expansion toward Asia, referring to it as one of the "like-minded partners."

The timing is not subtle. Ars Technica reports that repeated Trump administration attempts to claim Greenland were settled in September 2026 by a trilateral security agreement among the United States, Greenland and Denmark. The deal keeps Greenland an autonomous territory within the Kingdom of Denmark while expanding US military access. Relations between Washington and its European and Canadian allies have nonetheless frayed over trade wars and a more pro-Russia stance on Ukraine, and Europe appears to have concluded that its data should have a backup route.

Elisabeth Braw of the Atlantic Council told Ars that five years ago the project would not have looked commercially attractive. Its being considered now, she said, is "very much on trend and a sign of our times." Translation: the business case is not that the cable makes money, but that the alternative makes anxiety.

There is also a more practical motive. According to TeleGeography, more than 90 percent of communications between Europe and Asia pass through cables in the Red Sea. That has become riskier since open warfare resumed this year between the Yemeni government and the Houthi faction, with Houthi forces firing missiles and drones at commercial ships near the Bab el-Mandeb chokepoint. Concentrating nearly all of a continent's traffic in one waterway was efficient right up until it was not.

Meanwhile, on the other side of the planet, a different set of worries. Ars Technica reports that a report published in September 2026 for the US-China Economic and Security Review Commission, an independent body advising Congress, found that Pacific cables owned by companies such as Google and Meta face some of the greatest sabotage risks. The report was contracted to the London-based China Strategic Risks Institute and comes as Chinese research ships spend an extraordinary amount of time apparently surveying cables, and Chinese commercial ships are investigated over cable cuts in European and Asian waters.

To be fair to the seabed, most cable damage is still caused by fishing nets and dragging anchors, with storms and undersea landslides also contributing. The analysts' concern is the suspicious remainder, which they call gray zone tactics: actions that fall short of military confrontation, and that come with a convenient claim of accident. The report's authors built an interactive Trans-Pacific Cable Gray Zone Vulnerability Index, which scores routes by depth, nearby shipping traffic, ownership, proximity to China's exclusive economic zone and territorial claims, and whether the cable touches Taiwan.

The index's practical use is triage. Governments and operators cannot guard every kilometer of ocean floor, so a ranking suggests where to put underwater sensors or patrols first. That is useful, though it also amounts to a published list of which cables are the juiciest, which is the sort of thing security researchers have learned to accept.

The two stories fit together neatly: one continent is trying to build a cable that avoids a single chokepoint and a single ally, while another is mapping which cables are easiest to cut. Watch for whether the Pan-Arctic Cable gets a budget and a route, since a flagship project with neither is just a very ambitious line on a map.
