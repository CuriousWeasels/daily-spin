Rabbi Rules Olives Are Fair Game; Settlers, Olive Farmers, and Other Rabbis Weigh In

A religious ruling permitting settlers to pick Palestinian olives coincides with dozens of attacks on harvesters, including one on a U.S. photojournalist, weeks before Israeli elections.

The wire said: Israeli settlers beat Palestinians and a U.S. photojournalist in West Bank attacks · NPR World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 17, 2026 14:30 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Every autumn, Palestinian families harvest olives in the occupied West Bank, and settler violence during the season has been a recurring problem. This year, a prominent rabbi issued a ruling that critics say gives that violence a theological permission slip.

Why it matters

The olive harvest is a major source of income and tradition for Palestinian farmers, and sources describe many being driven off their land. With elections set for October 27, the story shows how religious authority, campaign incentives, and military protection can intersect.

Masked Israeli settlers attacked Palestinians harvesting olives in the occupied West Bank on Friday, injuring several people, including a photojournalist working for The Washington Post, according to NPR. Al Jazeera reported that at least 19 people were injured in 34 attacks by settlers and the Israeli military that day, and that five vehicles were torched. It is a busy schedule for a fruit harvest.

The same day, Rabbi Dov Lior issued a religious ruling, according to Al Jazeera. In it, Palestinian landowners who could not reach their olives due to Israeli restrictions were deemed already compensated by the years when they could. Translation: being blocked from your own trees counts as having been paid for them. Lior also called picking the olives an "additional virtue."

Lior urged Jews to harvest even far from Jewish communities, in order to "demonstrate our ownership" of the land. He wrote that Arabs never bought the land and so it is "stolen land in their hands." The ruling thus manages to make the harvest, the claim, and the commandment the same activity, which is efficient if nothing else.

Lior is not a fringe name in this world. Al Jazeera describes him as the spiritual guide of National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir's Jewish Power party and an authority among the Hilltop Youth, a group devoted to seizing Palestinian land. Analyst Mamoun Abu Amer told Al Jazeera that Ben-Gvir competes for votes with TikTok and populist rhetoric, in what he called a "market for extremism." Translation: in this market, the product is outrage and the voters are the customers.

The weekend's reported incidents are long. Al Jazeera cited the Palestine Red Crescent Society saying four women and a child were among six people injured in Ma'in village in Masafer Yatta. Wafa reported that Israeli forces fired on farmers in Deir Ammar and forced them off their land. Kafr Malek's mayor said settlers killed about 20 sheep. Al Jazeera's Nour Odeh counted 300 documented attacks since the start of October and said settlers advertise campaigns on social media.

Not every rabbi agreed with Lior. Rabbis of the Gush Etzion settlements' religious council called taking Palestinian crops theft, blasphemy, and a moral defect. Tzohar, an organisation of 800 Orthodox rabbis, said only the state can confiscate property, and that as long as it recognises Arab ownership, the olives belong to their owners. Opposition lawmaker Gilad Kariv said those who stand aside join the camp of Lior "and his criminals."

Abu Amer offered a second reading of the rabbinical pushback: it may be partly political, helping the government present the attackers as a small group of Hilltop Youth rather than the settler mainstream. Odeh said the settlers' names, locations and homes are known, yet no one stops them, because the violence is "politically expedient" before the vote. Sources here are Al Jazeera's reporters and analysts, and the Israeli government's response is not included in the material.

The harvest ends sooner than the election campaign does. After October 27, the olives will be picked or not, and the question of who is stopping whom will remain on the ballot, whether or not it is on the ballot paper.
