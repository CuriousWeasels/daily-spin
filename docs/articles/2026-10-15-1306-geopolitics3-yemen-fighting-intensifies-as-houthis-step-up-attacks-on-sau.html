Saudi Arabia Keeps Asian Cup on Schedule While Missiles Reschedule Everything Else

Houthi strikes have shut Riyadh's airport and delayed tennis and football fixtures, yet the Asian Football Confederation says it can wait until the end of November to decide whether January's tournament happens.

The wire said: Fighting in Yemen intensifies: What’s the latest on the battlefield? · Al Jazeera

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 15, 2026 13:06 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Yemen's Houthi rebels and the Saudi-backed Yemeni government have been fighting since the Houthis seized Sanaa in 2014, and a 2022 ceasefire has broken down in recent months. The Houthis are now striking Saudi airports and oil sites directly.

Why it matters

Saudi Arabia is trying to present itself as a safe host for global sport and investment, and attacks on its airports and energy sites threaten that. They also add pressure to oil exports and shipping routes already strained by the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, according to Al Jazeera.

Football's governing body for Asia said on Thursday that the 2027 Asian Cup, due to open in Saudi Arabia on January 7, could be pushed back a year to 2028 if needed. Asian Football Confederation president Sheikh Salman bin Ebrahim Al Khalifa said organisers are in "no hurry" to decide and could wait until the end of November, according to Al Jazeera. He also said that "everything is on schedule," which is a reassuring thing to say about a schedule that may be moved by twelve months.

The context is a sharp escalation. Al Jazeera reported that Houthi attacks on Riyadh's King Khalid international airport over the weekend killed 14 people and wounded more than 300. Authorities say the airport will stay shut at least until Saturday, and several airlines have cancelled flights. Two more people were killed in an attack on Abha airport in the south.

The Houthis, formally known as Ansar Allah, say they hit an Aramco site in Dhahran, along with Riyadh airport and a refinery in the capital. A Saudi energy official confirmed that an Aramco building in Dhahran was hit, causing a "limited fire" and no casualties. Translation: the building burned, but not by enough to alarm anyone who has not read the previous sentence.

Al Jazeera's correspondent in Sanaa described the Houthi strategy as meeting fire with fire: airport for airport, capital for capital, energy facility for energy facility. The correspondent noted that Saudi Arabia has "much more to lose" from this, because Sanaa's airport has been closed for years and has no Yemeni airliners to ground. It is hard to cancel flights that are not flying.

Sport has noticed. Al-Nassr's Asian Champions League match was delayed an hour on Monday after Saudi Civil Defence said it had intercepted a missile fired towards Riyadh. Three domestic league matches were postponed this week, and the Six Kings Slam tennis exhibition, featuring Djokovic, Alcaraz and Zverev, has been moved to the end of December. A conference nicknamed "Davos in the desert" is also in doubt, which is perhaps the first time anyone has worried about a Davos being cancelled.

Participants are less relaxed than the organisers. Australia, one of the 24 qualified teams, says it is "closely monitoring" the situation, which is what governments say when they have not yet decided to say something else. The Korea Professional Footballers Association voiced "serious concern" for player safety, and its president, Lee Keun-ho, said that no tournament's value can take precedence over lives, according to Yonhap.

On the ground in Yemen, the fighting is concentrated around Taiz and the Red Sea coast near the Bab al-Mandeb strait, a chokepoint that matters more now that Saudi Arabia relies on it for oil exports since Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz in March, according to Al Jazeera. Government forces report 2,309 "targeting operations" and 460 destroyed Houthi vehicles under their "Dawn of Yemen" offensive. Al Jazeera's correspondent described a routine since early September of advances, retreats and counterattacks. The Houthi siege of Taiz has left the city short of fuel and gas, and the World Food Programme says wheat flour stocks are nearly exhausted. The Red Cross reports some hospitals admitting more than 700 weapon-wounded patients in a single week.

The practical calendar is simple. The AFC will keep monitoring until the end of November, and Saudi Arabia will keep intercepting what it can. The tournament's date will probably be decided by whichever of the two is more persuasive.
