Britain Sends Typhoons to Saudi Arabia, Strictly for Defense, as 'Davos in the Desert' Gets Postponed

London will protect Saudi cities from Houthi drones and missiles but not strike back, while the kingdom's flagship investor conference waits until 2027.

The wire said: UK fighter jets to protect Saudi Arabia from Houthi attacks · DW

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 22, 2026 02:03 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Yemen's civil war has escalated since a years-long truce collapsed in September, with Houthi forces hitting Saudi airports and oil facilities. Britain announced more military support for Riyadh, and Saudi Arabia postponed its big investor conference.

Why it matters

Attacks on tankers and oil infrastructure push up energy prices, and a kingdom that cannot host its own investor conference has a harder time attracting the foreign money it wants. For travelers and expatriates, the risks around Saudi airports are now serious enough that the US is pulling diplomats' families out.

Britain will send Royal Air Force Typhoon fighter jets to Saudi Arabia to help defend the kingdom against Houthi attacks, the UK government announced on Tuesday, according to DW. The Ministry of Defense said the aircraft will protect Saudi cities and critical infrastructure from drones and missiles, and will not carry out offensive strikes in Yemen. It is the aerial equivalent of agreeing to hold the umbrella but not to ask who is making the rain.

The deployment follows a Saudi request and builds on assistance London announced in September. Britain will also send an Atlas A400M transport aircraft and military advisers, and has extended for another 90 days its offer of air-to-air refueling for Saudi aircraft operating against the Houthis. Translation: air-to-air refueling means a plane fills up another plane in mid-flight, which lets allies take part in a war without technically being in the part of it that has addresses.

British Defense Secretary Wes Streeting called the Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia "absolutely despicable." He singled out the assaults on Riyadh's international airport, which DW reports has been targeted 20 times since the start of October. Reports cited by DW say strikes on the airport have killed at least 18 people and wounded hundreds this month. Al Jazeera, citing Saudi civil aviation authorities, gave figures for individual attacks: 12 dead and more than 300 injured on October 10, and one dead and 11 injured on October 11.

The violence is not confined to Riyadh. Al Jazeera reported that one person was killed and eight injured in an attack on King Fahd International Airport in Dammam on Wednesday, and that two people died earlier that day when Saudi air defenses intercepted a drone aimed at an Aramco facility at the Shaybah oilfield. Inside Yemen, authorities said a Houthi strike on Monday killed 58 people at a training camp for Saudi-backed government security forces, according to DW.

The economic damage is arriving on schedule. DW reports the Houthis have imposed a naval blockade and attacked tankers, disrupting oil exports and pushing up energy prices. Saudi Arabia, which is the one party in this story that sells oil, is thus being hurt by a rise in the price of oil, a situation that tends to happen when the product cannot leave the building.

Then there is the conference. Saudi Arabia has postponed its Future Investment Initiative, informally called "Davos in the Desert," until 2027. It had been scheduled for October 26-29, and according to media reports cited by DW, major banks and private equity firms had raised security concerns about attending. The event is a central part of the kingdom's effort to attract foreign investment and diversify away from oil. Translation: the plan to reduce dependence on oil now depends on whether people can safely land at the airport.

Washington is also reading the room. Al Jazeera reported that the US State Department ordered diplomats and family members to leave its missions in Saudi Arabia, and Reuters-sourced reports say family members will be withdrawn from several other US missions in the region. Saudi Arabia and the United States accuse Iran of supporting the Houthis, which Tehran denies.

What to watch: whether a defensive-only mandate survives contact with a campaign that keeps hitting airports. Jets that only defend are only as useful as the war is willing to stay on the defensive, and so far the Houthis have not been consulted.
