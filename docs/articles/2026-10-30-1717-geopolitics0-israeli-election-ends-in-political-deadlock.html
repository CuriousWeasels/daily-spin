Israel Votes, Counts, and Finds Nobody Has Enough Seats to Govern

With roughly 90 percent of ballots counted and neither Netanyahu's bloc nor Eisenkot's opposition holding a majority, coalition arithmetic now decides everything.

The wire said: Israel is in a political deadlock. What comes next? · NPR World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 30, 2026 17:17 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Israel held a parliamentary election that NPR describes as consequential and ended in deadlock. Benjamin Netanyahu's right-wing and religious bloc leads the bloc headed by former military chief Gadi Eisenkot, but neither can reach a governing majority in the Knesset.

Why it matters

Until a coalition forms, Netanyahu's government stays on in an interim capacity, and German officials warn that aid to Gaza and action on settler violence could stall while talks drag on. The result also hinges on Arab-majority parties that one bloc has ruled out working with.

Israel's election has produced what NPR calls a deadlock. With about 90 percent of votes counted, no clear winner has emerged. DW reported that Netanyahu's bloc is ahead of Eisenkot's, but being ahead in this system is like being the tallest person in a cab queue: it does not get you a cab.

The reason is the Knesset's arithmetic. A government needs a majority of seats, and neither bloc has a clear path to one. Translation: a coalition is a group of parties that agree to govern together, usually after weeks of negotiations in which everyone discovers how much they are worth.

The most interesting bargaining chips belong to the Arab-majority parties led by Palestinian citizens of Israel. According to DW, they made large gains and are usually counted in the opposition camp, which could make them kingmakers. Translation: a kingmaker is a party that cannot win but can decide who does, and is therefore briefly the most popular guest at every table.

There is a complication. DW reported that Netanyahu's bloc has ruled out working with these parties. Eisenkot's bloc has not said whether it would, though United Arab List/Ra'am, which featured a Jewish candidate, has said it is willing to work with his bloc to unseat Netanyahu. One side has refused the guests, and the other has not yet decided whether to send invitations.

Berlin is watching carefully and saying little. Foreign ministry spokesperson Martin Giese declined to comment at the regular press conference, while Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul said Germany would do everything it can to work well with any future government. Translation: whoever wins, we will be delighted.

Germany's silence is a diplomatic achievement of a sort, since its officials have been less restrained elsewhere. DW noted the government has distanced itself from Netanyahu's policies, at least in public rhetoric, and Wadephul recently said Israel is obligated to protect Palestinians' rights after an operation near Hebron that displaced around 3,000 people. Germany still does not plan to recognize a Palestinian state for now.

Some German politicians were more forthcoming. Jürgen Hardt of the CDU said a government closer to the center would be best and floated an Eisenkot-led minority government backed by parties representing Arab Israelis. He added that Netanyahu's campaign aimed mainly at Arab Israelis mobilized the opposition so much that his camp lost its majority. Green politician Omid Nouripour called the outcome good news for Israeli democracy.

The practical worry is time. Development Minister Reem Alabali Radovan told Politico that winter in Gaza will be difficult and that aid cannot wait for coalition negotiations. She also said radical settler violence must be stopped, noting that the Netanyahu government remains in office on an interim basis. Translation: a caretaker government is still a government, and Germany would like it to act like one.

What to watch next is whether anyone can assemble a majority, and whether the Arab-majority parties end up in the room or outside it. Coalition talks have no fixed length, which is convenient for negotiators and less so for everyone waiting on the result.
