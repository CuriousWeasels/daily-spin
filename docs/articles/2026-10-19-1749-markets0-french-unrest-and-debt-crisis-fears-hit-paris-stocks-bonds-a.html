France Discovers Bond Markets and Streets Can Both Riot at Once

French 10-year yields jumped more than 10 basis points to near multi-decade highs as weekend unrest met a 5% deficit and an election year.

The wire said: French unrest compounds debt crisis fears. Paris-listed stocks, bonds and the euro fall · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 19, 2026 17:49 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Hundreds of thousands marched across France over the weekend against living costs and weak public services, and some protests turned violent. On Monday, French bonds, stocks and the euro all fell.

Why it matters

When France pays more to borrow, the cost spreads to taxpayers, banks and companies, and the euro area's second-largest economy becomes a bigger worry for European markets. Any eventual fix means higher taxes or less spending, which tends to be unpopular.

France's 10-year government bond yield rose by more than 10 basis points on Monday, to just short of its highest level in decades, CNBC reported. The CAC 40 stock index fell 1.7% in afternoon trading and the euro slipped 0.15% against the dollar. The proximate cause was a weekend of mass protest, which is an unusual thing for a bond trader to have to price, though not a new one for France.

Translation: a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, and a yield is what the government must pay to borrow. When yields rise, the government is told, politely and by spreadsheet, that it is a riskier customer. French 2-year and 30-year yields rose 6 and 8 basis points respectively, so the disapproval was spread across the maturity range.

The weekend itself was serious. CNBC reported that more than 500,000 people took to the streets, most of them peacefully, over the cost of living and the state of schools and healthcare. Some protests turned violent: the city hall in Limoges was gutted by fire, Lyon's tourist office was set ablaze, shops were looted, and police were attacked. More than 2,000 people were arrested. One protester died of a heart attack and another lost a hand. President Emmanuel Macron blamed the violence on radical far-left groups and criminals, and said looters would pay for damage. Investing.com reported that the government is urging curfews in some areas.

Investors care less about the fires than about what they say about governability. Mike Bell of RBC BlueBay Asset Management told CNBC it looks like either the bond markets riot, the people riot, or both. His point is that France needs to fix its finances, and the fix he describes, such as raising the retirement age from 62, is something voters would likely not accept at the ballot box or elsewhere.

The numbers explain the nerves. France's deficit is around 5% of GDP, against the 3% limit in European Union rules. The extra yield France pays over Germany, known as the OAT-Bund spread, is at its highest since the early 2010s sovereign debt crisis, and French yields now exceed those of Italy and Greece. Translation: the market now treats Paris as a slightly worse credit than two countries that once defined the term.

Analysts describe a loop. Weak growth and a high deficit push up yields, higher yields raise interest costs, and higher interest costs widen the deficit. Sharon Bell of Goldman Sachs told CNBC the deficit will be hard to cut without painful measures, and that taxes on French businesses could rise. Overseas investors now hold nearly 60% of French debt, up from about 48% at the start of 2022, which makes the market more skittish in a risk-off mood.

The politics offer little reassurance. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu leads a minority government that must pass a 2027 budget, a task that has toppled two of his recent predecessors. Next year's presidential election looms, with Marine Le Pen topping many polls and, according to Goldman's Bell, not having detailed how she would keep the budget in order.

There are counterpoints. Ken Egan of KBRA noted that auctions last week showed solid underlying demand for French debt. Banque de France Governor Emmanuel Moulin told CNBC that rising yields reflect political uncertainty and that a 3% deficit is possible by 2029, which is the government's objective. Goldman's Bell does not think the situation warrants the European Central Bank using its Transmission Protection Instrument, a bond-buying backstop, though she said it would if spreads disrupted market liquidity. Using it would probably come with strict reform conditions.

The next test is the budget. Markets want a credible plan, voters want fewer cuts, and the French government now has to satisfy both before the election.
