Ukraine Hits St Petersburg Oil Depots Days After Trump's Energy Ceasefire Meets Diesel Deal

An overnight drone attack killed at least one person and set fire to two oil sites, as Washington's pursuit of cheaper fuel collides with Kyiv's campaign against Russian oil revenue.

The wire said: Oil facilities hit in deadly overnight attack on St Petersburg · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 19, 2026 01:58 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Donald Trump announced an "energy ceasefire" last week; Ukraine paused strikes on Russian oil for three days, accusing Russia of carrying on, then resumed them. Meanwhile the US has struck a deal to import Russian diesel, which Zelensky has criticised.

Why it matters

Fuel prices, sanctions policy and winter heating for Ukrainian civilians are now tied to the same dispute. Strikes on energy infrastructure on both sides raise the odds of a harder winter and keep pressure on US-Ukraine relations.

At least one person was killed and at least five injured in an overnight attack on St Petersburg that hit two of the city's oil facilities, according to the BBC. Governor Alexander Beglov said fires were being put out at the St Petersburg Oil Terminal and the Ruchyi oil depot. Kyiv had not commented at the time of reporting, which in wartime is a form of comment.

The attack also reached the commercial sector. A logistics centre belonging to Ozon, which the BBC describes as Russia's Amazon, caught fire after a drone strike; staff were evacuated and no one was hurt. The company said it was not accepting seller shipments there because the site was "currently serving other operational purposes". Translation: the warehouse is on fire.

VK, the Russian social network, confirmed a drone had targeted its regional data centre but said services were available "as usual" after users reported early-morning outages. Beglov added that 12 apartment buildings and two homes were damaged, Pulkovo Airport was temporarily closed, and mobile internet might be disrupted. Russia's defence ministry said it shot down more than 1,000 Ukrainian drones overnight.

The timing is awkward for diplomacy. Trump declared an energy ceasefire last week, meaning both sides would stop hitting each other's energy infrastructure. Ukraine paused strikes on Russian oil for three days while accusing Russia of continuing its own. Zelensky said Russia had targeted Ukraine's energy sector 76 times since the announcement. Ukraine resumed strikes earlier this week, which drew Trump's ire, since he wanted a truce to facilitate a diesel supply deal he struck with Vladimir Putin.

Here is the mechanism. US energy prices have skyrocketed during the Iran war, the BBC reports, and the midterm elections are coming. The administration's answer was to import Russian diesel and temporarily suspend a raft of sanctions on Russia. Translation: sanctions are restrictions meant to starve a government of revenue, and they are being paused so American drivers can pay less. Ukraine argues its oil strikes do the same job from the other direction, by limiting Moscow's main revenue stream. Zelensky suggested the deal amounted to the US investing in Russia's war effort, and some Republicans have also criticised the suspension.

None of this has stopped the talking. On Sunday Zelensky said on Telegram he had spoken with Trump's negotiators Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, and that their teams are in constant communication and preparing the next diplomatic steps. So the ceasefire is imperfect, the diesel deal is contested, and the diplomacy is continuous.

The human cost is on the other side of the ledger. Russia says it hits targets used by Ukraine's military, though the UN noted on Friday that the first two weeks of October were the deadliest for Ukrainian civilians in years. Russian strikes on energy sites have left hundreds of thousands without power, and Zelensky said tens of thousands still were. He said Russia launched nearly 2,700 drones, 1,700 aerial bombs and 66 missiles in the past week, most aimed at energy facilities.

The ceasefire was supposed to protect energy infrastructure. Both sides now seem to regard it as a list of targets, with winter as the deadline. Watch whether the diesel deal survives the next round of fires.
