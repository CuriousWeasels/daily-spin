China Drafts Export Rules for AI Models, With Express Lane for Giving Them Away

A draft list covers 23 AI technologies, bans self-writing attack malware, and sets thresholds for models, while letting open-weight releases clear automatically upon filing paperwork.

The wire said: China plans to tighten export controls on open-weight models and 23 key AI technologies · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 17, 2026 01:44 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

China's commerce and science ministries have released a draft update to the country's catalog of banned and restricted export technologies, adding 23 AI areas including frontier models, agents, compute clusters and data processing. The US already restricts exports of certain model weights.

Why it matters

Companies and governments that build products on Chinese open-weight models now face the prospect that future releases could be restricted, which matters most for Europe's plans to rely on locally hosted open models.

China's commerce and science ministries have published a draft update to the country's list of banned and restricted export technologies, according to The Decoder. The new entries cover 23 AI areas spanning large models, agents, compute clusters and data processing. It is a regulation that says, in effect, here is what you may not export, and also here is how to export it faster.

The hardest line falls on AI tools that can write or alter malware on their own to attack critical infrastructure, the military or government agencies. Those face a total export ban. Certain tools for finding software vulnerabilities escape the ban but will face new export limits. Translation: a tool that finds the unlocked door is regulated; a tool that walks in and rearranges the furniture is prohibited.

Large models are caught in two ways. The first applies to models trained on China's "important data," as defined under its Data Security Law, that offer services based on it. Lawfully public data is exempt, and up to 0.1 percent is allowed if it was cleaned beforehand, never appears in outputs and doesn't substantially boost the model's R&D capacity for core technology in that field. That is a de minimis rule, which is regulatory for a pinch of salt is fine, provided no one can taste it.

The second trigger covers the core technology behind large models: weights, checkpoints, source code, architecture, and training or inference methods. Controls apply once a model reaches 10^26 FLOPs of training compute, at least 2 trillion parameters, or a place in the global top 10. Translation: FLOPs are a count of arithmetic operations, so this is a threshold on how much math the model did while learning. Export officials will judge top-10 status from public rankings by major domestic and foreign evaluators, looking at overall performance, specific tasks, safety or global usage. This means a model's export status could depend partly on a leaderboard it does not control.

Then comes the exception that makes the rule interesting. Publishing restricted AI tech and weights as open source qualifies for what the draft calls automatic licensing, with eligible exports cleared the moment paperwork is filed. Anything already public through open-source releases or research papers is exempt altogether. The same fast track covers Chinese firms running their own data centers abroad, taping out chips overseas, doing research with foreign partners or helping set international standards. Using restricted models through APIs, apps, websites, SaaS tools or lightweight local versions stays outside the controls, while installing them on local servers abroad requires a standard license.

Dean W. Ball, head of strategic futures at OpenAI, wrote that the move does not mean China is shutting down open-weight models, but that it establishes the government's "optionality to shut down future open-weight models at will." He called the framework a "mirror image" of US model-weight export controls. In plain terms, the door is open, and Beijing has now installed a lock and kept the key.

The Decoder expects US companies that depend on open-weight AI to lean further toward domestic models. Europe looks more exposed. Its sovereignty strategy rests on running open-weight models in local clouds, which works less well if the best ones come from the US or China and can be cut off by export controls. Beijing also pledged easier exports for Global South capacity building and joint rulemaking within the World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organization, a bloc it launched to counter Western AI bodies. Until final rules take effect, companies must register affected exports through the commerce ministry's portal. The draft, in short, is a freedom-of-choice policy in which one party holds the choice.
