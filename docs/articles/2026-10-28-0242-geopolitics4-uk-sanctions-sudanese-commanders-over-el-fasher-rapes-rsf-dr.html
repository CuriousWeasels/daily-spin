Britain Sanctions 15 Sudanese Commanders a Year After El Fasher, Pending Further Anniversaries

The UK has imposed travel bans and asset freezes on commanders from both the RSF and the army, as drone strikes knock out power across eastern Sudan.

The wire said: UK sanctions Sudanese military leaders implicated in El Fasher ‘campaign of rape’ · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 28, 2026 02:42 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Sudan's army and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces (RSF), once allies, have been at war since April 2023. The RSF took the city of El Fasher after an 18-month siege, and about a year later the conflict has become largely a drone war.

Why it matters

Sanctions on commanders signal that governments are tracking atrocities, but they do little to restore electricity, food or medicine for millions of civilians. The war's shift to drones means infrastructure, and the people who depend on it, are now a primary target.

On the first anniversary of the fall of El Fasher, the British government announced sanctions on 15 commanders from both the RSF and the Sudanese Armed Forces, whom it suspects of sexual violence during the siege, according to The Guardian. The penalties are a travel ban and an asset freeze. Translation: the men may not visit Britain, and any money they have in Britain is now stuck there.

The Foreign Office said it hoped the action would ensure that "the horrors that took place at El Fasher will be known to the world." The figures suggest the world has had some time to find out. Up to 10,000 men, women and children are believed to have been killed over two days, described by The Guardian as the fastest and largest massacre of civilians this century. Another 40,000 people remain unaccounted for.

The killings came at the end of an 18-month starvation siege by the RSF, which was allegedly backed by the United Arab Emirates. The UAE continues to deny supporting the RSF. The sanctions list does not, as reported, include any states, which is a convenient way to keep a diplomatic relationship and a moral position in the same drawer.

Among those named are Hussein Abu Jalak, an RSF colonel linked to atrocities during the siege, and Al-Nour Ahmed Adam, known as "Al-Qubba," who was identified as playing a prominent role in the attack. Fewer than 70,000 people are believed to remain in a city that had more than 1 million before the war. Testimony from inside describes routine detention, torture and unlawful killings.

The Guardian also noted that its own investigation earlier this year found internal US and UK warnings were sidelined before the assault, and that action that might have saved lives was not taken. Sanctions after the fact are therefore a form of accountability that arrives on a schedule of about one year, which is roughly how long it takes for a warning to become a press release.

Meanwhile the war continues by other means. The RSF launched a coordinated drone attack on five power stations, two each in Al-Jazira and Gedaref states and one in White Nile state, a government official told The Guardian on condition of anonymity. All three states are army-controlled. The United Nations has repeatedly warned that such attacks threaten health and services in a country already facing the world's largest hunger crisis.

The consequences are domestic and specific. Babiker Youssef, a resident of Al-Fao in Gedaref, said his diabetic son's insulin must be kept in a fridge and that he feared he could not replace it if it spoiled. Ibrahim Mohammed, a grocer, said he must either lose his stock or sell it at any price, which is still a loss. Farmer Abdelhafez Daafallah said a station repaired three months ago, giving hope that irrigation pumps would run again, had been hit.

The wider numbers are worse. A UN tally found drone strikes killed more than 300 people in the first three weeks of October. Aid workers estimate the war has killed more than 200,000, 14 million have fled their homes, most hospitals have closed, and at least 8 million children are out of school. The currency has lost more than 25% of its value in recent weeks.

The sanctions hit commanders on both sides, which at least has the virtue of consistency. Whether a travel ban matters to men whose main mode of transport is a drone war is a question the Foreign Office did not address. Watch for whether any later round names the people who supply the money and the weapons, rather than only those who use them.
