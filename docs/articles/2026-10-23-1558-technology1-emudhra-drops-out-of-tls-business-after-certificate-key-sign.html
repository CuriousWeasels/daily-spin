Certificate Authority With Private Key of 4 Discovers TLS Is Not Its Core Business

India's eMudhra will exit the web certificate market after researchers found eight of its intermediate credentials used keys small enough to guess in milliseconds.

The wire said: Disgraced eMudhra drops out of TLS business after using cert private key of 4 · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 23, 2026 15:58 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

eMudhra, an India-based certificate authority, issued eight intermediate TLS credentials whose private keys were trivially weak, according to Ars Technica. Researchers disclosed the problem earlier this week, and a Mozilla compliance bug was opened to track it.

Why it matters

Browsers and many other devices trust certificate authorities to vouch for websites, so a broken key could let an attacker impersonate nearly any site or mail server. The episode shows how much of internet security rests on a few organizations doing basic math correctly.

eMudhra, an India-based certificate authority, said it is withdrawing from the global TLS business days after researchers revealed that eight of its intermediate certificates used private keys so weak that anyone could derive them, Ars Technica reported. One of the keys was 4. Not 4 bits, not 4 million digits. Just 4.

Some background. A certificate authority is the organization your browser trusts to say that a website is who it claims to be. Translation: it is the notary of the internet, and your browser accepts its stamp without asking follow-up questions. An intermediate certificate is a credential that lets the authority delegate that stamping power, which is why a broken one is a problem for everyone who trusts the stamp.

The keys in question were meant to be NIST P-384 elliptic curve keys. Ars Technica says these should carry roughly 384 bits of entropy. Translation: entropy is a measure of unpredictability, and 384 bits means an attacker faces a number with 116 digits of possibilities. The defective keys had between 3 and 8 bits. The strongest had a maximum of 256 possibilities, which a laptop can work through before the page finishes loading. The keys were 4, 56, 66, 115, 155, 156, 230 and 237, a list that reads less like cryptography than like a locker combination.

According to Ars Technica, anyone who cracked a key, a task measured in milliseconds, could mint certificates trusted by all major browsers. The same trust extends to email servers, IoT devices, Docker containers, forgotten Linux machines, and unmaintained command-line tools that ship their own copy of the trusted roots. Many of those will never receive an update, so the exposure outlasts the apology.

On Thursday, eMudhra chairman V. Srinivasan gave the reasons for leaving. TLS generates just 1 percent of the company's revenue. Compliance costs and complexity are rising. Free certificate providers have reduced returns. All of this is plausible, and all of it was presumably also true last week, when the company was still issuing certificates.

The company also said that the level of social media amplification around the incident has brought scrutiny out of proportion to SSL/TLS's place in our business. Translation: people noticed. The statement frames the problem as one of attention, not of keys small enough to fit on a sticky note. The scrutiny does appear to be proportionate to the discovery that a trusted authority issued credentials a spreadsheet could break.

The Hacker News thread points to a Mozilla compliance bug, opened about five days earlier, titled as a private key compromise of eMudhra intermediate certificates. The tracker shows it assigned, marked P1, and still awaiting information from several people. The thread's headline says eMudhra ignored a Mozilla investigation deadline, though the tracker excerpt does not show that. Mozilla's root program governs which authorities Firefox trusts, so unanswered questions there tend to matter.

The practical test now is whether browsers and operating systems distrust the affected credentials, and how quickly. Leaving the TLS business is a commercial decision. Removing the trust is a technical one, and it is not up to eMudhra. Until then, the number 4 remains, in an unusual sense, a very secure key, because it has been thoroughly published.
