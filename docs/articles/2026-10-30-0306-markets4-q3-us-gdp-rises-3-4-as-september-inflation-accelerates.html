Economy Grows 3.4% as Inflation Also Grows, Fed Promises to Keep Looking at Both

Third-quarter GDP beat forecasts and jobless claims sat near 1960s lows, but September prices rose 0.5% and the Fed has chosen to study the matter further.

The wire said: Third-quarter U.S. GDP increases a better-than-expected 3.4% as September inflation accelerates · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 30, 2026 03:06 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The Commerce Department reported Thursday that U.S. GDP grew at a 3.4% annualized pace in the third quarter, while the Fed's preferred inflation gauge hit 3.6% for the year. The Fed held rates at 3.75%-4% the day before.

Why it matters

Strong growth with inflation well above the Fed's 2% target makes rate cuts harder to justify, and borrowing costs may stay high for longer. Energy-driven price increases hit household budgets directly, whatever the headline growth number says.

The U.S. economy grew at a seasonally adjusted annualized rate of 3.4% in the third quarter, beating the 3.1% forecast and the 2.2% of the second quarter, the Commerce Department reported Thursday, according to CNBC. The same morning brought news that inflation had accelerated in September. The economy, in other words, is doing better and costing more, which is a combination most households recognize from the grocery store.

The personal consumption expenditures price index, the Fed's favored inflation gauge, rose 0.5% in September and 3.6% from a year earlier. Translation: prices are climbing at a rate the central bank describes as a target overshoot, a phrase that means 'we said 2%, and this is not that.' Core PCE, which strips out food and energy because the Fed assumes you can live without both, rose 0.3% for the month and 3.1% for the year. Both readings matched forecasts.

The culprit was energy. Gasoline and other energy goods rose 9.2% in the month, and energy goods and services overall rose 5.4%. Economists, CNBC reported, worry the increase could spread to other parts of the economy. Services excluding energy and housing rose 0.4%, which suggests the worry is not purely theoretical. Brent crude, the global oil benchmark, was most recently at $123.10, which suggests relief is not imminent.

The growth itself leaned heavily on the artificial intelligence build-out. Gross private domestic investment jumped 19.4%, with equipment spending up 20.9% and intellectual property products up 8.4%. Translation: companies are buying a great deal of hardware and software to prepare for a future in which they will need less of other things. Consumer spending rose 3.8%, the same pace as the prior quarter, and real sales to private domestic purchasers, which the Fed treats as a gauge of underlying demand, rose 4.9%.

The labor market offered no argument. Initial jobless claims fell to 194,000 for the week ended Oct. 24, below the 200,000 forecast and near levels last seen in the late 1960s. Unemployment stood at 4.2% as of the latest official reading. Few people are being laid off, which means few people are available to be talked into accepting lower inflation.

There was some mild good news. On a quarterly basis, PCE prices rose 2.1% overall and 2.5% for core, down from 5% and 3.3% in the second quarter. Chris Rupkey of Fwdbonds said growth was 'powering ahead' despite geopolitical risks and higher energy costs. The quarter looked better than September, which is a bit like praising a restaurant's average meal after a bad dessert.

The Fed, for its part, held its benchmark rate at 3.75%-4% on Wednesday. Chairman Kevin Warsh said the period ahead is 'information rich' and that he and colleagues are keen to take notice of it. Thursday's data was a great deal of information, and most of it points toward the Fed having more work to do. Markets reacted accordingly: stock futures fell and Treasury yields declined, though the 10-year yield at 5.05% is hardly a bargain.

What to watch next is whether energy costs keep leaking into everything else. If they do, the Fed's careful evaluation of incoming data will end with a conclusion it could probably have drafted this week.
