Spain's Rail Control Centre Tells Trains to Resume, Minister Calls It 'Inexplicable'

At least 68 people died when a Renfe train hit a stopped Iryo train south of Madrid, in Spain's second major rail disaster this year.

The wire said: High-speed train crash kills at least 68 people in Spain · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 29, 2026 03:01 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

On Wednesday morning, a Renfe high-speed train from Madrid to Seville struck the back of a stopped Iryo train near Mora and Orgaz, about 140km south of the capital. Spain's transport minister blamed a computer failure followed by human error.

Why it matters

Spain markets its high-speed network as a point of national pride, and two fatal crashes on the same line in one year raise questions about whether the safety systems and the people backing them up can be trusted. Election campaigns are under way, so the answers will carry political weight.

A high-speed train crash in central Spain killed at least 68 people and injured dozens more on Wednesday, the BBC reported. A Renfe train carrying 397 passengers ran into the back of a stationary Iryo train carrying 147, shortly after 09:00 local time. Several carriages fell down an embankment. It is Spain's deadliest train disaster since 2013, when 80 people died near Santiago de Compostela.

The sequence matters, because it is the whole story. According to Transport Minister Óscar Puente, an automatic signalling failure had forced trains on the affected stretch to stop. Translation: the computer that normally keeps trains apart stopped working, so the system fell back on people. A 24-hour control centre in Madrid takes over in such cases, and Puente said it made an "inexplicable" decision to tell the trains to move again, with no speed restrictions.

BBC timings put numbers on the gap. Six trains were told to stop at about 08:30. The Renfe train was ordered to proceed at 08:36, and roughly 17 minutes later it hit the one ahead. The backup plan for a computer failure, in other words, was a human instruction, and it pointed the wrong way.

Passengers described the aftermath in terms that need no commentary from us. Survivors told Reuters, the BBC and Spanish outlets of being thrown into the air, of people trapped between seats, and of passengers breaking windows to get out. A piece of track went through the roof of one carriage. Spain's Civil Guard said it would need DNA samples from relatives to identify some victims.

Anger came quickly, and some of it was practical. One passenger on the stopped train asked how it could be halted for five or ten minutes without the following train being told. It is a reasonable question, and it is the one the investigation will have to answer.

The politics arrived on schedule. Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez called two serious crashes in one year "unacceptable" and promised to find out whether there was negligence. Opposition leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo said any failures in the security system would be addressed "with the utmost firmness." Most parties cancelled campaign events. Vox did not, saying it would not join attempts to "use mourning to silence criticism" of the rail infrastructure.

The minister's explanation is also contested. The CSIF union demanded that Puente resign, accusing him of scapegoating a traffic controller by blaming human error instead of taking responsibility. Blaming a person is simple, but a system that lets one instruction cause a collision may be the bigger problem. Whether the fault lies with the controller or the design is what investigators will weigh.

The crash comes after the Adamuz disaster near Córdoba in January, which killed 46 people on the same high-speed line, further south. Spain now has two inquiries to run and one rail line to explain. Watch for whether the findings point to the control room or to the system that left a single order as the last safeguard.
