Mistral Releases Weights of 1-Trillion-Parameter 'Le Chonk,' Asks Everyone to Please Use It Responsibly

The French AI lab says its open-weight Mistral Large 4 leads open models in coding, while also admitting its own guardrails may let someone conduct real attacks.

The wire said: We're releasing the weights of Mistral Large 4 · Hacker News

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 29, 2026 17:39 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Mistral, a Paris-based AI company, put Mistral Large 4 into public preview earlier this month. It has now published the model's weights, the numerical settings that make it work, so anyone can download and run it.

Why it matters

Open weights let companies and governments run a top-tier coding model on their own servers rather than renting one, which is the whole pitch of 'sovereign AI.' The same openness means safety guardrails are harder to enforce once the model is out.

Mistral announced it has released the weights of Mistral Large 4, nicknamed Le Chonk, a 1-trillion-parameter model that handles text and images. The company says three additional weeks of reinforcement learning since the preview improved its coding and cybersecurity performance. Translation: reinforcement learning is a training method in which the model is scored on its attempts and nudged toward the ones that score well, a bit like a school where only the exam matters.

The headline claim is coding. Mistral says the model leads open-weight rivals on Cognition's private FrontierCode 2.0, with a score of 19.7%, and on Terminal-Bench 4.0, where it rose 13 percentage points to 41%. On the Artificial Analysis Coding Agent Index it scored 48%, effectively tied with GLM-5.3, the leading open-weight model, while using 47% fewer tokens per task. Translation: tokens are the chunks of text a model reads and writes, and you pay by the chunk, so the same answer in fewer chunks is a smaller bill.

That is the economic argument. A 19.7% score on a test of whether a maintainer would merge the AI's change is the best among open models, according to Mistral, which also means about four in five attempts would not be merged. Mistral also says human raters in Surge Code's blind comparisons preferred it to GLM-5.3, Kimi K3 and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, ranking behind only Claude Opus 5.5 among the models evaluated. All of these figures come from Mistral's own announcement.

The model now has three reasoning modes: low, medium and high, with high as the default. Low replaces the previous 'None' mode, which is a rare case of a product feature being renamed to sound more like effort. The company says the modes let developers trade accuracy for cost.

Then comes security, where the release gets more interesting. Mistral says the model scores 51% on the Artificial Analysis Cyber Index, which tests finding, reproducing and patching software vulnerabilities, and refuses 96.6% of malicious cyber requests on average, the highest it measures among open models. In the same post, it says that after monitoring live traffic it sees potential for uplift to conduct real attacks, and is modifying its guardrails accordingly. Translation: the lock works well, and the company is already planning a better lock.

The caveat is structural. Guardrails live in the model's behavior, and once weights are public, anyone with the hardware can in principle retrain them away. Mistral's post does not address this directly. It does say a specialized security variant is available only to vetted enterprise partners such as Orange Cyberdefense, under contractual safeguards, which is a more conventional way of controlling access than hoping for the best.

The commercial pitch is sovereignty. Mistral says the model was trained from scratch on European infrastructure, and La Banque Postale's executive vice president Zakaria Moursli said technological sovereignty is 'not optional' for a public and citizen bank. Mistral also reports gains on web browsing (84.5% on BrowseComp), finance spreadsheet work (73.3% on Finch) and resistance to agent attacks (97.1%, up from 93.3%).

Independent verification is the thing to watch. Nearly every number here is the vendor reporting a benchmark, some on private tests, and the real review will come when thousands of developers download a trillion parameters and see what they actually do.
