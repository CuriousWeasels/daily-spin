ECB Holds Rates at 2.5% After Debating Whether Not Raising Them Was Brave Enough

The central bank paused despite 3.8% euro zone inflation, while markets quietly bet on a December hike and Christine Lagarde announced her departure the same day.

The wire said: European Central Bank keeps key interest rate on hold at 2.5% · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 29, 2026 17:39 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The ECB raised its deposit rate by 25 basis points in September, and earlier hiked in June for the first time since 2023, after energy prices jumped following the outbreak of the U.S.-Israeli war with Iran in February. Euro zone inflation has since climbed to its highest level in over three years.

Why it matters

Higher borrowing costs affect mortgages, business loans and government debt across the euro area, while energy-driven inflation squeezes household budgets. A pause now, followed by a possible hike in December, means rate relief is not close.

The European Central Bank voted on Thursday to keep its key interest rate, the deposit facility, at 2.5%, which is what markets expected, according to CNBC. This came even though euro zone inflation hit 3.8% last month, the highest since September 2023. Holding rates while inflation rises is a decision, technically, and the ECB would like it understood as one.

Energy prices are the culprit. Energy price inflation reached 18.8%, which CNBC attributed to continued turbulence in the Middle East. Translation: the thing the ECB controls is interest rates, the thing driving prices is oil and gas, and the two are connected mainly by hope.

Christine Lagarde said the Governing Council debated whether to hike, then unanimously decided to hold. She said that given accumulating uncertainty and underlying inflation that has stayed broadly unchanged, a measured approach remained appropriate. Translation: they looked at the hike, considered it, and put it back on the shelf, but did not leave the shop.

Lagarde also said the ECB is not seeing second-round effects, meaning wages and other prices are not yet chasing energy costs upward. This is the central banker's preferred state of affairs, because it lets them call high inflation temporary without technically being wrong yet. The Governing Council statement said underlying inflation and wages were so far broadly stable.

Long-term borrowing costs are doing some of the ECB's work for it. Lagarde said the average 10-year sovereign yield rose by around 35 basis points since the last meeting. Translation: governments now pay noticeably more to borrow for a decade, which tightens conditions without anyone having to vote on it. She attributed the global rise partly to inflation and rate expectations and partly to debt issuance by some of the hyperscalers, the large tech companies now competing with governments for bond buyers. She also said the ECB would keep watching rising French borrowing costs and fragmentation, which is central-bank language for countries being charged different prices for the same currency.

The growth picture is mixed. The Governing Council said the economy kept expanding in the third quarter, with services recovering, manufacturing and exports lifted by AI-related investment, and government spending on infrastructure and defense supporting activity. Energy costs and higher financing costs are expected to weigh on consumer confidence.

Markets did not take the pause as a pledge of calm. LSEG data showed about a 67% probability of a December hike. Deutsche Bank's Mark Wall said predicting policy is becoming more challenging, and expects one more hike to 2.75% while Lagarde is still president. UBS's Dean Turner called the decision a hawkish pause rather than the end of tightening. Translation: the ECB is stopping, but with its coat on.

Lagarde also announced earlier on Thursday that she will leave as ECB president, according to CNBC, which did not give details in the text provided. Wall said the institution is bigger than the person, but acknowledged uncertainty over how her exit might affect decisions next year.

Investing.com reported that European shares climbed to near two-month highs after the decision, apparently reading a hold as good news. The next meeting in December will show whether investors correctly read a central bank that has said it is watching closely, which it always is.
