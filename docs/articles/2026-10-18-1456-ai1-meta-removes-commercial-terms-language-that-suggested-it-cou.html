Meta Deletes Clause Letting It Scrape Publishers, Says Clause Did Nothing

After legal experts and Nieman Lab objected, Meta removed commercial-terms language that appeared to allow crawling of business accounts' linked websites for AI training, while insisting nothing had changed.

The wire said: Meta may use sweeping new commercial TOS to avoid millions in AI licensing fees · Ars Technica AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 18, 2026 14:56 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Meta announced an update to its commercial terms, due October 30, that let it crawl websites linked to business accounts and use the content to "train" its products. Legal experts read it as a way to get AI training material without paying licensing fees, and Meta has now removed the language.

Why it matters

Publishers and other businesses depend on Meta accounts, so terms that quietly grant content rights could have undercut their leverage in AI licensing talks. The episode shows that objecting loudly to fine print can still work.

Meta has removed language from its commercial terms that experts said could have allowed it to scrape websites tied to business accounts and use the material to train AI, The Verge and TechCrunch reported. The company says the language never changed anything. This is a common arrangement in corporate communications: the clause was harmless, and also it has been deleted.

The original text, which Ars Technica said was to take effect on October 30, authorized Meta to use automated software, including "web scrapers, bots, robots, spiders, crawlers, and user agents," to access content on domains associated with a business's products or services. It added that Meta could use that content to "provide, develop, improve, research, train, and maintain the integrity of" its products. Translation: a list of every synonym for "bot," followed by a verb, "train," doing the work that the phrase "artificial intelligence" was conspicuously not asked to do. Ars Technica noted that AI was never mentioned by name.

Ars Technica reported that the terms did not allow businesses to opt out, and suggested those who objected should stop using Meta services. Nieman Lab pointed out that virtually every news organization has at least one Meta business account, itself included. That matters because news publishers are in a fight over whether AI training on their articles is fair use, and some could plausibly charge millions in licensing fees. A clause that grants access as a condition of having a Facebook page would be a rather efficient way to skip that invoice.

Cornell law professor James Grimmelmann was among those who, according to The Verge, read the language as an attempt to get free training material and to override the instructions in a site's robots.txt file. Translation: robots.txt is the polite sign a website posts asking crawlers to stay out. It is a courtesy rather than a lock, and the clause appeared to make Meta's reading of the sign optional.

Meta first pushed back. Spokespeople Andy Stone and Daniel Roberts said, "There is no change to how Meta uses web crawlers; any suggestion to the contrary is false." The company then removed the language anyway. Roberts said the consolidated crawling terms "only applied to our ads and business products," and that content owners can still use robots.txt to signal their preferences. He added that the updates "don't give us new rights that supersede those preferences."

The sequence is worth noting: the text was said to be meaningless, then judged meaningful enough to delete. TechCrunch noted that Meta won a high-profile copyright lawsuit last year, though other cases against AI companies, Meta included, continue. Courts are still deciding what AI training is allowed to take, so every contract that might answer the question in advance gets read closely.

Publishers may want to keep checking the terms. Companies that update fine print on a Thursday and clarify it by the weekend have taught readers when to read it.
