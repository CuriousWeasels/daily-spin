U.S. Posts Near-$2 Trillion Deficit, Its 25th Straight Year of Spending More Than It Has

The Treasury's $1.992 trillion shortfall is up 12% from last year, and interest on the debt alone now tops $1 trillion.

The wire said: U.S. budget deficit swells to almost $2 trillion for fiscal 2026, up 12% in a single year · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 18, 2026 01:40 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The federal government's fiscal year ended September 30, and Treasury has released the final tally. Long-term bond yields have climbed above 5% this year, as the war with Iran, heavy borrowing and AI-related corporate debt compete for investors' money.

Why it matters

Higher Treasury yields feed directly into mortgage rates, which are around 7.5%, and into the government's own borrowing costs. With divided government expected after the midterms, a fix looks years away.

The United States ended fiscal 2026 with a budget deficit of $1.992 trillion, the Treasury Department reported Friday. That is about 12% above last year's $1.775 trillion, and the third-largest in American history, behind only the two pandemic stimulus years of 2020 and 2021. According to CNBC, this is the 25th consecutive year in the red. The last surplus came in 2001, a year many staffers now in government were still learning to read.

Translation: a deficit is the gap between what the government collects and what it spends in a year. Treasury also reported record receipts and record outlays, which is a way of saying the government has never taken in so much money and has still managed to fall this far short of it.

The biggest line items are familiar. Social Security cost nearly $1.7 trillion, Medicare almost $1.1 trillion, and net interest, meaning what Treasury pays on its debt minus what it receives, topped $1 trillion for the first time in the sources' accounting, up from $970 billion. Gross interest on the national debt exceeded $1.3 trillion. The national debt passed $40 trillion earlier this year and has more than doubled in a decade. The government is now spending more on the cost of having borrowed money than on most things it actually bought with it.

On the revenue side, individual income taxes brought in more than $2.8 trillion, up about 7%. Corporate income tax fell by roughly $70 billion to $381 billion, which CNBC attributes to the tax cuts in last year's One Big Beautiful Bill act. Tariffs set a record for gross customs duties, but net customs revenue took a hit because the Supreme Court ruled many of the tariffs unconstitutional, forcing refunds of billions of dollars to retailers. The White House has kept raising levies anyway. A revenue plan that must be partly paid back is a novel concept.

The administration's stated answer is growth. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said in Dallas last month: "we have to grow our way out of it." CNBC's analysis cites the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget, which calculates that balancing the budget on growth alone would take a decade of 7.2% annual growth. Second-quarter GDP was recently revised up to 2.2% at an annualized rate. The distance between 2.2% and 7.2% is what economists call a gap and what everyone else calls a hope.

Bessent has also argued for a deficit below 4% of GDP by the end of Trump's term. The current deficit is about 6.2% of GDP, per CNBC's estimate. The analysis calls hitting the target almost impossible.

Markets are already charging for the habit. The 10-year Treasury yield stood at 5.1% as of October 15, up 114 basis points since the end of February. Translation: a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so the cost of lending to Washington for a decade has risen by about 1.14 points. CNBC ties the rise to the war with Iran that began Feb. 28, higher oil and diesel prices, heavy Treasury issuance, and companies borrowing hundreds of billions to build AI infrastructure. Brent crude was at $126.55 in the latest data. Mortgage rates track the 10-year, and Redfin reported pending home sales fell 4% in September, the biggest monthly drop in three years.

The Congressional Budget Office estimates that if rates run a half-point above its forecast, deficits over ten years grow by $1.9 trillion. A 1.5-point overshoot adds $6 trillion. The CBO expects the 10-year to average 4.3%, against 5.1% today.

The political outlook is not helping. Republicans are widely forecast to lose at least the House in the Nov. 3 midterms. Rep. Hakeem Jeffries, the likely next Democratic speaker, wants to reverse $1 trillion in healthcare cuts enacted last year. Trump, meanwhile, pledged $5,000 checks to every adult citizen if Republicans swept the election. CNBC notes the parties are far apart and mutually hostile.

The analysis does offer some comfort: higher yields also attract buyers to Treasuries, and expensive borrowing is not the same as a crisis. Its conclusion is that economics alone cannot solve this, and the politics need fixing too. Both are currently scheduled for after the election, then after the next one.
