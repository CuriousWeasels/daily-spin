IMF Raises Growth Forecast, Credits AI Boom, Warns Everything Else Is Held Together With Tape

The fund now sees 3.1% global growth in 2026, but says the buffers that absorbed the Hormuz shock are running out and AI enthusiasm is doing a lot of the lifting.

The wire said: IMF sees milder global slowdown as AI boom offsets war hit — warns resilience is nearing limits · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 15, 2026 14:38 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The IMF released its latest World Economic Outlook at its annual meetings in Bangkok, amid a Middle East war that has disrupted the Strait of Hormuz and pushed up energy costs. The fund nudged global growth up from July's forecast, mostly because of AI investment.

Why it matters

The cushion keeping energy prices and markets calm is thinning, while borrowing costs sit at multidecade highs. If AI spending disappoints, the IMF says U.S. stocks could fall 20%, which would reach well beyond people who own AI shares.

The International Monetary Fund raised its 2026 global growth forecast to 3.1% from 3% in July, according to CNBC, which is still below last year's 3.5%. The fund kept its 2027 forecast at 3.4%. It credited the improvement to AI-related investment, and then spent the remaining pages explaining why that is not entirely reassuring.

The headline message from Silvana Tenreyro, the IMF's economic counsellor, was that the global economy has been resilient but that resilience is "reaching its limits." Translation: the world has been paying for this war with savings, and the account balance is visible from here.

The savings in question are buffers. Governments diversified fuel sources and tapped strategic reserves, which the IMF says contained the turmoil from the war. But global oil reserves are being drawn down by about 2.7 million barrels a day, close to levels that could trigger sharp price moves. The latest data show Brent crude at $125.44, which suggests the buffer is working in the sense that it could be worse.

The fund now expects the Strait of Hormuz to reopen in March next year and return to its pre-war state by October. In July it had assumed a reopening by mid-July. Gulf crude exports averaged 88% of pre-conflict levels in September. Inflation is forecast at 4.7% this year and 4.1% in 2027, up from 3.9% previously. The fund also puts the odds of global inflation topping 5% in 2027 at about 35%, up from 30% in April.

Growth is also lopsided. Thirty-two economies now account for 95% of global growth, down from 54 in April. Taiwan is forecast to grow 10.7% and South Korea 3.3%, both on chip demand. India leads the major economies at 7.0%, and the U.S. is at 2.3%. The euro zone is expected to manage 1.1%, and the Middle East and Central Asia region is projected to contract 0.3%, with Bahrain, Iraq, Kuwait and Qatar facing double-digit declines before double-digit rebounds in 2027. China is forecast at 4.5%. In the IMF's words, growth is being "reallocated" according to exposure to the war and to AI demand. Translation: your forecast depends on whether you sell energy-hungry things or buy them.

The AI boom, meanwhile, is both the rescue and the risk. The IMF called it a "large, front-loaded demand shock": money pouring into chips, data centers and power generation before productivity gains have shown up. It also said calm financial markets may partly reflect "AI-related exuberance." If profit and productivity hopes are reassessed and the gains fail to appear, the fund's scenario has U.S. equities down 20% and global growth 1.1% below baseline by 2028. The S&P 500 stood at 7718.94 on Oct. 14, so there is a considerable distance to fall.

Then there is the bill. Advanced-economy benchmark yields are at multidecade highs, and tech companies borrowing to fund AI are adding to the pressure. The 10-year Treasury yield was 5.21% on Oct. 13. Translation: governments and companies are competing for the same borrowed money, and lenders have noticed. The IMF projects advanced-economy public debt reaching 115% of GDP by 2031, up from 108% in 2025.

Its prescription is predictable and not cheap. Governments should rebuild fiscal space, keep household energy support "temporary, targeted, and nondistortionary," and keep monetary policy "sufficiently restrictive." Central bank independence is critical, Tenreyro said, as governments face financing pressure. Asking governments to spend less during a war, an energy shock and a defense buildup is the sort of advice that is easy to endorse and hard to attend.

What to watch: the oil drawdown, the reopening date for Hormuz, and whether anyone in the AI supply chain begins to mention profits.
