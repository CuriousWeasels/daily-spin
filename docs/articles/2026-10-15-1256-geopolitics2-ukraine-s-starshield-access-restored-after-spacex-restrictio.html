Ukraine's Drones Regain Satellite Access After Brief Lesson in Who Owns the Sky

Kyiv says Starshield service is restored after reports of speed limits on drone terminals, a reminder that a war can depend on someone else's settings menu.

The wire said: Ukraine says its Starshield access is back (NPR World)

By The Daily Spin Desk · Geopolitics Oct 15, 2026 12:56 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Ukraine uses SpaceX's Starlink and its military-grade Starshield variant to connect drones and other battlefield systems. This week Ukrainian officials said service for fast-moving drones was throttled, then said it was back.

Why it matters

A country fighting a war on satellite links it does not own can have its drone operations changed by a decision made in Washington or at a private company. That dependence now sits alongside fears about reduced US intelligence-sharing, which affects how quickly civilians are warned of missile attacks.

Ukraine said on Thursday that its access to SpaceX's Starshield has been restored, according to NPR. Starshield is the system the country uses for its offensive drone attacks on Russia. The announcement followed reports that access had been temporarily restricted, which is the polite way to describe a battlefield tool briefly behaving like a free trial.

The sequence, as reported by Al Jazeera, went like this. On Wednesday, Ukraine's drone czar, Serhiy Beskrestnov, accused SpaceX of throttling Starlink terminals that are part of Starshield. He said the company abruptly and without warning limited service for terminals on drones flying faster than 160km/h (100mph). Translation: the drones that need to be fast were the ones that stopped working properly.

The limit hit interceptor drones, which are used to shoot down incoming Russian drones and are, by design, in a hurry. Presidential adviser Serhii Sternenko said the restriction followed a request from Trump. Elon Musk said the White House owns and controls the Starshield systems and that any speed limits are decided by Trump. So the owner of the company says the company does not decide, and the government says nothing in these sources. Everyone has a clear account of who is not responsible.

NPR's Michel Martin discussed the reports with Democratic Rep. Mike Quigley of Illinois, and Ukraine's later statement that access is back suggests the restriction was temporary. The sources do not explain why it was lifted. They also do not say who decided to lift it, which, given the previous paragraph, is a fair question.

None of this is new. Reuters reported that in September 2022 Musk secretly ordered engineers to switch off Starlink coverage in the Russia-occupied Kherson region, which thwarted a Ukrainian push to retake it. In 2023 he threatened to cut off Ukrainian military access unless the US defence department paid the costs. The Pentagon, Poland and other European allies later took over the bills. Ukraine has thus been a customer, a beneficiary and a hostage, sometimes in the same quarter.

The timing is awkward. Al Jazeera reported that Volodymyr Zelenskyy said this week that Ukraine's partners did not provide data from NATO's early-warning satellites. He did not name the United States, and Trump has denied Washington stopped sharing intelligence. The satellites detect ballistic missile launches by their thermal flames, and the warning can give Ukrainians up to 10 minutes. Ground radars, by contrast, can only see missiles moving at 6,000 to 12,000km/h once they are close. Overnight attacks this week killed at least 15 people, and the sirens sounded only seconds before or after the missiles hit.

Ukrainian observers offered a political explanation. Former deputy chief of the general staff Ihor Romanenko told Al Jazeera that Trump is pressuring Kyiv to gain an advantage in the election campaign. The context is an 'energy ceasefire' that Trump announced on Sunday, covering strikes on energy infrastructure. Kyiv said it was surprised, and Moscow declined to say whether it agreed while continuing to hit Ukrainian energy sites. Trump has denied the intelligence cut, and the sources provide no evidence linking it to the ceasefire push, so the motive remains the analysts' theory.

Analysts also disagreed on strategy. Nikolay Mitrokhin of Germany's Bremen University argued that Ukraine's focus on refineries is not producing the collapse some expected, since Russia rerouted fuel through Siberia, Belarus and Kazakhstan. He said strikes on data centres, bridges and drone factories are more effective. Ukrainian operator Andriy Pronin said his side had prepared for any turn of events, which is what you say when your supply chain includes someone else's mood.

The practical lesson is that the access is back, for now. What to watch is whether the speed limit returns, whether the intelligence data does, and whether anyone writes down who is allowed to change either.
