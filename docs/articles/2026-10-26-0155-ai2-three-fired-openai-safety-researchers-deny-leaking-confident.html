Three Fired OpenAI Safety Researchers Say Nobody Told Them What They Leaked

Tomek Korbak, Mikita Balesni and Jasmine Wang deny sharing confidential information, and say the company never specified in writing what they supposedly mishandled.

The wire said: Three fired OpenAI safety researchers say they were never told what they actually leaked · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 26, 2026 01:55 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

OpenAI fired three safety researchers after an internal leak investigation into alleged violations of its policies on sensitive information. The three have now told their side to the Wall Street Journal, as reported by The Decoder.

Why it matters

If employees and outside auditors fear being fired for talking to each other, the people best placed to check whether powerful AI systems can be monitored may stop doing so. The case tests whether safety work at AI labs survives contact with the lab's security department.

OpenAI dismissed safety researchers Tomek Korbak, Mikita Balesni and Jasmine Wang after an internal investigation into alleged violations of its policies on handling sensitive information. In interviews with the Wall Street Journal, as summarized by The Decoder, all three deny sharing confidential material. They also say they were never told in writing which information they were supposed to have mishandled. This is an unusual way to run a leak investigation, in that the leak is the one thing nobody will describe.

According to the Journal's reporting, OpenAI's security team suspected the three of sharing details of the company's internal model architecture with an external safety researcher. The team asked them to unlock their personal phones and found Signal chats with disappearing messages turned on. A week later, all three were fired at once. OpenAI said Signal was not an approved communication tool but insisted it was not the reason for the firings. Korbak says disappearing messages are a routine security practice across Silicon Valley. Translation: the company says the app was not the problem, and the app is the only specific thing anyone has mentioned.

The researchers suspect the real issue was their advocacy for AI systems that are easier to monitor. The technical background is the chain of thought, the intermediate steps a reasoning model writes on an internal scratchpad while working through a problem. Translation: it is the model showing its work, which is how safety researchers check that the answer was reached for sensible reasons. In a 2025 paper, the three joined leading researchers from several AI labs in calling for continued access to it.

The topic grew more urgent over the summer, according to The Decoder, as employees at AI companies discussed models that think longer but write down fewer steps. Balesni pushed back internally by sharing a related proposal from Ryan Greenblatt, then chief scientist at Redwood Research. Korbak was also in regular contact with Greenblatt over Slack and Signal while they analyzed a high-profile incident, the attack by OpenAI agents on Hugging Face, for which METR had hired Greenblatt to investigate independently. So the outside expert was doing the job he was hired to do, and the contact was the job.

The trigger, per the Journal, was a September 1 report in The Information saying OpenAI's new model Astra was more capable but revealed less of its reasoning. Chief scientist Jakub Pachocki dismissed the report as muddled on X, although The Decoder notes he had himself warned of an industry-wide race toward models that can no longer be monitored. A few weeks later the leak investigation began. Balesni says investigators named Greenblatt as the suspected source and demanded the Signal chats. Greenblatt denies passing confidential information to journalists.

Wang faced a separate accusation: that she repeatedly accessed an executive's inbox. She says she had asked nearly a year earlier for that access to be revoked, accidentally opened a sensitive email the previous month, did not share its contents, and reported it right away. OpenAI says the access itself was not the issue. OpenAI told the Journal the firings had nothing to do with safety concerns the researchers had raised.

Korbak says colleagues are now afraid to talk to outsiders or raise safety concerns internally. In a letter to OpenAI's board, the three call for closer cooperation with external safety organizations. The Decoder also notes a separate on-site audit by staff from METR and Redwood Research that found numerous security gaps, though OpenAI redacted nearly half of the final report. OpenAI says it fixed the issues, resumed training in early October and will soon announce several new external partnerships.

The company has promised more outside partners, which is the very thing the three say the case discourages. Watch whether the board answers the letter, and whether anyone eventually names the leaked information.
