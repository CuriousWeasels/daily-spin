North Korea Prepares to Restart Border Loudspeakers, Reviving Sixteen-Month Pause in Shouting

Kim Yo-jong says speakers will be ready within days along the whole border, while Seoul, which dismantled its own, weighs a 'proportional measure.'

The wire said: North Korea says it will resume propaganda broadcasts across border into South Korea · NPR World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 23, 2026 02:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

North and South Korea have long blasted propaganda across their border, but Seoul halted its broadcasts in June 2025 and removed the speakers that August. Ties have since worsened, including a September landmine blast that injured three South Korean soldiers.

Why it matters

Loudspeaker duels are low-tech, but they signal how far inter-Korean relations have deteriorated and raise the odds of friction along a border packed with soldiers, mines and wire. Seoul's response will show whether it stays conciliatory or returns to tit-for-tat.

North Korea says it will restart propaganda loudspeakers aimed at South Korea along the entire border, with Kim Yo-jong, the sister of leader Kim Jong-un, saying they would be ready within days. The Guardian reported that the statement was carried by the state news agency KCNA on Thursday, a day after she said the speakers would run along the whole frontier from some time in October. It is a modest technology to be the centerpiece of a diplomatic crisis, but the Korean peninsula has never been in a hurry to modernize its grievances.

Some background. Both Koreas have historically used giant loudspeakers to broadcast at each other across the border. The North in recent years has favored haunting bestial howls. The South has gone with K-pop and news criticizing Pyongyang. Translation: one side plays what it believes will unsettle the other, and the other side plays what the first side most fears, which is a catchy chorus.

If the broadcasts resume, they would be the first loudspeaker exchange across the border in 16 months. South Korean president Lee Jae Myung suspended Seoul's broadcasts just days into his presidency in June 2025 to ease tensions, and the military dismantled the speakers that August. That leaves South Korea in the awkward position of having disarmed first in a contest whose entire premise is volume.

Seoul's defence ministry said on Thursday that if the North goes ahead, 'naturally there would have to be a proportional measure,' while making clear no decision had been made. Translation: a response is coming, but nobody has yet found the receipt for the speakers. South Korea's joint chiefs of staff added that they had observed routine maintenance of the North's loudspeakers before Kim Yo-jong's statements, which suggests the announcement was at least preceded by a sound check.

Relations have been sliding since September, when a landmine blast south of the military demarcation line injured three South Korean soldiers, two of them seriously. Seoul and the UN command, which oversees the 1953 armistice that halted but did not end the Korean war, concluded it was a North Korean anti-personnel mine and an armistice violation. Pyongyang denied responsibility and called the incident a 'self-staged farce.'

Beneath the noise is construction. Since Kim Jong-un declared in December 2023 that the two Koreas were 'two states hostile to each other,' the North has laid mines, strung barbed wire and erected anti-tank barriers along the border. On Wednesday, Kim Yo-jong inspected fortification points with Pak Jong-chon, a marshal and adviser to Pyongyang's defence ministry, and said the border had been 'clearly blocked and turned into a fortress more thoroughly.' She added that Kim Jong-un had told her it 'should be a permanent line never to be crossed.'

Seoul says that line is, in practice, crossed rather often, by North Korean troops doing the construction work. South Korean soldiers have responded with warnings over loudspeakers and warning shots. North Korea calls those broadcasts and shots provocations by 'armed rogues,' which means that at least one set of loudspeakers is already in use and already offending someone.

What to watch next: whether Seoul decides a 'proportional measure' means reinstalling speakers it took down in August, and whether anyone on either side finds a way to be heard without being shot at.
