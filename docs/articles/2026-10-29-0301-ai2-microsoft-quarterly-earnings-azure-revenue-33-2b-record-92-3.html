Microsoft Finally Tells Investors What Azure Earns, Mostly Because Rivals Already Did

Azure brought in $33.2 billion last quarter, Microsoft's first dollar disclosure, as total revenue hit a record $92.3 billion and $10 billion came from OpenAI.

The wire said: Microsoft just shared Azure’s actual revenue in dollars: $33.2 billion · TechCrunch AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 29, 2026 03:01 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Microsoft had long described Azure's size only through growth percentages, while Amazon and Alphabet reported cloud revenue in dollars. In its latest earnings, Microsoft also reorganized its reporting segments and disclosed its OpenAI revenue in a regulatory filing.

Why it matters

Investors can now compare the three big cloud providers on the same terms, and see how much of Microsoft's AI boom rests on one customer that is gradually becoming a competitor. The $45.2 billion quarterly spending bill shows what it costs to keep that business running.

Microsoft said on Wednesday that Azure, its cloud computing service, brought in $33.2 billion in the July-September quarter, up 48% from a year earlier, according to TechCrunch. It is the first time the company has put a dollar figure on the business. Until now, investors got a growth percentage and the occasional vague milestone, a bit like asking a friend how much he earns and being told he is doing better than last year.

Translation: Azure is where companies rent computing power to run their software and build AI models. It is now Microsoft's largest and fastest-growing product line, at roughly 36% of the company's $92.3 billion in quarterly revenue. That total is up 19% and marks the tenth record quarter in a row. Net income rose 32% to $36.6 billion.

The timing of the disclosure is tidy. Alphabet reported the same day that Google Cloud earned $30.7 billion, which puts Azure slightly ahead. TechCrunch calculated, however, that Google Cloud roughly doubled its revenue from a year earlier, about twice Azure's pace. Google Cloud also includes Workspace subscriptions, so the comparison is imperfect, which is the sort of caveat that appears the moment anyone starts publishing comparable numbers. Amazon reports Thursday, and nobody expects Microsoft to overtake Amazon Web Services yet.

Azure beat expectations. Analysts had predicted $32.3 billion, so it landed about $850 million above. Growth of 48% also topped the 44% to 45% that CFO Amy Hood had forecast in July. Microsoft also reorganized its segments. Azure now sits in Agents and Infra, which brought in $76.8 billion, up 25%. The other segment, Devices and Consumer, earned $15.5 billion, down 3%, with Windows OEM and devices revenue falling 22%.

Then there is OpenAI. In a regulatory filing, Microsoft said it recorded $10 billion in revenue from commercial arrangements with OpenAI, including revenue-sharing payments, nearly 11% of the total. For all of fiscal 2026 the figure was $24.1 billion, and at the latest pace it would reach $40 billion a year. OpenAI also owed Microsoft $7.2 billion in receivables at the end of September, up from $6 billion in June. Translation: receivables are bills sent but not yet paid, which is not extra revenue, only a larger tab.

The relationship is loosening while the money keeps flowing. Under an October 2025 agreement, OpenAI gained freedom to use other cloud providers and committed to buying an additional $250 billion of Azure services. An April 2026 agreement keeps revenue-sharing payments going through 2030, subject to a cap. Microsoft's ownership stake has slipped from about 25% in June to 24% in September, and the company did not say why. TechCrunch noted it is unclear how much of the $10 billion is revenue sharing versus cloud services.

Microsoft's commercial remaining performance obligation, meaning future revenue committed under existing contracts, rose 73% to $677 billion. Translation: customers, largely AI labs, have promised to spend a very large amount of money. The company is spending in kind. Capital expenditures reached $45.2 billion, another quarterly record, to build the capacity those promises require.

The picture is a company earning record profits from a partner it increasingly competes with, while spending record sums to serve that partner. Now that all three cloud giants report real dollars, the next test is whether those contracts turn into revenue before the data centers finish being paid for.
