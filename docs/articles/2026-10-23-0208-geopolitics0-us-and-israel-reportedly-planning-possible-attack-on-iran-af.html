U.S. and Israel Reportedly Schedule Possible Iran Attack for After the Midterms, Out of Courtesy to the Ballot

An anonymous source told NPR the two allies are planning for a possible early-November strike, while Iran says it is prepared.

The wire said: U.S. and Israel are planning for a possible attack on Iran, source tells NPR · NPR World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 23, 2026 02:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

NPR reported that the U.S. and Israel are preparing plans for a possible attack on Iran in early November, citing a person briefed on war planning. The report follows suggestions from President Trump and Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu that military action is on the table.

Why it matters

A possible strike on Iran would carry risks for regional stability, energy markets and U.S. forces in the Middle East, and the reported timing suggests domestic politics may shape when such a decision is made. For now it is a plan attributed to one unnamed source, not an announced decision.

The United States and Israel are preparing plans for a possible attack on Iran in early November, after the U.S. midterm elections, according to a person briefed on war planning who spoke to NPR. Iran, for its part, said it is prepared. The source was not named, which is standard practice when discussing things governments would rather not be asked about on the record.

The timing is the detail that stands out. Early November falls after the midterms, which means that, if the report is accurate, the schedule leaves the voting finished before the shooting might start. Translation: a possible war is being slotted around the election calendar, the way a company avoids announcing layoffs the week before a shareholder meeting.

NPR's explainer on the planning notes that President Trump and Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu have both been suggesting that an attack is on the table. That is a notable thing about this story: the leaders have been hinting publicly, while the dates come from a private source. Public ambiguity is a tool in its own right, since it keeps an adversary guessing and keeps a domestic audience from asking follow-up questions.

It is worth being clear about what is and is not known. The sources describe planning, not a decision. Militaries draw up plans for many contingencies, and having one on file is not the same as ordering it. The word doing the heavy lifting in every headline here is "possible." Translation: the plan exists, the decision may not, and the difference is the entire story.

Iran's statement that it is prepared is the sort of reply that costs nothing to say and would be alarming to omit. Neither side has an obvious incentive to sound relaxed. Each statement is aimed partly at the other government and partly at its own public, and it is difficult to tell from outside which audience gets the larger share.

For ordinary people, the practical stakes sit in the usual places: oil prices, regional security and the safety of troops stationed in the area. None of those have moved because of one anonymous source, but markets and governments tend to pay attention to a date once someone has written it down.

NPR's companion newscast pairs this report with word that Elon Musk is considering merging SpaceX and Tesla. The two stories have little in common, other than both being plans that have been discussed and not yet done. What to watch next: whether any official confirms, denies or declines to comment, since in Washington the third option is often the most informative.
