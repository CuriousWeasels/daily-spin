Data Centres Become Rare Issue Uniting Both US Parties, Neither Of Which Can Stop Building Them

With about 12,250 sites worldwide and 16 US states weighing curbs, the buildings that run the internet are now a midterm campaign issue, according to the BBC.

The wire said: Data centre debate heats up ahead of US midterms - here's what you need to know · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 26, 2026 01:55 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Data centres are the warehouse-like buildings that house the chips and servers behind everything from online banking to ChatGPT. Their rapid growth and heavy electricity use have prompted a backlash ahead of the US midterm elections on 3 November.

Why it matters

Data centres can strain local power grids, water supplies and energy prices, so where they are built affects household bills as well as the AI industry's plans. The political fight will help decide how fast the buildout continues and who pays for it.

The BBC reported that opposition to data centres has become a significant issue in the United States ahead of the midterm elections on 3 November, with both Republicans and Democrats coming out against their development. President Donald Trump has dismissed the pushback as a "sick conspiracy". In a polarised country, this is notable: voters have found something they can agree to dislike, and it is a warehouse.

The scale explains the attention. According to Data Center Map, which tracks both operational and planned sites, there are about 12,250 data centres worldwide, up from around 8,000 operational sites in 2021. The US has 4,767 of them, and leads by an even wider margin in total capacity, with China second and Europe far behind. Translation: capacity is the amount of electricity a site can use, which is a better measure of how much computing is going on than counting buildings, since some data centres fit in a garden shed.

The sites are also getting larger. The biggest operational one is widely reported to be run by China Telecom in Inner Mongolia, covering about one million square metres, or roughly 140 football pitches. Larger ones are under construction, built solely for AI. Colossus 2, part of a wider campus, will house hundreds of thousands of Nvidia chips and help train xAI's Grok chatbot, and xAI will use 41 methane gas turbines to power it.

Those turbines illustrate the dispute neatly. Activists say they could significantly increase local air pollution, while local regulators say they will meet air quality standards. Both statements can be true at once, which is the usual arrangement when a standard is set by the people who also approve the project.

The wider complaint is about resources. Data centres worldwide are thought to consume more electricity than all of France, and some use significant amounts of water for cooling, which experts say could contribute to shortages. Analysis shows the demand is driving up carbon emissions and straining local grids, and some people fear higher energy prices. Add noise and land use, and the BBC says about 16 US states have proposed laws to ban or temporarily block new facilities. Opposition has also appeared in Mexico, France, Indonesia and the UK.

Supporters argue the buildings matter for national security and AI development, and that they bring investment, tax revenue and construction jobs. A Brookings report cited by the BBC found they do create local jobs, but fewer than industry advocates claim. Hyperscale sites, the huge ones run by firms like Amazon and Microsoft, help local employment more. Colocation sites, which lease space to tenants who are not physically present, deliver fewer benefits. Translation: the building that rents out racks for faraway customers is the one that employs the fewest neighbours.

The industry is not slowing down. The BBC says tech firms are expected to spend a combined $765bn on AI infrastructure in 2026, with the total topping $1tn next year. Proposed fixes include putting data centres in space or on the Moon, where solar energy is abundant, or doing more AI processing on the chips already inside our phones and laptops. Moving the problem off the planet is, at least, a solution that has no local zoning board.
