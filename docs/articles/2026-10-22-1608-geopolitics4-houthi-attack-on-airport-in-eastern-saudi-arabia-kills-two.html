Houthis Hit Saudi Airport, Proving Four-Year Ceasefire Was Mostly a Long Intermission

Two people died at Dammam's King Fahd International Airport, and two more died from drone debris in the oil-rich Eastern Province, as the Houthi-Saudi conflict widens.

The wire said: Two killed in Houthi attack on airport in eastern Saudi Arabia, authorities say · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 22, 2026 16:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Yemen's civil war began in 2014 and escalated in 2015 when a Saudi-led coalition intervened. An informal ceasefire that held for about four years began unraveling in July, when the Houthis announced a maritime embargo against Saudi Arabia.

Why it matters

Attacks on airports, oil facilities and a shipping chokepoint raise the risk of higher energy prices and disrupted travel. The foreign workers among the dead also show who is exposed when the war reaches civilian infrastructure.

Yemen's Houthi movement said it attacked an airport in eastern Saudi Arabia on Wednesday evening, and Saudi authorities said two people were killed and eight injured, according to the BBC. The Saudi General Authority of Civil Aviation identified the dead at King Fahd International Airport in Dammam as a Saudi citizen and an Indian resident. It did not say what kind of attack it was, which is one way of describing an event without describing it.

The Houthis were somewhat more forthcoming. They said the airport was among several sites targeted with drones and missiles over the previous 48 hours. Translation: the group is publishing a list of targets the way a restaurant publishes a specials board, except the customers did not order.

Separately, the Saudi energy ministry said two more people were killed on Wednesday morning by debris from a drone intercepted elsewhere in Eastern Province. The drone was reportedly aimed at an Aramco processing facility in the Shaybah oil field, and a contractor's vehicle was set ablaze. The ministry identified the dead as residents from the Philippines and Nepal. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Sarea did not claim that attack, a rare moment of restraint in a week otherwise short on it.

Sarea did name other targets: the Saudi defence ministry headquarters in Riyadh, an Aramco facility in Yanbu, and Abha International Airport. The Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted four drones over Eastern Province on Wednesday night and four more over Riyadh and Medina provinces on Thursday. Interception is the policy of shooting things down before they arrive, and debris is what happens to the part of the policy that works.

The Saudi authorities say at least 21 people have been killed in Houthi attacks on Saudi airports since the start of October. That includes a missile strike on Riyadh's international airport on 10 October that killed 13 and injured 308, which the BBC described as the deadliest attack in Saudi Arabia in more than a decade. The Houthis say they are retaliating for Saudi air strikes on territory they control in western Yemen.

The casualty counts there are disputed by source. Houthi-run health authorities say 155 civilians have been killed since the war flared up three months ago, while the government's health ministry says at least 1,574 people have died over the same period. The fighting has displaced more than 256,000 people. The two sides agree on very little, but both agree the number is not small.

The widening matters beyond Arabia. The Houthis seized part of Yemen's south-western Red Sea coast near the Bab al-Mandab Strait in September, a chokepoint for global shipping. The BBC says their advance contributed to a rise in oil prices, following six months of war between the US, Israel and Iran. Pro-government forces say they have retaken territory overlooking the strait with Saudi air support, but fierce fighting continues there and around Taiz, Marib, al-Jawf, Dhale and Shabwa.

The diplomatic signals are not calming. The BBC reported that the US has ordered some diplomats to leave Saudi Arabia and that the UK plans to deploy jets to help defend the kingdom. When governments start moving staff out and aircraft in, it usually means they have read the same list of targets as everyone else.
