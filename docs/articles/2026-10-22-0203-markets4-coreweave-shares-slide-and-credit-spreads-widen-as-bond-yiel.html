CoreWeave Learns That AI Vibes Are Not Collateral, Bond Market Says 15%

The biggest neocloud has lost roughly $56 billion in market value since its peak and now pays about 15% on unsecured debt, while three big banks stay bullish.

The wire said: CoreWeave’s market confidence erodes as bond yields spike and stock sinks · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 22, 2026 02:03 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

CoreWeave rents out Nvidia chips to AI companies and has financed its expansion largely with debt. It went public in March of last year and hit a market cap of about $90 billion three months later.

Why it matters

If the most prominent AI infrastructure borrower struggles to finance itself, the data center build-out that investors and tech giants have promised may slow or get more expensive. Retail investors holding AI-heavy funds are exposed to the same shift in sentiment.

CoreWeave is having a crisis of confidence on Wall Street, CNBC reported, with stock and bond investors both heading for the exits at the same time. The company is now valued at roughly $34 billion, down from a peak of about $90 billion, and its stock is down 29% this month. Its unsecured debt yields about 15%, which is the market's way of saying it would like to be paid for its optimism in advance.

Translation: a yield is the return a lender demands for holding a bond, and it rises as the lender gets more nervous. Credit default swaps, which are insurance against a borrower failing to pay, tell a similar story. The spread on CoreWeave's five-year contracts stood near 1,067 basis points, or 10.67%, up 164% in the past year. Translation: a basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point, and 1,067 of them is a lot of points.

The unease is not confined to one company. CNBC reported that Nebius shares are down 27% this month and IREN is down 32%. Australia's NextDC fell 40% on Wednesday after cutting its annual earnings forecast, and Firmus scrapped its planned IPO, citing market volatility. Barclays credit analyst Andrew Keches wrote of the "speed at which AI vibes are shifting on a day-to-day basis," which is a fair description of a sector whose collateral is partly vibes.

The timing is awkward because CoreWeave needs a great deal of money. It has about $20.7 billion in debt due through 2030 and another $14.9 billion after that, against $5.5 billion in cash at the end of the second quarter. Analysts expect negative free cash flow of $10.1 billion in the third quarter, the worst since it began reporting quarterly results, according to FactSet. Translation: free cash flow is cash left after running the business and buying equipment, and negative means the company is spending the difference from someone else's pocket.

Credit desks told CNBC the high-yield debt market is effectively on hold until after the midterm elections in two weeks, because of political pushback against data centers. One source said fixed income investors want permitting and regulatory requirements "substantially resolved" before committing. Janus Henderson's Seth Meyer said pausing makes sense. Lenders, it turns out, like to know whether the building is allowed to exist.

CoreWeave is also unusually entangled with its suppliers and customers. Nvidia holds about 9% according to FactSet, and more than two-thirds of last year's revenue came from Microsoft. Nvidia bought a 10% stake in January for $2 billion at $87.20 a share; the stock trades at $61.82. CEO Jensen Huang said last week that CoreWeave does not need a backstop, though some investors think it may need one anyway.

Management is projecting calm. CEO Mike Intrator told employees in a memo that the company's financing combines infrastructure-backed borrowing, corporate debt, equity and customer prepayments, with room to raise equity over time. Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan and Barclays all published bullish notes this week, and Goldman upgraded the stock to buy, saying CoreWeave is likely in the top quartile of neoclouds. Baird's Rob Oliver estimates the company may need roughly $30 billion in 2027, though not until the first quarter.

Next month's earnings report is the test. Investors will look for a financing plan that is larger than a memo. Until then, the market is pricing the company at 15% and the analysts are pricing it at buy, and both cannot be right for long.
