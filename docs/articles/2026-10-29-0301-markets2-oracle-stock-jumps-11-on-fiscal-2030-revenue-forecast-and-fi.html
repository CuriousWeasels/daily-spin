Oracle Promises $225 Billion in Revenue, Stock Jumps 11% on Strength of Spreadsheet

The company's investor-day targets and a $20 billion cap on new fundraising calmed nervous creditors, at least until the money actually has to arrive.

The wire said: Oracle stock pops 11% as long-term revenue forecast and clarity on financing ease debt concerns · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 29, 2026 03:01 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Oracle spent the past year borrowing heavily to build AI data center capacity, including for OpenAI, and its shares had lost about a third of their value this year as investors worried about the bill. On Wednesday it held an investor day to explain how it intends to pay for all of it.

Why it matters

Oracle's debt load has become a gauge of how nervous markets are about paying for the AI buildout, and its credit rating sits one notch above junk. If the company's targets slip, the cost of borrowing for AI infrastructure could rise for everyone else too.

Oracle shares rose 11% on Wednesday after the company told investors it expects $225 billion in revenue in fiscal 2030 and will limit additional fundraising over that stretch to $20 billion, CNBC reported. The forecast implies average annual growth of 35%. The market, which had spent months asking whether Oracle could afford its ambitions, responded to a slide deck that said yes.

The financing plan covers fiscal 2027 through 2030, and Oracle said the $20 billion will be split equally between equity and debt. Translation: equity means selling new shares, which dilutes existing owners, and debt means borrowing, which Oracle has already done rather a lot. Half and half is the corporate version of splitting the check.

The reason for the nerves is on the balance sheet. According to CNBC, Oracle has issued $117 billion in long-term debt and holds a BBB- credit rating from S&P, the lowest level of investment grade above junk. Translation: it is one downgrade away from being classed with borrowers who pay the most to borrow. Its shares had fallen about a third this year on fears that rising interest rates would make the buildout too expensive.

The spending is not modest. FactSet figures cited by CNBC show capital expenditures more than doubled to $55.7 billion in fiscal 2026 and are expected to rise another 65% to $91.9 billion this year. Analysts expect negative free cash flow of about $35 billion this year and a similar amount next year. Translation: Oracle is spending far more than it earns, and will for a while.

Oracle's answer was a timeline. It said it will turn free cash flow positive in the second half of fiscal 2028 and reach more than $30 billion the following year, with operating margin topping 40% in fiscal 2030. It also said vendor financing, customer-provided hardware and customer prepayments should reduce upfront cash needs. In plain terms, suppliers will wait to be paid and customers will pay early, which is a clever way to have other people's money do the building.

The forecast was less of a shock than the stock move suggests. Analysts polled by LSEG already projected fiscal 2030 revenue of $224.2 billion, slightly below Oracle's own $225 billion. The news was less the number than the confidence that the company would not need to raise much more money to get there.

Credit markets were calmer but not convinced. The spread on Oracle's five-year credit default swaps, which are insurance against default, stood at 277 basis points after touching about 320 earlier this month, and is still up almost 100% this year. Translation: insuring $10 million of Oracle debt costs roughly $277,000 a year, down from about $320,000 a few weeks ago, and nearly double the start of the year.

The backdrop is not helpful. The 10-year Treasury yield stands at 4.99% and the 2-year at 4.7%, so borrowing remains expensive for everyone, including companies promising 35% growth. Oracle has now told investors when the cash will turn positive. The next step is doing it, which tends to be harder than presenting it.
