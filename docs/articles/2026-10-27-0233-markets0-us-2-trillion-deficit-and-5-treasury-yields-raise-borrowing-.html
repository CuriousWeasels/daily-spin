America Posts $2 Trillion Deficit, Bond Market Asks for a Raise

With the 10-year Treasury yield above 5% and divided government expected after the midterms, CNBC reports that no one has a plan to fix the debt.

The wire said: Analysis: Why a $2 trillion deficit and 5% Treasury yields are becoming America's biggest economic challenge · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 27, 2026 02:33 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The U.S. government ran a deficit of $1.992 trillion in fiscal 2026, up 12% from last year, according to Treasury data cited by CNBC. Long-term bond yields have climbed since the war with Iran began on Feb. 28.

Why it matters

Treasury yields set the price of mortgages and other borrowing, so a government that borrows heavily makes everyone else's loans costlier. Mortgage rates near 7.5% are already cooling the housing market.

The United States closed its 2026 fiscal year with a deficit of $1.992 trillion, 12% more than the year before, according to Treasury figures reported by CNBC. That is roughly 6.2% of gross domestic product. The Treasury Secretary's stated goal is a deficit below 4%, a target CNBC says the country has almost no chance of hitting.

The bond market has responded in the way markets do, by quoting a higher price. The 10-year Treasury yield traded at 5.1% on Friday, up 114 basis points since the end of February. Translation: a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so the government now pays about 1.14 percentage points more to borrow for a decade than it did before the war began.

Several things are pushing yields up. The Iran war, which began Feb. 28, lifted oil and diesel prices, and bond traders want extra yield in case central banks such as the Federal Reserve raise rates in response. Investors also expect a steady flood of government bonds, while companies are issuing hundreds of billions in debt to build artificial intelligence infrastructure. Translation: the Treasury is no longer the only borrower at the party, and it has the biggest tab.

Ordinary people feel this through mortgages, which track the 10-year yield. The average 30-year fixed rate is hovering around 7.5%, and Redfin said pending home sales fell 4% in September, the largest monthly drop in three years. Buyers are discovering that the national debt has a personal interface.

Higher rates also make the debt harder to shrink. The Congressional Budget Office estimates that if rates run half a point above its forecast, ten-year deficits grow by $1.9 trillion; a 1.5-point overshoot adds $6 trillion. The CBO expects the 10-year yield to average 4.3% over that period, against 5.1% today, so the forecast currently requires the market to calm down on its own.

The administration's answer is growth. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said in Dallas last month, "we have to grow our way out of it," and he and others point to artificial intelligence as the engine. CNBC notes that the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget calculates that balancing the budget by growth alone would take a decade of 7.2% growth. Second-quarter growth was recently revised up to 2.2% annualized, which is progress in the sense that the gap is now 5 points instead of 5.7.

Politics offers little help. Republicans are widely forecast to lose at least the House, and CNBC reports that President Trump pledged $5,000 checks to every adult citizen if Republicans swept the election, which is not a classic deficit-reduction measure. Rep. Hakeem Jeffries, the likely next Democratic speaker, wants to reverse $1 trillion in healthcare cuts passed last year. The two sides begin from opposite ends of the ledger.

CNBC stresses that this is not yet a debt crisis. Total debt reached $40 trillion this year, and no one knows precisely what level becomes unsustainable. A real crisis would look like today with yields spiking and global investors dumping dollar assets, with little the Treasury or the Fed could do. For now, higher yields are also attracting buyers, a reminder that expensive borrowing is not the same as catastrophe.

The fix, per CNBC's analysis, is political rather than economic. Watch the post-election negotiations, and the 10-year yield, which will say what it thinks well before Congress does.
