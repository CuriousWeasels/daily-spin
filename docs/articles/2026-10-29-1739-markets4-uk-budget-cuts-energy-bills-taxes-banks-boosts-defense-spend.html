Britain Taxes Banks, Cuts Bills, and Asks Markets Not to Notice

Chancellor John Healey's first budget raises the bank surcharge to 6% while lowering household energy costs, and gilt yields and bank shares both moved the right way.

The wire said: The UK cuts energy bills, taxes banks and boosts defense in high-stakes budget · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 29, 2026 17:39 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The UK's new finance minister, John Healey, delivered his first budget on Wednesday against a backdrop of 3.5% inflation in September, rising government borrowing costs and an energy price shock he linked to the U.S.-Iran war. Markets had been braced for something worse.

Why it matters

Households get lower gas and electricity costs and some direct payments, while banks pay a temporary levy. If inflation pushes the Bank of England to raise rates next month, the budget's mild reception may matter less than the price of heating a house.

The UK government presented a budget on Wednesday that cuts household energy costs, raises taxes on banks, gambling firms and vape shops, and increases defense and Ukraine support, CNBC reported. Finance minister John Healey said he wanted to give families "breathing space." It is the rare budget in which almost everyone in the room could claim a win, which is usually a sign that someone is being billed later.

The cost-of-living package is the headline. Healey will remove standing charges on household gas heating bills (Translation: the fixed daily fee you pay for being connected, before you use any gas), extend an electricity tax break through July 2027, and send £100 checks to households in the four lowest local housing tax bands, at a cost of £2.6 billion. Tariffs come off more than 100 grocery products, the minimum wage rises 3.9%, and a planned cut to tax-free savings thresholds was canceled. Healey cited the inflationary effect of the U.S.-Iran war, a conflict he said Britain "did not want." Inflation reached 3.5% in September, and markets expect the Bank of England to raise rates next month.

Someone has to pay, and that someone is the banking sector. The bank surcharge rises by 3 percentage points to 6%, starting in January and ending 15 months later. (Translation: a surcharge is an extra slice of profit taxed on top of the normal corporate rate, and "temporary" is a word governments use with great sincerity.) Healey said he would raise revenue "from those who can afford to pay," and also promised no tax rises on wages or dividends. This is the same Healey who earlier this month called Britain's banks a "jewel" in the economic crown. It is a generous way to describe something you are about to invoice.

The banks took it well. The increase was milder than some in the sector feared, so Barclays rose 3.3%, Lloyds more than 2.6% and NatWest 2.5%. JPMorgan Chase was less relaxed. It said singling out the industry for additional taxes "lacks principle," and CNBC noted that CEO Jamie Dimon has suggested the bank might rethink its Canary Wharf headquarters buildout if the government is "hostile." On Wednesday JPMorgan said it remained "committed" to the UK, which is how a company says it is staying while keeping the receipt.

On the numbers, Healey says the budget respects the fiscal rules, meaning day-to-day spending must be funded by taxes rather than borrowing, and debt must fall as a share of GDP. The Office for Budget Responsibility, the official forecaster, estimates £15 billion of headroom by 2029/30. It sees debt rising slightly to 83.8% of GDP next year before falling to 81.7% by 2032, and raised its growth forecasts to 1.2% this year, 1% in 2027 and 1.6% in 2028. Headroom is the gap between the plan and the rule, and it is the thing chancellors spend their careers trying not to lose.

Bond investors, the audience that matters most in any British budget, approved cautiously. The 10-year gilt yield fell 2 basis points to 5.323% as Healey spoke. (Translation: a basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point, so this was a very small vote of confidence at a very high price for borrowing.) Sterling was marginally lower against the dollar and 0.2% lower against the euro.

Elsewhere in the budget, Healey pledged £10 billion of economic and military support for Ukraine and said he would set out how Britain reaches NATO's 3.5% of GDP defense target by 2035, with 3% by 2030. He announced hundreds of millions of pounds for AI security and sovereignty, plus a "covenant" requiring data centers to invest locally, create jobs and protect water supplies in exchange for grid connections. Founders get a reduced capital gains tax rate on gains up to £10 million. He also promised to reduce the welfare bill, which hit 10.7% of GDP in 2024/25, a subject on which his predecessor Rachel Reeves learned how much backbenchers can object.

The kicker is that the budget pleased the markets by being less alarming than expected, which is the current standard for fiscal success. What to watch next is the Bank of England's rate decision next month, which can undo a lot of breathing space very quickly.
