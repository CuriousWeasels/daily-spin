Serbia Votes on Whether Its Strongest Leader Can Be Beaten by a Student Council

A leaderless movement of students, a cardiologist and 250 candidates with no political experience has Aleksandar Vučić facing the closest race of his 14 years in power.

The wire said: Student movement takes Serbia's Vučić to the wire in neck-and-neck election · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 24, 2026 14:57 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Protests began after a canopy collapsed at Novi Sad railway station in November 2024, killing 16 people, and students have since channeled the anger into a candidate list for Sunday's parliamentary election. Vučić, who stood down as president to run for prime minister, has led Serbia in one office or another since 2012.

Why it matters

A close result in an EU candidate country that also keeps warm ties with Russia and China could produce a disputed count and street confrontations. Brussels' reaction may decide whether any post-election crisis cools or escalates.

Serbia holds parliamentary elections on Sunday, and for once the outcome is genuinely uncertain. The BBC reported that analysts expect an extremely tight vote, though reliable polls are scarce. DW quoted political scientist Dusan Spasojevic saying these are the first competitive elections since Aleksandar Vučić came to power in 2012, and that the opposition could win more than 40 percent. In a system where the ruling party has usually known the result in advance, uncertainty counts as a major reform.

The challenger is the Student List, which grew out of nearly two years of anti-corruption protests. Student assemblies at university faculties chose 250 candidates, among them academics, athletes, economists and engineers, none with a political background. Translation: the movement looked at professional politicians and decided the ideal qualification was never having been one. Ilija Srdanović, a cardiologist and Novi Sad professor, tops the list, though the BBC notes he is not the movement's leader and might not be its first choice for prime minister.

That last detail is the movement's central quirk. It has no hierarchy and no nominated leader, which makes it unlike anything in Serbia's short democratic history. Vučić's campaign offers the opposite proposition. At its final rally in Belgrade Arena he was the only speaker, while across the Sava River the Student List drew a reported 150,000 people to Slavija Square for a lineup of students and candidates. Serbia is being asked to choose between one man at a microphone and a committee at a dance.

Vučić's argument is stability and sovereignty. He has painted the students as foreign-backed and bent on bringing down the state, and told supporters, according to DW, that opponents had been 'attacked with billions of euros.' He has also warned that Serbia's ties with Russia and China could be severed if the students win, and complained that they dodge the question of sanctions on Russia. Neither source reports evidence for the foreign-funding claim. The BBC does note he can point to real economic growth and new infrastructure.

The trouble is that the infrastructure is also the problem. The Novi Sad station was renovated as part of the Belgrade-Budapest high-speed line, his signature project, so the canopy collapse is hard to blame on a subordinate. ACLED analyst Magda Anastasijevic told the BBC that Vučić has survived past crises by finding a scapegoat, which was not possible this time.

The playing field is, to put it gently, not level. CRTA, an independent election monitor, told DW that nearly three-quarters of national television airtime for political players went to government representatives, and more than 80 percent of coverage of the Student List was negative. It also recorded 116 incidents of violence or threats by the final week. DW further reported, citing anonymous sources, offers of €30 to €150 per vote and holiday packages for families who pledge every vote to the ruling party. These are allegations, and the BBC says the SNS did not respond to its interview request. International observers in 2023 had already noted an 'overwhelming ruling party advantage.'

Both sides are preparing for election night. Authorities have warned that students, allegedly aided by foreign intelligence, plan violence, and several people were arrested on suspicion of plotting disturbances. The students have vowed to defend every vote. Spasojevic told DW that the best case is a decisive win for either side, but that a narrow margin probably means a post-election crisis, with a narrow SNS win almost certain to be contested.

That puts the European Union in a familiar role. Spasojevic called its response potentially the only thing that could persuade authorities to accept defeat and avoid a heavy-handed reaction. An EU Commission spokesperson told DW that recommendations on improving Serbia's electoral framework remain unimplemented. Serbia has been a candidate country since 2012, which in Brussels terms is roughly the length of a long engagement.

Watch Sunday night for the count, the margin and the first statements from both camps. In an election where one side has the media and the other has the crowds, whoever speaks first may matter almost as much as who wins.
