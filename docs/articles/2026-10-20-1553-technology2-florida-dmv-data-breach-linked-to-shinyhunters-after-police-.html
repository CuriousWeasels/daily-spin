Florida DMV Breach Traced to Police Login Stored Somewhere Helpful to Everyone

Florida's motor vehicle agency is mailing breach notices and offering a year of credit monitoring, though it may lack addresses for many former residents whose data it kept.

The wire said: Florida DMV hacked after police dept insecurely stored login info · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 20, 2026 15:53 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The Florida Department of Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles said a malicious actor accessed department records on September 4, 2026, using compromised credentials. Ars Technica's headline attributes the compromise to a police department that stored login information insecurely.

Why it matters

Driver's license data, including document images, is the kind of material that helps criminals impersonate people, and it does not expire when a monitoring subscription does. Anyone who has ever held a Florida license may need to check their own status instead of waiting for a letter.

Florida's Department of Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles has begun notifying people affected by a breach of driver's license and motor vehicle data, according to Ars Technica. The department said an intruder accessed records on September 4, 2026, using compromised credentials. Translation: credentials are a username and password, and the intruder did not break in so much as get let in by someone else's key.

Ars Technica's headline says the credentials came from a police department that stored its login information insecurely. The excerpt we have does not elaborate on the details, so the specifics of that arrangement remain between the police, the DMV and a very uncomfortable meeting. What is clear is that a system holding the identity documents of millions of people was guarded, in effect, by whatever care the weakest authorized user took with a password.

The department says there is no evidence the data has been misused so far. It also says law enforcement determined the affected information potentially includes driver license and motor vehicle data, including document images, and other personal information. Translation: no evidence of misuse means nobody has found any yet, which is not the same as nobody planning any. Pictures of licenses are especially useful to fraudsters because they come with the face, the signature and the number already attached.

The remedy on offer is one year of credit monitoring from Sontiq, Inc., delivered by letter. The department acknowledged, however, that it may not have current contact information for many former Florida residents whose data it was still storing. That is the quiet punchline: the state kept the records of people it can no longer find, and now cannot tell them it lost them. Those people can email the department to check whether they were affected.

Residents have not been patient about the timeline. Some complained they received notices only this week, though the breach happened more than a month ago and news outlets had already reported it. On the Florida subreddit, several people said they had already frozen their credit or placed fraud alerts, which was the department's third recommendation, so the public got there ahead of the mail.

The department confirmed that an international cybercriminal organization was behind the breach but did not name the group or say how long it had access. The Ars Technica report carries a subhead linking the hack to ShinyHunters, though the portion of the text available here does not detail that attribution. The department says it promptly mitigated the breach, launched an investigation and notified state authorities as required.

What to watch: whether the department ever says how long the intruder had access, and whether anyone besides the affected citizens is required to change a password. A year of credit monitoring is a generous offer, as long as the data agrees to expire on the same schedule.
