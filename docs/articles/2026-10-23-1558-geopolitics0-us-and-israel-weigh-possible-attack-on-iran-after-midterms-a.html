U.S. and Israel Draft Iran Strike Plans, Politely Scheduled Around the Midterms

Reports say Washington and Jerusalem have a 'short but intensive' operation ready, while two elections and a stalled negotiation decide when the 'short' part begins.

The wire said: What we know about war plans that Trump and Netanyahu are drawing up · NPR World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 23, 2026 15:58 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The U.S. and Israel launched a war against Iran in late February, and the U.S. last struck in early September. Talks to end it have stalled, a U.S. blockade of Iranian oil exports continues, and both countries face elections within days.

Why it matters

A renewed attack could widen the war across the Gulf and keep energy prices high, which already weigh on U.S. voters and the economy. The timing shows how closely military decisions and election calendars can be linked.

The United States and Israel have developed specific plans for a short but intensive operation in Iran, the New York Times reported Wednesday, citing senior U.S. officials. Targets would reportedly include nuclear facilities, energy infrastructure, and Iran's stockpile of missiles and drones. The plan is not scheduled for before the American midterms on November 3, which is a coincidence in the sense that calendars are a coincidence.

On October 8, according to DW, President Trump posted that the U.S. would not attack Iran before the midterms, citing 'productive discussions' with Tehran. Polls show high energy prices from the war are hurting his Republican Party. Translation: the war has made gasoline expensive, and voters have noticed, so the next phase of the war will wait until they have finished noticing.

The preparations are visibly continuing. Axios reported in early October that the Pentagon told U.S. Central Command to finish preparations for resuming major combat operations. Two U.S. aircraft carrier strike groups are in the Arabian Sea, with a third expected by late November. NPR reports military reinforcements are heading to the region and some personnel have left it, which analysts read as a sign of a potential attack. Meanwhile, NPR notes, millions of Americans have already voted early.

The stated goal, per one official quoted by the Times, is to force Iran into 'diplomatic concessions' by further weakening its economy. Translation: bomb the country until it agrees to a deal it has not agreed to while not being bombed. The logic is that negotiations have gone nowhere, so the alternative to talking is a more forceful version of the thing that has not produced talks.

Some of the details are less tidy. Trump told Newsmax on October 15 he was considering striking Pickaxe Mountain near Natanz, where U.S. officials say sensitive nuclear material may be underground. CNN reported a day later that he was weighing the MOAB, a massive bomb. DW notes that a weapon built around an enormous above-ground blast wave has limited effect on facilities buried in a mountain, which is a real constraint when the target is a mountain.

Tehran says it is ready. Reuters, citing Iranian officials, reported Iran is preparing for renewed attacks, not a deal, and is dragging out talks to weaken Trump before the midterms. So both sides are using the negotiation as a waiting room. Iran has warned neighbors that U.S. bases and infrastructure on their soil could be targets if they assist an operation, and Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi promised a 'decisive and proportionate response.'

Iran's latest proposal, sent through Qatari mediators, would reopen the Strait of Hormuz under Iranian control in return for lifting the U.S. blockade, releasing frozen assets and ending military action. U.S. officials say the nuclear program must top the agenda. The Wall Street Journal, citing Kpler data, reported oil flows through Hormuz are approaching prewar levels under U.S. escorts, which raises the question of how much leverage Iran is holding.

Iranians face this at a bad moment. A Tehran doctor told DW the economy has become extremely bad and she expects internet restrictions if fighting resumes. Asked about strikes on underground nuclear sites, another woman said: 'What do the nuclear sites have to do with us?' They are, she noted, inside mountains. Israel votes October 27, and Netanyahu has campaigned on regime change. The plans are ready; what remains is the ballot count.
