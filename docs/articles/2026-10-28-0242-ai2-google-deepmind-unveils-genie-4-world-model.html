Google's Genie 4 Builds Whole Worlds That Stay Coherent for Two Minutes

DeepMind's new world model adds audio, better physics and mid-simulation edits, though the worlds still forget how they started.

The wire said: DeepMind unveils Genie 4 world model promising more realistic physics and humans · Ars Technica AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 28, 2026 02:42 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Google DeepMind's Genie models generate interactive, explorable environments from text prompts, frame by frame in real time. Genie 3, released as a research preview a little over a year ago, rattled gaming stocks and helped make world models a major focus for AI labs.

Why it matters

World models are being pitched as a cheap way to prototype films and games and to train robots in simulation before they touch the real world. Investors and studios are watching, but a model whose scenes drift after two minutes is not yet replacing anyone's production pipeline.

Google DeepMind released Genie 4, its latest world model, in research preview on Tuesday, according to TechCrunch. The model generates interactive environments from prompts, now with real-time audio including music, ambient noise and dialogue. Google says the worlds are more photorealistic, with better physics and more humanlike characters.

Translation: a world model does not play back a pre-built video game. It invents each frame as you move through the scene, so there is no level designer, only a very confident guess about what should appear next.

The headline improvement is length. The Verge reports that Genie 4 experiences run about two minutes, up from one minute for Genie 3. Ars Technica notes that worlds stay consistent for only a couple of minutes before they forget how things started. That is a doubling, and also an admission that the model has the attention span of a toddler at a museum.

Google says the model leans more heavily on its own Gemini system. Ars Technica reports that Gemini 3.8 Flash acts as a guiding agent, breaking down goals and evaluating each output frame. TechCrunch describes Gemini as the director that plans steps and checks frames while Genie makes the visuals. Users can also type new prompts mid-simulation to change the weather, add people or objects, or move the camera.

Tejas Kulkarni, a research director at Google, told reporters the future holds hybrid world models combining Gemini writing code and text with Genie simulating reality. In one experiment tied to Gemini Robotics, a Gemini model broke tasks into steps that Genie 4 simulated in real time. The idea is to test robot scenarios virtually before they happen in the physical world, where the furniture is less forgiving.

The commercial pitch is broad: game prototyping, interactive learning, robot training and, according to trusted testers, film pre-visualization. Diego Rivas, a DeepMind engineer, said real-time generation is most valuable in the ideation and storyboarding phases. TechCrunch also floated entering a historical painting and chatting with its subjects, a use case it labeled speculative. Rivas stressed that it is early days and that the end-to-end product experience is not yet clear.

Google concedes the limits. Details and objects drift the longer a simulation runs, and the team has not figured out how to guarantee accurate audio or legible in-world text. The Verge's reporter, who has not tried Genie 4, noted that he used Genie 3's Project Genie to produce bad Nintendo knockoffs, which is at least a benchmark.

Access is staged. Genie 4 is available now to trusted testers. Google AI Ultra subscribers are due to get it in Project Genie in the coming weeks, and Google has opened a waitlist. Early access partners of the Gemini API and Google AI Studio are also on the schedule. Ars Technica put the pricing logic plainly: pay Google enough money and you may get to use it.

The competition is busy. TechCrunch notes that Runway released its second world model, GWM Worlds 2, a couple of months ago, and that the sector is consolidating: World Labs is being acquired by AMD, Amazon is in talks to buy Decart, and Rivian acquired Third Dimension for self-driving simulation. When everyone is buying the company that builds the fake world, it is a fair sign that the real one is considered too expensive to test in.

What to watch: whether the two-minute ceiling rises, and whether gaming stocks react this time or have finally accepted that the worlds are not yet staying put.
