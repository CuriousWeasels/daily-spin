Maduro Told to Plead, Critiques Indictment Instead; Judge Enters Plea Himself

A New York court added torture allegations to the drug case against Venezuela's deposed president, who called them false, leaving the judge to handle the paperwork.

The wire said: Nicolás Maduro tells New York court new torture allegations are ‘false’ · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 17, 2026 14:30 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

US forces seized Nicolás Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores, from their Caracas home in a January night raid, and they face a drug trafficking case in New York. Prosecutors have now rewritten the indictment to add allegations of a state-run torture campaign.

Why it matters

The case tests whether a US court can try a foreign leader for torture, something the Guardian describes as exceedingly rare, and whether a head-of-state immunity defense can survive it. The outcome will shape how far Washington's courts can reach into other governments.

Nicolás Maduro appeared in a New York court on Thursday, shackled at the ankles, and described the torture allegations in a rewritten indictment as "false accusations," the Guardian reported. Judge Alvin K Hellerstein asked him to enter a plea. Maduro twice answered by criticizing the indictment instead, so the judge entered a not-guilty plea on his behalf. Translation: when a defendant keeps giving a closing argument at the arraignment, the court will simply fill in the form.

Maduro's wife, Cilia Flores, a former attorney general, was more economical. Speaking through an interpreter, she said: "I am completely innocent. I am not guilty." Both spoke through interpreters, and both are now officially contesting the charges.

The revised indictment alleges that Maduro oversaw a system that beat, asphyxiated and electrically shocked its victims, according to the Guardian. It says he personally recommended torture methods, received updates on specific victims' treatment and threatened to kill one prisoner's child as psychological torture. Prosecutors say Flores helped develop the system and ordered the torture of certain individuals. The charges say victims included scores of people, among them three Americans. The three Americans matter legally, but the allegations about everyone else are the substance of the case.

The torture charges stretch back to 2013, when Maduro succeeded his mentor Hugo Chávez as president. Maduro served until his capture in January. The International Criminal Court has also investigated him for years over alleged crimes against humanity tied to the crackdown on opponents. He has always denied wrongdoing, and his attorney Barry Pollack said last week that he "looks forward to his day in court."

There is a legal mechanism behind the new charges. Torture is a crime under international law and can be prosecuted outside the country where it happened, regardless of the victims' nationality. Translation: a country can try you for it even if you did it somewhere else, to someone else. In practice, the Guardian notes, the US has rarely done this to foreign officials, citing the son of former Liberian president Charles Taylor, Russians responsible for war crimes in Ukraine and a former prison warden in Bashar Assad's Syria.

The couple already pleaded not guilty earlier this year in the drug case, which alleges a conspiracy to flood the US with cocaine. Their lawyers argue it should be dismissed on immunity grounds, since the defendants were the leader and first lady of a foreign country. That argument is awkward to make for a government that was removed by a military raid, but it is the argument available.

On scheduling, both sides told the judge they want the trial moved from 1 June to 14 June. It is the one point on which prosecution and defense agree, and it involves a two-week delay. Watch whether the immunity motion succeeds, since it decides whether any of the rest gets heard.
