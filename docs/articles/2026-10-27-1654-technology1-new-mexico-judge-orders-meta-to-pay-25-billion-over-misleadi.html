Meta Loses $25 Billion in New Mexico, Making Settling States Check Their Receipts

A New Mexico judge's order dwarfs the multistate deal, leaving attorneys general who settled to wonder whether they left money on the table.

The wire said: Meta’s $25 billion legal loss just put blood in the water · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 27, 2026 16:54 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

This summer, attorneys general from across the US announced a $17 billion settlement with Meta over kids' online safety, with a $459 million portion covering Cambridge Analytica-style claims. Last week a New Mexico judge ordered Meta to pay $25 billion on similar claims.

Why it matters

The ruling could change how much states and private plaintiffs demand from Big Tech, and whether they bother settling at all. If it survives appeal, tech regulation may increasingly be decided by state courtrooms rather than Congress.

A New Mexico judge ordered Meta to pay $25 billion to resolve claims that the company misled users about the 2018 Cambridge Analytica scandal and related incidents, The Verge reported. The figure is larger than the $17 billion multistate settlement announced this summer, which was billed at the time as unprecedented. It appears that unprecedented has a shelf life of a few months.

The order covers millions of violations of New Mexico's unfair practices law, after a jury found Meta had misled users about how it protected their data and policed misinformation and hate speech. Translation: a violation is counted individually, and when a company has a very large number of users, the arithmetic does the rest. In most other states, similar claims were folded into the settlement and resolved for $459 million.

That comparison is awkward for the states that settled. Daphne Keller of Stanford's Program in Law, Science & Technology told The Verge that if even a fraction of the award is upheld, the settling states "took pennies on the dollar compared to New Mexico." She said those attorneys general may now experience regret over the claims they waived, and start hunting for whatever they did not waive.

In fairness to the settlers, a bird in the hand has real value. The settlement offered certainty and a faster payout, though Meta will be paying over 10 years. It also secured product commitments, such as restrictions on teen usage, that a court ruling might not have delivered, and advocates say speed matters for preventing further harm to kids. Other states may also have had different laws or thinner evidence than New Mexico did.

Meta, for its part, says it is "confident we will prevail on appeal." Chief legal officer C.J. Mahoney called the New Mexico case an attempt to impose "a tax on speech." Translation: the company would like the $25 billion to be treated as a hypothetical, preferably forever. Whether the order stands up is the main variable in everything that follows.

Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier, whose state stayed out of the settlement, called the multistate deal "a slap on the wrist" on X and said Florida would not sell out to Meta. The District of Columbia, which did not settle its Cambridge Analytica claims, could be the next test of whether New Mexico's result can be repeated. School districts, individuals and tribes with outstanding claims also have fresh reason for optimism, Keller said.

The picture is not simply one of a company on the ropes. Keller noted that Meta would go broke if every case produced multipliers like this one, but that any one, or 10, or 50 of them could hit the jackpot. Colorado Law's Blake Reid pointed out that 50 states have different resources, politics and litigation skill, so Meta faces 50 small opponents that will break in different ways. He also asked whether every attorney general wants to play this game, noting that the settlement suggests maybe not.

What to watch is the appeal. If the $25 billion holds, expect settlement demands to rise, and expect the states that signed early to have some uncomfortable meetings. If it does not, the $459 million may start to look like a bargain that someone was clever enough to take.
