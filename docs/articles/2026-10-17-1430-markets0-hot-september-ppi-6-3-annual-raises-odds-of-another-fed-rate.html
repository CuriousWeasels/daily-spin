Wholesale Prices Jump 6.3%, Fed Asked to Hold Rates and Also Not

September's producer price index rose 1.3% on the month, more than double forecasts, leaving traders split on whether the Fed hikes on Oct. 28.

The wire said: Wholesale prices jumped 6.3% annually in September, biggest gain since 2022 · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 17, 2026 14:30 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The U.S. war with Iran has curtailed oil supply and pushed energy prices sharply higher, and the Fed raised rates in September under new Chairman Kevin Warsh. This week's inflation reports landed ahead of the Fed's meeting later this month.

Why it matters

Higher wholesale costs tend to reach store shelves, and a rate hike would make mortgages, car loans and credit cards costlier. Markets also face volatility if the Fed does something traders have priced as unlikely.

The producer price index, which tracks what businesses charge one another, rose 1.3% in September and 6.3% from a year earlier, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, as reported by CNBC. Economists polled by Dow Jones had forecast 0.6% and 5.5%. These were the largest monthly and annual gains since March 2022 and December 2022, respectively. Forecasters were off by roughly a factor of two, which in economics counts as a rounding error with a press release.

Translation: the PPI measures inflation before it reaches you. It is the part of the price increase currently sitting in a warehouse, waiting to be explained to a consumer.

Energy did much of the work. CNBC reported that gasoline prices rose 20%, and the BLS said almost half of September's increase in processed goods for immediate demand was tied to diesel. Brent crude stood at $126.55 in the latest data. Diesel is what moves goods, so when it gets expensive, everything that was ever on a truck gets a surcharge.

Defenders of the theory that this is merely an oil story will find it harder going. Core PPI, which strips out food and energy, rose 0.7% on the month and 5% on the year. A separate measure that also excludes trade services rose 0.5%, which CNBC said indicates inflation is broader than energy alone.

Wednesday's consumer price index was milder but still a hair above forecast: 0.6% on the month and 3.7% on the year, against expected 3.6%. Core CPI rose 0.3% on the month and 2.6% on the year. Bespoke Investment Group found the Fed's latest Beige Book, a collection of anecdotes about the economy, contained a record number of inflation-related terms. The Fed's own staff, in other words, are running out of ways to say it.

Consumers are still spending, though perhaps with less enthusiasm. Retail sales rose 0.8% in September versus 0.4% expected, but excluding autos and gas stations they rose only about 0.25%, down from roughly 1.1% a month earlier. CNBC read this as a sign that fuel costs are forcing shoppers to cut back elsewhere. Translation: people are spending more at the pump, so they are buying less of everything else.

The Fed's reaction is where the sources politely disagree with themselves. CNBC reported futures traders put the chance of an Oct. 28 hike at nearly 34% in one report and 29% in another, while a third piece cited 15% as of Friday morning, after Warsh spoke in Bangkok. Those numbers shifted over a few days, which is the market equivalent of changing your mind in public. All the reports agree that roughly 90% expect at least one hike by year-end.

Several Fed officials have said there is no hurry. Vice Chairman Philip Jefferson and New York Fed President John Williams both indicated little urgency. But a CNBC analysis noted that Warsh has repeatedly rejected the habit of telling markets what the Fed will do before it does it. He also told a reporter in September that the committee's projections were not his forecasts.

This matters because the Fed has historically signaled moves in advance so that nobody is surprised, including the people trading on them. Warsh has called the 2% inflation target "a firm, fixed target" while core PCE, the Fed's preferred gauge, is on track for about 3.1%, per estimates gathered by CNBC. The Fed funds rate sits at 3.88%, and the 10-year Treasury yields 5.1%.

The next test arrives with the PCE release later this month, then the Fed meeting on Oct. 28. Investors are betting on a pause. The chairman has so far declined to confirm it, which is his right, and their problem.
