Hundreds of AI Cloud Start-Ups Expected to Become Seven to Ten

After a week of crashing shares and a pulled IPO, executives predict a shakeout among 'neoclouds,' and Nvidia, which sells them its chips and helps guarantee their loans, has the most exposure.

The wire said: The neocloud shakeout is coming, and Nvidia has the most to lose · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 19, 2026 17:49 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

'Neoclouds' are young companies that rent out Nvidia-powered computing capacity for AI work, in competition with the large cloud providers. Hundreds have entered the market, many funded by borrowed money.

Why it matters

Nvidia's growth increasingly depends on customers outside the biggest tech companies, so trouble among neoclouds could slow chip sales and the data center buildout. A glut of computing capacity could also push AI rental prices down, which would be good for buyers and bad for anyone who borrowed to build it.

A week of falling share prices and scrapped listings has led executives in the AI cloud business to predict a shakeout. Two executives from the data center and neocloud industries told The Information that only seven to ten of the hundreds of providers now crowding in will still be around in a few years. Translation: a gold rush is being described, by participants, as a game of musical chairs.

The evidence arrived quickly. Australian provider Firmus abruptly pulled its IPO (initial public offering, the first sale of shares to the public), and The Information reported that shares of CoreWeave and smaller rivals fell about 20 percent. Bloomberg reported that CoreWeave lost a quarter of its market value, while a measure of its credit risk hit a record high. Translation: lenders now charge more to find out whether the money comes back.

Nscale, a British provider, shows how fragile things have become. The Telegraph reported that Microsoft pulled out of a deal at a Loughton, England, site where it had planned to lease 23,000 Nvidia chips, and that utilities do not expect to supply power there until the 2030s. A data center without electricity is, technically, a very expensive building. Nscale said its relationship with Microsoft remains 'strong and long-term.' Bloomberg also reported, citing people familiar with the matter, that some investors want to cut Nscale's target market value from about $30 billion to between $16 billion and $19 billion.

Nvidia cares because its largest customers are building their own chips. The segment outside the big cloud companies, which includes the neoclouds, brought in $40 billion in the July quarter, according to The Decoder. That is 45 percent of Nvidia's data center revenue and more than double the figure a year earlier. Nvidia is therefore helping its customers stay customers, through equity investments, chip allocations, loan guarantees, and customer leads. A team led by Jensen Huang's son-in-law, Nico Caprez, is even helping neoclouds find sites with available power.

The loan guarantees have been revised. One executive said larger neoclouds turned down Nvidia's first offer, which came in exchange for a share of their future revenue. Nvidia now offers shorter terms for a smaller cut. It is a rare case of a customer negotiating down the terms on which the supplier helps pay for the customer's purchases from the supplier.

Not every failure would hurt equally. The Information noted that CoreWeave mostly leases its data centers, so the sites could find new tenants. Nebius, IREN, and Nscale build their own, so financial trouble could stall construction, and Nvidia's sales with it. Analysts are not optimistic: Gartner predicts 80 percent of independent neoclouds will 'cease independent operations' by 2030, and Bernstein estimates only 35 percent of announced North American data center capacity will ever get built, according to Bloomberg.

The industry is also feuding. Bloomberg reported that CoreWeave CEO Michael Intrator blamed smaller rivals Firmus, Lambda, and Nscale for the turmoil in a memo, saying they are raising huge sums without the 'execution, revenue, scale, or software solutions' CoreWeave offers. Cirrascale CEO David Driggers expects asset managers and telecom and fiber companies to 'pick up a neocloud here, a neocloud there.' Consolidation, in other words, is a polite word for a clearance sale.

Then there is oversupply. One neocloud executive called it a given that compute supply will eventually exceed demand. Satya Nadella said on a podcast in late February 2025 that he expected a glut starting in 2027: 'prices will fall.' Microsoft then canceled or delayed projects in the UK and several US states, yet remains central to Nscale, supplying more than 40 percent of its $103 billion in contracted revenue, per The Telegraph. Watch whether the survivors number closer to seven or to ten, and who is left holding the chips.
