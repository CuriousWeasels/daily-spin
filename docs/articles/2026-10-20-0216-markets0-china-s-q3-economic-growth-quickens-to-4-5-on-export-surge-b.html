China's Economy Grows 4.5% as Factories Carry a Country That Isn't Shopping

Third-quarter growth beat forecasts on record exports, while property investment fell 20.6% and shoppers stayed home, leaving Beijing with a strong number and a lopsided economy.

The wire said: China's third-quarter economic growth quickens to 4.5% as exports surge, beating estimates · CNBC Markets

Catch up

China's economy grew 4.5% in the third quarter from a year earlier, up from a three-year low of 4.3% in the second quarter. The figure beat the 4.4% economists polled by Reuters expected and sits inside Beijing's target range of 4.5% to 5%.

Why it matters

An economy that grows by selling more abroad than its own households buy at home exports its slack to trading partners, who are already anxious about it. It also reduces Beijing's urgency to stimulate domestic spending, which is what the property-hit Chinese consumer needs.

China's economy grew 4.5% in the third quarter from a year earlier, according to CNBC, edging past the 4.4% economists had predicted. The improvement came mostly from overseas buyers of Chinese technological goods, who kept ordering while Chinese households kept not spending. It is a recovery in the sense that a car is moving when someone is pushing it from one side only.

The trade numbers did much of the lifting. China's monthly trade surplus hit a record $133.5 billion. Translation: the country sold the world $133.5 billion more than it bought in a single month, which is excellent for factory owners and a source of what CNBC called growing anxiety among trading partners, who tend to notice when the gap is theirs.

Elsewhere the picture was less cheerful. Fixed asset investment in urban areas fell 7.2% over the first nine months, and real estate development investment fell 20.6% in January to September, a deeper slide than the 19.9% recorded through August. Translation: people and companies are building and buying far less property, and since Chinese households keep much of their wealth in property, they feel poorer and spend accordingly. Sheana Yue of Oxford Economics said the data offer little sign that the downturn is bottoming out.

Retail sales rose 1.1% in September, beating a forecast of 0.6%, which is the sort of beat that needs a magnifying glass to appreciate. Industrial output grew 5.5%, exactly as forecast, and urban unemployment was 5.2%. Growth over the first nine months averaged 4.6%, comfortably inside the official range, so the target is met even if the shopping is not.

Prices tell the same story from another angle. Producer prices rose 5% in September, the fastest in more than four years, while consumer inflation stayed below 1%. Translation: factories are paying much more for inputs but cannot charge customers more, because customers are not buying enough. Yue said this leaves firms absorbing the cost shock through thinner margins.

Economists largely expect the People's Bank of China to keep benchmark lending rates unchanged this year, leaving fiscal measures to do the work. Authorities have recently announced targeted rate cuts and mortgage subsidies in late September, and allocated 550 billion yuan of unused debt quota to ease local government strains and fund infrastructure. Yue expects implementation of those measures to speed up.

The risk, as Cornell's Eswar Prasad put it, is that the government grows more sanguine and keeps a passive approach rather than pairing stimulus with reforms. A decent headline number is the one thing that makes doing less look reasonable. Watch whether Beijing treats 4.5% as a success or as a warning that happens to be in range.
