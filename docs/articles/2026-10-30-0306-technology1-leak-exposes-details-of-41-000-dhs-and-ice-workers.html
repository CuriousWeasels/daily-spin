DHS, Which Hid Its Employees' Names, Finds the Internet Has Them All

A leak dubbed Icepick exposed records on more than 41,000 Homeland Security personnel, days after officials spent months widening the definition of doxing.

The wire said: Massive leak exposes 41,000 DHS workers and undercover ICE agents · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 30, 2026 03:06 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

On Wednesday, Distributed Denial of Secrets published an archive of Department of Homeland Security staff records, which it says came from a hacking group calling itself the Department of Peace. DHS says it is aware of a potential breach, while Okta, the login provider named as the source, says it has no evidence of a compromise.

Why it matters

The leak raises real safety questions for the people named, including those in undercover roles, and sharpens a fight over whether government employees can be both anonymous and accountable. It also shows that a single login system can hold an agency's entire org chart.

Records on more than 41,000 Department of Homeland Security employees and contractors were leaked online on Wednesday, according to Ars Technica and the BBC. The archive, named Icepick, was published by the transparency nonprofit Distributed Denial of Secrets (DDoSecrets) and includes email addresses, phone numbers and office addresses. It is, in effect, a company directory that the company never agreed to publish.

The BBC reported that more than 21,000 of the people listed work for Customs and Border Protection and nearly 6,600 for Immigration and Customs Enforcement. Others come from Homeland Security Investigations, the Federal Protective Service and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. Translation: the agency whose job includes cybersecurity now has some of its cybersecurity staff in the leak.

The data also reportedly includes dozens of previously unknown operation names, the agents who led them, and some of the technology used, including products from Cellebrite and Palantir, the BBC said. Ars Technica described the material as references to internal systems and sensitive details on undercover and surveillance programs. In all, it said, the leak provides a map of how DHS is organized and how it operates.

The hackers say they pulled the data through Okta, a single sign-on service. Translation: one login that lets staff into many internal systems, which also makes it one very attractive door. DDoSecrets says the data came from DHS's Okta system. Okta told both outlets that it is secure and that it has no evidence its data or systems were compromised. Both statements can be true if the problem sits somewhere between the vendor and the customer, a space where blame traditionally goes to rest.

The timing is awkward. Ars Technica notes that since the start of the second Trump administration, DHS and the federal government have moved to hide employees' names and faces, and that the agency has stretched the word doxing to cover publishing basic facts like names and titles of public employees. The BBC added that ICE agents often work masked and rarely identify themselves despite legal requirements to do so. The agency's privacy philosophy has now been tested by people who do not accept memos.

The Department of Peace pointed to a July 17 remark by Markwayne Mullin, who became Homeland Security Secretary in March, that he was "turning up the heat." The group's spokesperson wrote that this "is what happens to ICE when you do that." DDoSecrets co-founder Emma Best said the aim was to replace "anonymity with accountability" and that the leak was not meant as a directory of every agent. Readers can judge for themselves how a 41,000-person spreadsheet differs from a directory.

DHS said it is "aware of a potential breach" and will update the public when more information is available. The BBC counted this as the third recent government data incident, after a breach attributed to ShinyHunters that took personal information about FBI agents and a suspected hacking attempt that took the Federal Election Commission offline. Ars Technica noted that arrests have been made among suspects linked to ShinyHunters.

The BBC set the leak against a year of violent immigration operations, in which ICE agents shot at more than two dozen people and killed at least nine, including Alex Pretti and Renee Good in Minnesota, neither of whom was the target of an arrest. It also cited Migration Policy Institute data showing nearly 1 million deportations since Trump returned to office, more than 70% of them of people with no criminal record. Those are the stakes on both sides of this argument: officials worried about the safety of their staff, and critics who say the public cannot hold masked agents to account.

What to watch next is what DHS confirms about how the data was taken, and whether anyone else named in the files can be protected after the fact. Once a directory is public, the usual remedy of asking nicely for it to be deleted tends to underperform.
