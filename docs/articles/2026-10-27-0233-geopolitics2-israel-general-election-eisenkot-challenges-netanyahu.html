Israel Votes Tuesday as Former General Tries Moroccan Arabic on Netanyahu's Home Turf

Gadi Eisenkot's Yashar party is courting Likud strongholds like Sderot with a slogan that mixes Hebrew and Moroccan Arabic, after years of war made this election feel unusually consequential.

The wire said: This center-right former general could unseat Netanyahu as Prime Minister in Israel · NPR World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 27, 2026 02:33 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Israel holds a general election on Tuesday, after years of war that followed the Oct. 7, 2023, attacks. Former general Gadi Eisenkot, leading the Yashar party, is the main center-right challenger to Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, according to NPR.

Why it matters

The vote will decide who leads Israel after years of war, and it tests whether a challenger can peel voters from Netanyahu's traditional base. Ethnic and class divisions that are decades old are now part of the campaign.

Israelis vote on Tuesday in a general election that NPR describes as, for many, the most consequential in decades. The main challenge to Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu comes from Gadi Eisenkot, a former general described by NPR as a center-right figure. Israel has a habit of producing elections that feel decisive. This one has the added feature of following years of war.

The campaign has produced a small linguistic experiment. According to DW, posters for Eisenkot's Yashar party in Sderot carry the slogan "Rosh Memshala Dialna," which combines the Hebrew for prime minister with the Moroccan Arabic for "of our own." Translation: the candidate would like voters to know he is one of them, and has chosen a grammar to prove it.

The location is deliberate. Sderot sits in the Negev about 75 kilometers south of Tel Aviv and a kilometer from the Gaza border, and has a large Moroccan Jewish community. DW reports that Likud won more than 40 percent of the vote there in 2022, against 23 percent nationwide. In electoral terms, that is less a swing district than a home game for the incumbent.

The reason runs back to the state's early decades. DW explains that Mizrahim, Jews mostly from Middle Eastern and North African backgrounds, make up roughly half of Israel's Jewish population, about 4 million people. Early governments led by the largely Ashkenazi Mapai party expected them to "Europeanize," and many were sent to transit camps that became underfunded development towns. Likud, founded in 1973, harvested that frustration under Menachem Begin and won power in the upheaval known as the Mahapach. DW notes the party has never had a Mizrahi leader.

Yashar has tried to turn that into an opening. DW says the party accused Likud of sidelining its Mizrahi politicians, with a slogan urging Netanyahu to let Mizrahim give interviews. Then the right-wing journalist Yinon Magal, a vocal Netanyahu supporter, claimed on live television that Eisenkot was controlled by elitist Ashkenazim and called him a "monkey." Netanyahu, who is of European origin, condemned the remark.

Former lawmaker Daniel Ben-Simon told DW that Eisenkot's embrace of his roots may have been forced on him by Magal's comments, but still carries historical weight. Using a word like "Dialna" with pride, he said, creates an Israeli-Moroccan identity, because Israeli society long tried to erase the Arab identities of North African Jews. It is an unusual campaign in which the slogan is both a pitch and a minor act of cultural repair.

Whether it works is another matter. DW reports Sderot has grown by about 5,000 people in three years to 40,000, after more than 50 people were killed there on Oct. 7, 2023. Many newcomers are young families, and the national-religious community is increasingly dominant. That group tends to vote for Bezalel Smotrich's far-right Religious Zionism party, a member of Netanyahu's coalition. Translation: Likud itself could lose votes in Sderot while Netanyahu's bloc keeps them.

So the town may be the rare place where a candidate can win the slogan battle and still lose the arithmetic. Polls open Tuesday, and the posters will have done all they can.
