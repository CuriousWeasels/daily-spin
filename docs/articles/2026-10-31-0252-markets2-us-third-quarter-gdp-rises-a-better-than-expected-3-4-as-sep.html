Economy Grows 3.4% While Prices Also Grow, Fed Promises to Keep Looking at Both

Third-quarter GDP beat forecasts and layoffs stayed near 1960s lows, but September inflation hit 3.6% a day after the Fed held rates steady.

The wire said: Third-quarter U.S. GDP increases a better-than-expected 3.4% as September inflation accelerates · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 31, 2026 02:52 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The Commerce Department reported Thursday that GDP grew at a 3.4% annualized pace in the third quarter, while its preferred inflation gauge rose 0.5% in September. The Fed had voted a day earlier to hold its benchmark rate at 3.75%-4%.

Why it matters

Strong growth combined with inflation well above the Fed's 2% target makes rate cuts harder to justify, which affects mortgage, loan and savings rates. Energy costs, which jumped in September, hit household budgets directly.

The U.S. economy grew faster than economists expected from July through September, according to Commerce Department figures reported by CNBC. GDP rose at a seasonally adjusted annualized pace of 3.4%, against a forecast of 3.1% and 2.2% in the second quarter. The same morning, the government reported that inflation was also doing well, in the sense that it was still there.

Translation: annualized means the quarter's pace is projected across a full year, so the economy did not literally grow 3.4% in three months. It grew at a speed that would produce 3.4% if it kept going. Economists use this convention because it makes every number look like a different, larger number.

The inflation figures came from the personal consumption expenditures price index, the Fed's favored gauge. Prices rose 0.5% in September, the biggest monthly rise since March, taking the annual rate to 3.6%, a tenth below forecasts. Excluding food and energy, the so-called core measure rose 0.3% on the month and 3.1% over the year, in line with expectations. Translation: core strips out the items people actually buy most often, on the theory that it shows the underlying trend more clearly.

Energy was the culprit. Gasoline and other energy goods rose 9.2% in September, and energy goods and services overall climbed 5.4%. Economists, CNBC noted, fear this could spread to other parts of the economy. Services excluding energy and housing rose 0.4%, and housing costs rose 0.3%. Brent crude was most recently listed at $123.10 a barrel.

There was some mild good news. Measured quarterly, headline PCE prices rose 2.1% and core prices 2.5%, down from 5% and 3.3% in the second quarter. The quarterly figure says prices behaved better across the summer, and the monthly one says September did not get the memo.

Growth is being driven largely by the artificial intelligence build-out and by consumers and government. Consumer spending rose 3.8% in the quarter, and private domestic investment soared 19.4%, with equipment spending up 20.9% and intellectual property products up 8.4%. Real sales to private domestic purchasers, which the Fed treats as a gauge of underlying demand, rose 4.9%. Translation: nobody is slowing down, which is wonderful news for growth and a headache for anyone trying to cool prices.

The labor market offered no relief either. Initial jobless claims fell to 194,000 for the week ended Oct. 24, below the 200,000 estimate and near levels last seen in the late 1960s. Chris Rupkey of Fwdbonds said growth is "powering ahead" despite geopolitical risks and higher energy costs.

All of this landed one day after the Fed held its benchmark rate at 3.75%-4%. Chairman Kevin Warsh said Wednesday that "the period right in front of us is information rich." CNBC observed that Thursday's data deluge suggests the Fed could have more work to do. Futures fell and Treasury yields slipped after the releases. The 10-year yield stood at 4.98% on Wednesday, and the Fed's next meeting will reveal how much information is too much.
