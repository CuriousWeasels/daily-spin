Wholesale Prices Surge 6.3%, Giving Fed Another Reason to Look Concerned

September's producer price index rose 1.3% on the month, more than double forecasts, and traders now see a one-in-three chance of a rate hike on Oct. 28.

The wire said: Wholesale prices jumped 6.3% annually in September, biggest gain since 2022 · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 16, 2026 15:38 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Energy prices have climbed since the U.S. war with Iran curtailed oil supply, and the Fed raised rates last month. This week's inflation reports are the last major readings before the midterm elections.

Why it matters

If wholesale costs keep passing through to store shelves, households face higher prices and possibly higher borrowing costs at the same time. Markets are already betting on at least one more Fed hike this year.

The producer price index rose 1.3% in September and 6.3% from a year earlier, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Thursday, according to CNBC. Economists polled by Dow Jones had expected 0.6% and 5.5%. The monthly gain was the largest since March 2022, and the annual gain the largest since December 2022, a vintage the Fed had hoped to keep in storage.

Translation: the producer price index measures what businesses receive for goods and services before they reach you. It is the price you will eventually be asked to accept, delivered in advance to the people who will be asking.

Energy did much of the damage. CNBC reported that gasoline prices rose 20%, and the BLS said almost half of the increase in processed goods for immediate demand was tied to diesel. But the problem is not only fuel. Excluding food, energy and trade services, prices still rose 0.5%, which economists cited by CNBC read as a sign that inflation has spread beyond the gas pump. Core PPI, which strips out food and energy, rose 0.7% on the month and 5% annually.

Wednesday's consumer price index was less dramatic but pointed the same way. Prices rose 0.6% on the month and 3.7% on the year, with core CPI at 0.3% and 2.6%, both a touch above forecasts. The Fed's Beige Book, a regional survey of business conditions, contained the most inflation-related terms on record, according to Bespoke Investment Group. Translation: the people being asked about the economy mostly answered with the word for what is happening to prices.

Consumers are beginning to adjust. Retail sales rose 0.8%, ahead of the 0.4% forecast, but excluding autos and gas stations they rose about 0.25%, down from roughly 1.1% a month earlier. CNBC read that as a sign that shoppers paying more for fuel are cutting back elsewhere, which is how a fuel tax collected by geopolitics shows up in a retail report.

The Fed now has a decision to make. Fed funds futures showed roughly a 29% to 34% probability of a hike on Oct. 28, depending on the source and the hour, and about 90% for at least one by year-end. Several officials have said there is no hurry, though they have suggested more hikes are needed. Chair Kevin Warsh has described one test for holding off: confidence that inflation will return to the 2% target in a timely manner. Core PCE, the Fed's preferred gauge, has been above target since 2021, and Wall Street estimates for September point to 3.1% annually.

Markets, for their part, are not panicking. The S&P 500 closed at 7744.06 on Oct. 15 and the VIX, the market's fear gauge, sat at 16.46. The 10-year Treasury yield was 5.19% and Brent crude was 126.55. Fed funds stood at 3.88%, well below both the 10-year yield and the inflation rate, which gives the central bank a visible gap to explain.

Chris Zaccarelli of Northlight Asset Management said the Fed seems to have no choice but to raise rates again this year. Nationwide's Kathy Bostjancic put real risk on a hike in two weeks. With the midterms approaching, the Fed will be deciding in public, with both parties watching, while insisting politics plays no role.
