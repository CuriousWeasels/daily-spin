Starlink Throttled in Ukraine, Europe Discovers It Has Been Renting Its Security

A 10-day slowdown of military Starlink service has Ukrainian drone makers hedging and European policymakers asking who, exactly, holds the off switch.

The wire said: Ukraine learns to fight without Starlink as Musk plays politics with satellites · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 24, 2026 14:57 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Starlink, the satellite network run by Elon Musk's SpaceX, has carried Ukrainian military communications and drone control for years. Between October 13 and October 24, military-grade service was throttled for fast-moving terminals, disrupting drone missions.

Why it matters

Governments and companies that depend on a single foreign vendor for critical communications now have a live example of that dependence being tested mid-war. Expect more spending on backups, and more sales pitches built around the word sovereignty.

According to Ars Technica, military-grade Starlink service in Ukraine was throttled starting October 13 for terminals moving faster than about 99 miles per hour (160 kilometers per hour). Ukrainian officials reported the restriction, and it reportedly began without warning in the middle of the night. Full service was restored on October 24, Ukrainian Defense Minister Yevhenii Khmara said, as reported by the Associated Press. That is roughly ten days of a critical communications link behaving like a hotel Wi-Fi network.

Translation: throttling means deliberately slowing a connection rather than cutting it. It is the technology equivalent of not locking the door but standing in the doorway. Military analyst Michael Kofman noted that in practice the service became unstable at even lower speeds, around 80 miles per hour (130 kilometers per hour). Drones, it turns out, tend to be fast.

The reported impacts included disruptions to drones that resupply frontline units, reconnaissance flights, and mid-range strike missions. Who ordered it is disputed. Ars reports the restriction was imposed reportedly at Musk's direction and potentially in consultation with President Trump, while Musk and the Trump administration each denied responsibility and pointed at the other. The Wall Street Journal reported that Trump eventually called Musk to ask that the limits be lifted, and administration officials gave contradictory accounts of who started it.

The restoration followed bipartisan calls in Congress for more oversight of Musk's so-called space monopoly and criticism from European leaders. Ars also notes this was not the first warning. In February 2023, SpaceX President Gwynne Shotwell confirmed steps to stop Ukraine's military from using Starlink with drones, after Musk commented on not allowing it for long-range strikes. In February 2026, SpaceX shut off any Ukrainian terminal not on a whitelist, which also disrupted Russian units that had been using smuggled terminals.

Ukrainian defense firms, to their credit, were already hedging. A BlueBird Tech representative told Ars, speaking anonymously for security reasons, that the company avoids making systems critically dependent on Starlink, and that Starlink is a small percentage of its product lineup. Its Zhakh FPV drones can unspool fiber-optic cable to stay connected to operators, which makes them immune to electronic warfare. Translation: when the satellite landlord gets moody, a very long piece of string does not care.

There are limits. The same representative said some configurations have no direct replacement, given Starlink's capabilities and accessibility, and described the company as exploring redundancy. Promising alternatives include ground-based LTE or 5G networks, laser-based optical links and radio mesh networks, in which drones and ground transmitters relay signals for one another. Russian jamming of Starlink on the battlefield is a further reason to diversify, so the dependence problem comes from two directions.

In a widely shared essay, a commentator writing from London argued that the episode has rattled European policymakers. The author, citing the Wall Street Journal, described the restrictions as a personal decision by one American CEO and said Europeans are now asking what happens if Microsoft, OpenAI, Anthropic, Oracle, Palantir or Google restricted access to their products. The author predicts lost revenue for American vendors, tens or even hundreds of billions of dollars, and argues that AI makes building sovereign alternatives cheaper. That is an opinion and a forecast, not a finding, but it is the sort of forecast procurement departments read closely.

The market logic is plain. Sovereignty has long been a European talking point, and a ten-day demonstration has made it a feature request. For vendors, the new sales question is who holds the switch. The most reassuring answer, as Ukraine's drone makers have quietly shown, is a cable.
