US Bans DeepSeek From Federal Computers; Investors Respond With $15 Billion

Two days after Washington ordered civilian agencies to remove the Chinese AI lab's technology, Bloomberg reports it raised roughly 100 billion yuan.

The wire said: DeepSeek reportedly raises $15B days after US restrictions · TechCrunch AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 23, 2026 15:58 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

DeepSeek, a Hangzhou-based AI lab, rattled U.S. rivals in early 2025 with a free, open-weight reasoning model. The U.S. has now ordered federal civilian agencies to remove its technology over security concerns.

Why it matters

Export-style restrictions are meant to slow a rival, but this funding suggests that U.S. government disapproval does not stop capital from flowing to Chinese AI labs. It also shows that the AI funding boom is global, which complicates the idea that any one country can set its pace.

Two days after the United States ordered federal civilian agencies to remove DeepSeek's AI technology from their systems over security concerns, the Chinese AI lab raised about $15 billion, or roughly 100 billion yuan, Bloomberg reported, as relayed by TechCrunch. It is described as one of the largest funding rounds in China. As deterrents go, a ban that is followed within 48 hours by a nine-figure-times-one-hundred cash infusion is a gentle one.

According to the report, potential investors include battery maker CATL and Tencent, the company behind WeChat, among others. Translation: a company that makes batteries and a company that makes a messaging app have both looked at a language-model lab and concluded they want in. That is what an investment boom looks like from the inside.

The round follows an earlier one in June, when DeepSeek reportedly raised more than $7 billion at a $50 billion valuation. TechCrunch also noted that the company was reportedly seeking another $1.5 billion at a valuation of around $71 billion in July, with a possible IPO in China as early as next year. Translation: valuation is what investors agree a company is worth, and in AI the agreement tends to arrive faster than the revenue.

The money is headed for AI infrastructure and open models aimed at domestic and international markets, the report said. Infrastructure here means the chips and data centers needed to train and run models. It is the unglamorous part of AI, and it is where most of the cash actually goes. The expansion could also sharpen competition among Chinese rivals, who will now have to explain to their own investors why they are not raising $15 billion.

They are not doing badly, though. TechCrunch listed Moonshot AI, the developer of Kimi, which secured $2 billion at a $20 billion valuation in May, and Manus AI, which raised more than $500 million this month. Zhipu AI and MiniMax, both listed in Hong Kong in January, have market capitalizations of roughly $29 billion and $7.2 billion. Market capitalization is simply share price times shares outstanding, and here it serves as a scoreboard.

DeepSeek was founded in 2023 by Liang Wenfeng, who previously co-founded the quantitative hedge fund High-Flyer, which helped finance the lab's early development. Corporate ownership data analyzed by WireScreen indicates Liang retains an estimated 81.6% stake in the Chinese operating entity after the earlier round. Even after raising billions, the founder still owns most of the company, which is an unusual achievement in venture capital.

DeepSeek did not immediately respond to TechCrunch's request for comment. Given the week it has had, it seems reasonable that it was busy. Watch for whether the IPO plans firm up, and whether the next U.S. restriction is timed to arrive before or after the next round.
