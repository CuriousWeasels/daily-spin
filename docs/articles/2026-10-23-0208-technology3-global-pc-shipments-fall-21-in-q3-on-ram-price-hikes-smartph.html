PC Shipments Fall 21 Percent as Memory Costs Teach Buyers Patience

Counterpoint Research says global PC shipments dropped sharply in Q3 2026 on component price hikes, and expects a steeper fall in Q4.

The wire said: PC shipments dropped over 20 percent in Q3 due to RAM price hikes · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 23, 2026 02:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Counterpoint Research reported that global PC shipments fell 21 percent year over year in the third quarter of 2026, matching earlier estimates from IDC and Omdia. Smartphone shipments also hit a 14-year low in the same quarter, according to The Verge.

Why it matters

If you were planning to buy a laptop, the price has probably gone up, the cheap models have probably shrunk in number, and the analysts say waiting may not help. Companies that make and sell computers are heading into a weak holiday quarter.

Global PC shipments fell 21 percent year over year in the third quarter of 2026, according to Counterpoint Research, as reported by The Verge. That matches earlier figures from IDC and Omdia, so the industry's three major counters of boxes have independently confirmed that fewer boxes are being bought. Until the second quarter, shipments had grown for more than two years straight.

The stated cause is component prices. RAM, SSDs, and chips from Intel, AMD, and Qualcomm have all become more expensive in recent months, and the increases have flowed into products ranging from MacBooks to Raspberry Pis. Translation: the parts inside the computer cost more, so the computer costs more, so people are buying fewer computers. Economists call this a demand curve. Everyone else calls it checking the price tag and closing the tab.

Counterpoint senior analyst Minsoo Kang noted that some companies are shifting to "prioritize configurations that can better absorb component inflation." Translation: manufacturers are steering toward pricier models where a rising memory bill is easier to hide, which leaves fewer affordable options for price-sensitive buyers. The people most sensitive to price are, by definition, the ones who notice first.

Some companies are going the other direction and reviving older technology, including DDR4 RAM and 4GB GPUs, to hold prices down. This is the hardware equivalent of responding to a grocery price spike by dusting off the pantry. It works, in the sense that a product exists, and the sense in which it is new is mostly legal.

There is a statistical wrinkle. Counterpoint says Q3 2025 was inflated by purchases ahead of the Windows 10 support deadline and by tariff-related inventory adjustments, which makes the year-over-year comparison look worse. In other words, part of the 21 percent drop is that last year's quarter was unusually strong. Part of it is that this year's quarter is simply weak. The report does not say what share belongs to which.

The outlook is not cheerful. Counterpoint associate director David Naranjo expects global shipments to plunge 27 percent in Q4 2026 and stay broadly flat in 2027, adding that "stabilization should not be mistaken for recovery." That is a polite way of saying the bottom may be flat rather than a bounce.

For buyers, the practical advice implied by the data is unglamorous: the old laptop will be asked to serve a while longer. For the industry, the quarter offers a lesson in how a market can grow for two years and then shrink by a fifth once the parts get expensive enough that the customers decline to participate.
