Central Banks Brace to Raise Rates Into Slumping Housing, Frozen Hiring, and Fuel Bills

UK inflation hit 3.5% and US housing data sank, yet markets still expect both central banks to hike, because prices are the problem that currently has a mandate.

The wire said: UK inflation jumps to 3.5% — cementing rate hike expectations · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 22, 2026 16:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

An oil-supply shock tied to the U.S.-Iran war and disruption in the Strait of Hormuz has pushed fuel prices sharply higher, reviving inflation on both sides of the Atlantic. Central banks that had hoped to be cutting rates are now weighing increases.

Why it matters

Higher policy rates tend to push up mortgage and borrowing costs just as housing is already weak and hiring is soft. Anyone with a variable-rate loan, a house to sell, or a budget to balance should expect no relief soon.

UK inflation rose to 3.5% in the 12 months to September, the Office for National Statistics said Wednesday, above the 3.4% economists polled by Reuters had forecast, according to CNBC. It was the third straight monthly rise, after 2.9% in July and 3.1% in August. The main cause was motor fuel, which rose 29.8% over the year, the sharpest jump since the aftermath of Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022.

Translation: Britain imports much of its energy, so when a distant war makes oil hard to move, British drivers receive the invoice. Markets now price a 98% chance that the Bank of England raises rates by 25 basis points (a quarter of a percentage point) to 4% next month, per LSEG data cited by CNBC. Most traders also expect another such hike in December.

The central bank is not only looking at petrol. J.P. Morgan Personal Investing strategist Scott Gardner noted that services inflation has accelerated, raising concern about so-called second-round effects. Translation: that is when higher energy costs leak into wages and prices elsewhere, and a one-time shock becomes a habit.

This lands awkwardly on the Chancellor, John Healey, who unveils his first Autumn Budget next week. Separate data showed national debt exceeded £3 trillion for the first time in September, and the cost of servicing it has risen sharply. Healey promised to maintain fiscal discipline while giving families breathing space, which is a commitment to two things that currently cost money in opposite directions. The Institute of Chartered Accountants' Suren Thiru called the Budget a perilous high-wire act.

In the United States, the Federal Reserve faces its own version of the problem. CNBC reported that pending home sales fell 7.3% in September to the lowest level in records going back to 2001, against a forecast drop of 0.4%. Housing starts fell 5.2% versus an expected 0.4% decline, and single-family permits, at 845,000, were the lowest since 2023. Mortgage rates ended September near 7.6%, according to Mortgage News Daily, against about 3% five years ago.

Normally, a housing slump would encourage a central bank to cut rates. But wholesale goods prices rose 2.6%, the fastest pace in a data series going back to 2009, and the latest official CPI reading stands at 3.74%. Goldman Sachs economist Ronnie Walker pointed to the lock-in effect: more than 95% of mortgage borrowers hold a rate below the current market, so they stay put. Translation: homeowners will not trade a cheap loan for an expensive one, so no one sells, and no one can buy what no one sells.

The labor market offers little counterweight. Initial jobless claims fell to 194,000, near the lowest since 1969, yet payrolls grew only 29,000 in September. Employers are neither hiring nor firing, a state economists call low-hire, low-fire and workers call staying exactly where they are. Markets expect the Fed to hold steady next week, with a quarter-point hike likely in December and another in March 2027, per CME's FedWatch tool.

One hopeful note came from NAR chief economist Lawrence Yun, who suggested mortgage rates may be topping out, since they have already risen more than the Fed's own moves. If so, buyers and sellers may emerge from hiding. Until then, the central banks will keep fighting inflation, and housing will keep serving as the collateral damage.
