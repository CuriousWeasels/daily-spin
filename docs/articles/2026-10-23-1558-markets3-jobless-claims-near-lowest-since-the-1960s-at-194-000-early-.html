Employers Refuse to Fire Anyone, Also Refuse to Hire Anyone, Call It Stability

Weekly jobless claims fell to 194,000, near a 57-year low, even as payrolls grew by just 29,000 in September and the Fed eyes another rate hike.

The wire said: Jobless claims near the lowest since the 1960s as low-fire trend continues · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 23, 2026 15:58 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The US labor market has settled into a pattern economists call low-hire, low-fire: companies keep the staff they have but add few new ones. Meanwhile inflation remains well above the Federal Reserve's 2% target, and markets expect further rate increases.

Why it matters

If you already have a job, the data say it is probably safe; if you are looking for one, the data are less encouraging. Steady employment also gives the Fed room to keep fighting inflation, which means borrowing costs may keep rising.

Initial claims for unemployment insurance fell to a seasonally adjusted 194,000 for the week ended Oct. 17, the Labor Department reported Thursday, according to CNBC. That is just above the 189,000 recorded in the week of July 18, the lowest since September 1969. Put plainly, almost nobody is being laid off, which the economy is treating as good news and a hiring freeze as a separate matter.

Translation: initial claims count people who file for unemployment benefits for the first time in a given week. A low number means few people are losing jobs. It says nothing about whether anyone is being hired, a distinction the current labor market is demonstrating with some commitment.

The hiring side is less cheerful. Nonfarm payrolls rose by only 29,000 in September and have averaged 69,000 a month in 2026, CNBC reported. The unemployment rate rose to 4.2%, though the report attributes that largely to a sharp jump in labor force participation. Translation: more people started looking for work, and not all of them found it yet, which makes the rate go up for a fairly encouraging reason.

Claims have averaged 212,000 over the past year, and the four-week moving average is now 197,750. The layoff rate over the prior 12 months fell to 1% of the workforce, down 0.2 percentage point, while job openings were roughly flat. Employers, in short, have reached a stable arrangement with their workforces: you stay, we do not expand.

Thomas Simons, chief U.S. economist at Jefferies, noted in a client note that claims data suggest stable conditions despite gloomy consumer confidence surveys, headlines about job cuts, and what he called the "phantom job-stealing power of AI." The phrase is worth savoring: the technology blamed for taking jobs has so far not shown up in the unemployment line.

The data matter beyond bragging rights. The week in question is the reference week the Bureau of Labor Statistics uses for its payrolls estimate, so low claims hint at a tolerable October jobs report, due Nov. 6. CNBC also noted the Fed meets next week, and officials have recently described the labor market as a source of strength while worrying more about inflation.

That worry has numbers behind it. Consumer prices were up 3.74% from a year earlier as of the latest reading, against a 2% target, and Brent crude sits at $115.22 a barrel. The Fed funds rate is 3.88%, the 2-year Treasury yields 4.81%, and the 10-year yields 5.13%. Markets expect the Fed to hold in October and raise by a quarter percentage point in December, according to CNBC.

MarketWatch, for its part, headlined its own take on the economy's fourth-quarter start as good, with one big problem: warning signs that consumers will be hit with rising prices. That is the other half of the story. A strong job market is what lets the Fed tighten without much worry about the damage, which is cheerful for the labor statistics and less so for anyone with a variable-rate loan.

Watch the Fed next week and the jobs report on Nov. 6. If employers keep not firing people, the central bank will keep not worrying about it.
