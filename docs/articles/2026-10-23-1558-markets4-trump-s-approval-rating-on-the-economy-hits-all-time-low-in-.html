Trump's Economic Approval Hits Record Low, Uniting Americans Mostly Around Disliking Groceries

CNBC's latest survey shows 34% approve of the president's handling of the economy, the weakest of either term, with two weeks left before the midterms.

The wire said: Trump's approval rating on economy hits all-time low as midterm looms: CNBC survey · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 23, 2026 15:58 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

CNBC's All-America Economic Survey, released Thursday, polled 1,000 registered voters ahead of the Nov. 3 congressional elections. It found economic pessimism rising, including among Republicans, and a 7-point Democratic edge on which party should control Congress.

Why it matters

Prices, not policy abstractions, are driving the mood: 80% say prices are rising, and official data show inflation at 3.74% with Brent crude at $115.22. If voters act on that gloom, control of Congress and the direction of economic policy could change.

According to CNBC, President Donald Trump's approval rating on the economy has fallen to 34%, with 65% disapproving. That is the lowest of either of his terms, and the survey says the 65% disapproval is the highest for any president CNBC has polled going back to Barack Obama. His net economic approval is -31, which is what happens when you subtract the people who like something from the people who do not and the second number wins by a lot.

The drop is sharp. In July, 38% approved and 60% disapproved, so the president lost 4 points of approval and gained 5 points of disapproval in three months. Translation: net approval is approval minus disapproval, so small movements on both sides add up to a large-looking gap. His overall approval is steady at 40% to 59%, which means voters have apparently decided to dislike the economy specifically and the president generally, in separate compartments.

The more notable shift is within the president's own party. Overall, 63% of Americans are pessimistic about the economy and the outlook, up 1 point from July, but that group now includes 35% of Republicans, up 6 points and the highest of either Trump term. Micah Roberts, the survey's Republican pollster, said the change came "because Republicans have" soured, not independents or Democrats. Eight in 10 respondents rate the economy fair or poor, the highest since December 2023.

The cause appears to be the grocery receipt. Eighty percent say prices are rising, up 5 points from a year ago, and 55% say they are rising faster than usual. Food now worries people more than gas, 34% to 23%, and combined concern about food, gas and housing is the top issue for 57%. For context, the latest official CPI reading is 3.74% year over year, meaning a basket that cost $100 a year ago costs about $103.74 now, before anyone mentions the cheese.

Gas is its own story. Brent crude, the global oil benchmark, sat at $115.22 in the latest data. CNBC reports only 35% say the Iran war was worth it given the rise in gasoline prices, and 46% say it was worth it to disrupt Iran's nuclear development, down from 53% in April. Democrats now lead on Iran by 5 points, compared with a 2-point Republican lead before.

On the ballot question, Americans prefer Democratic control of Congress 52% to 45%, a 3-point gain for Democrats since July. Independents lean Democratic 49% to 33%, and 72% of Democrats are highly interested in the election versus 62% of Republicans. Democrats now lead by 16 points on food and grocery costs, up from 7. Republicans keep an edge on border security and immigration, though it shrank to 9 points from 15.

The one thing that unites the country is corruption. Ninety-three percent say elected officials should not be allowed to own or trade individual stocks of companies with federal contracts, and 89% say companies owned by or invested in by a sitting president's immediate family should not get federal contracts. In 2026, that is about as close to consensus as a national poll can get without asking about the weather.

Both pollsters urged caution. Roberts framed it as a contest between "the map and momentum," noting Democrats would need to flip many districts and states Trump won handily. Jay Campbell, the Democratic pollster, said he does not see a realignment election like 1994 or 2008, but a return to the mean. Translation: the wave may be real, but it still has to cross terrain drawn by someone else.

The survey has a margin of error of 3.1 points, so every number above could wobble in either direction. What will not wobble before Nov. 3 is the price of groceries, which is presumably the point.
