Microsoft and Japan Shut Down Scam Supermarket That Sold Fake Accounts by the Billion Message

Microsoft says it disabled more than 20,000 accounts tied to YAM Store, a marketplace that supplied scammers with accounts and phishing kits used in over a billion malicious messages since January 2026.

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 15, 2026 12:56 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Phishing is the practice of sending fake messages that impersonate banks, delivery firms or police to trick people into handing over credentials or money. Microsoft, working with Japan's National Police Agency, traced a large share of this activity to a marketplace called YAM Store and two phishing kits, CoGUI and Iron Man.

Why it matters

The fake parking ticket and missing package texts people receive are often produced by industrial supply chains, not lone hackers, so taking apart one supplier can reduce volume for a while. It will not end the problem, but it shows the most effective target may be the infrastructure that makes scams easy to buy.

Microsoft said it helped dismantle a marketplace that supplied scammers with the tools to send phishing messages around the world, according to The Verge. Working with Japan's National Police Agency, the company traced scam activity to YAM Store and two phishing kits named CoGUI and Iron Man. The operation, in short, was a one-stop shop for fraud, which is more customer service than most legitimate companies manage.

Translation: a phishing kit is a ready-made bundle for impersonating a trusted organization, including the fake login page, the message text and the machinery to send it. Think of it as flat-pack furniture for crime, with the instructions in better condition than most. The Verge reported that Iron Man alone offered more than 2,300 templates for posing as organizations such as banks and police departments.

The numbers are large. Since January 2026, Microsoft says, the three platforms enabled more than one billion malicious messages aimed at over 75,000 organizations in more than 150 countries. More than 750 million of those messages targeted Microsoft customers. The targets included consumers, healthcare organizations and financial institutions, and the operators made tens of millions of dollars.

YAM Store was the supply depot. According to Microsoft's report, scammers could buy stolen or fraudulent Microsoft and Google accounts there, then use the kits to send their messages. Accounts from real, established providers matter because they make a message look more legitimate and are more likely to get past filters. Translation: the scam works better when it comes from an address that looks like it belongs to someone.

The marketplace also manufactured its own inventory. Microsoft says YAM Store hijacked expired web domains belonging to real organizations and built websites for organizations that do not exist, then used those to obtain Microsoft accounts it could resell. This is a business model in which a lapsed website renewal becomes raw material, a risk few IT departments list on the renewal reminder.

The response had several parts. Microsoft says it disabled more than 20,000 accounts connected to YAM Store and blocked more than 100,000 web domains used for impersonation and fraud. Together with Health-ISAC, a health sector cybersecurity non-profit, it also seized and redirected the web domains powering all three platforms. Translation: visitors who type in the old addresses no longer reach the criminals' storefront.

The sources do not say whether anyone was arrested, nor how long the disruption is expected to last. Marketplaces like this tend to be replaceable, since the demand for cheap phishing tools does not vanish when one shop closes. For now, the fake parking tickets may thin out. Watch for whether they come back under new branding, which in this industry is usually a matter of weeks.
