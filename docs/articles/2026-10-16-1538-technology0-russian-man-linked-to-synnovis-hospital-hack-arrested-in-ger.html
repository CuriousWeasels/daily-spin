Alleged Qilin Hacker Arrested on Holiday in Japan, Proving Vacations Are Not Jurisdiction-Free

German police are adding the 2024 Synnovis hospital attack to their case against a 28-year-old Russian, as British police weigh whether an unlawful killing charge is possible.

The wire said: Russian man linked to deadly London hospitals hack arrested · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 16, 2026 15:38 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

In June 2024, the Qilin gang attacked Synnovis, a blood-testing provider serving London hospitals, disrupting care and eventually leaking patient data. King's College Hospital later confirmed a patient death with a long wait for a blood test result among the contributing factors.

Why it matters

Ransomware is usually treated as a financial crime, but a possible unlawful killing case would treat it as a threat to patient safety. Arrests are rare because Russia does not cooperate with Western police, so travel plans matter more than most suspects would like.

German police have detained a 28-year-old Russian man, named by German press as Vladimir K, and the BBC can confirm investigators are now including the 2024 Synnovis hack in their inquiry. He is accused of being a core member of Qilin, a cyber-crime gang that has allegedly hacked more than 4,000 organisations since 2024 and extorted more than $140m (£105m). It is a large sum for a business whose product is, essentially, other people's files held hostage.

According to German officials, he was arrested by Japanese police in May when he entered Japan on what was meant to be a holiday. His plans were intercepted before he arrived. The BBC reports that it is very rare for hackers to be arrested in Russia, because the Russian government refuses to cooperate with Western law enforcement requests. Translation: the safest place for a ransomware suspect is home, and the riskiest is anywhere with a beach and an extradition treaty.

The case Germany originally wanted him for is modest by the gang's standards. He is wanted over a September 2024 attack on a logistics company, in which data was encrypted and about $160,000 in cryptocurrency was demanded. Encrypting means scrambling a victim's files so they cannot be opened without a key, which the criminals then offer to sell back. His extradition was first reported in early October, and the Synnovis link is the new development.

Synnovis is a pathology partnership between hospitals and the German company Synlab. After Qilin struck on 3 June 2024, a critical incident was declared at some London hospitals. Blood transfusion services were severely disrupted, operations were cancelled and emergency patients were diverted elsewhere. In total, more than 10,000 appointments were disrupted, with weeks of knock-on trouble.

King's College Hospital NHS Foundation Trust, one of the two most affected, confirmed a year later that a patient had "died unexpectedly". A spokesperson said a number of contributing factors led to the death, including "a long wait for a blood test result". This made it the first cyber attack linked directly to a death, according to the BBC. It also complicates the usual defence of the genre, which holds that nobody was really harmed, only a server.

The hackers threatened to publish the data unless a ransom was paid. None was, and they published the highly private data online anyway. They told the BBC at the time that the attack was not only about money but also a protest over the UK's lack of help in Russia's war on Ukraine. The BBC notes that they did not say whether they were from Russia or Ukraine, and that the claim was dismissed as an attempt to distance themselves from Qilin's known extortion tactics. Extortion with a political statement attached is still extortion.

Separately, the BBC has learned that British police are examining whether its own inquiry could lead to an unlawful killing case, potentially a world first in cyber-crime. Qilin, tracked since 2022, has also been linked to attacks on schools, councils, companies and health organisations, and to the October 2025 hack of Japanese brewer Asahi. The suspect's holiday destination, in a country that has now been involved in both stories, was an unfortunate itinerary choice. What to watch next is whether prosecutors can turn a technical crime into a charge that carries the weight of a death.
