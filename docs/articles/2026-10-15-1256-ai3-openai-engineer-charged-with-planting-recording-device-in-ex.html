OpenAI Engineer Allegedly Bugged Executive Room With Toilet Paper and a Chatbot

Abdulrahman Alfozan, 35, faces up to five years in prison after the FBI said he hid a recording device in a restricted OpenAI conference room, apparently with help from an AI chatbot.

The wire said: Recording device hidden in toilet paper found in top-secret OpenAI meeting room · Ars Technica AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 15, 2026 12:56 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The FBI arrested an OpenAI engineer on October 9, alleging he planted an audio recorder in a leadership-only conference room. OpenAI says it found the device within two days and worked with investigators before firing him.

Why it matters

AI labs hold trade secrets that governments now describe as national security assets, and this case shows how much of that protection rests on locked doors, camera placement and HR timing. It also shows that the products these companies sell can be used to build the tools that threaten them.

The FBI has arrested Abdulrahman Alfozan, a 35-year-old Saudi national and US permanent resident, on suspicion of hiding a recording device in a restricted executive conference room at OpenAI. According to Ars Technica and The Decoder, the room is where leadership discusses technical progress, government engagements and commercial partnerships. Alfozan, an engineer at the company for more than a year by Ars Technica's count, allegedly lacked clearance to enter it. The sources differ slightly on his tenure: The Decoder says he started in July 2025.

The concealment method, according to the FBI, was to wrap a USB device in toilet paper and plug it into the back of a television. Translation: the most advanced security perimeter in artificial intelligence was allegedly defeated by a bathroom supply. The device was likely planted on September 28, and OpenAI found it on September 30, after about two days, and determined that its contents were encrypted.

OpenAI then did something that looks counterintuitive but is standard in investigations: nothing visible. Rather than escort him out, it gradually cut his access to systems while working with the FBI to analyze the device. On October 5 it installed a camera in the room, and the footage allegedly showed Alfozan trying to retrieve the device that same day. Translation: the company set a trap, and the suspect walked into it on opening day.

The most modern detail comes from the indictment, as reported by The Decoder. A chatbot account linked to Alfozan's wife was allegedly used between September 24 and 30 to ask how to build a continuous audio recorder, adjust Wi-Fi connectivity, improve live listening and encrypt the SD card. The account reportedly shared several identifiers with his own, and prompts even referenced the recovered device's MAC address. Translation: a MAC address is a device's hardware serial number, and typing it into a chatbot is the digital equivalent of signing the work. The Decoder summed it up as the engineer apparently vibe-coding his spy gear. It was not specified which chatbot was used.

OpenAI timed his firing on Friday to coincide with the arrest. The company said there were, in its words, "no indications of this individual inappropriately accessing or exfiltrating model weights, source code, or customer data." Translation: the crown jewels, meaning the trained AI models themselves, appear to be intact. A second recording device was reportedly found in his bag, according to The Decoder.

The case lands in a political context. FBI Director Kash Patel called protecting American "super intelligence innovations" one of the bureau's highest counterintelligence priorities. The Decoder also noted that OpenAI's ties to Gulf states have deepened as its appetite for capital and infrastructure has grown, with the UAE's MGX appearing as a partner or backer, including in Stargate, and a Stargate UAE project announced in May 2025. The sources do not allege any connection between Alfozan and any government or investor, and the FBI's account describes a suspected attempt to spy on the company and risk leaking trade secrets.

If convicted of violating laws against unlawful recordings, Alfozan faces up to five years in prison and a $250,000 fine. The charges are allegations, not findings. What to watch is whether prosecutors say who the recordings were for, and whether other labs quietly start checking the backs of their televisions.
