Moody's Spares France a Downgrade, Keeps Its Negative Outlook as a Souvenir

The ratings agency held France at Aa3 while borrowing costs sit at a 2002 high and protesters plan more marches.

The wire said: What next for France as riots rattle markets? Three questions facing investors ahead of more protests · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 24, 2026 14:57 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

France has had a week of unrest over public services and living costs, while its 10-year bond yield rose above 5% for the first time since 2002. Investors were watching Moody's, since S&P Global and Fitch already cut France's rating in 2025.

Why it matters

France's borrowing costs help set the tone for euro zone debt, and a government that cannot pass a budget tends to pay more to borrow. Higher yields eventually feed into bank lending, company investment and the price of keeping the state running.

Moody's on Friday kept France's credit rating at Aa3, which it still calls high grade, and left its outlook negative, CNBC reported. In rating-agency terms, this is the equivalent of telling a student he has not failed, but that the teacher will be keeping an eye on him.

Translation: an outlook is the agency's hint about which way the next move might go. Negative means a downgrade is more likely than an upgrade. Moody's said the reason is that the fragmentation of France's political landscape will keep impairing how its legislative institutions function. Moody's said it could return the outlook to stable if institutions can narrow the deficit.

The timing was awkward. According to CNBC, the yield on benchmark 10-year French bonds, known as OATs, rose 11 basis points on Thursday to its highest since 2002, and passed 5% this week. Translation: a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, and the yield is the interest rate investors demand for lending to the government. The gap over German Bunds, the euro zone's safe benchmark, is the widest since November 2011. That gap is, roughly, the price of doubting Paris.

The arithmetic behind the doubt is plain. CNBC reported the deficit is about 5.5% of GDP, against a 3% limit for EU members, and that more than $1 trillion of debt matures by 2030. The finance ministry warned a month ago that debt could reach 121.7% of GDP in 2027. The government aims to cut the deficit to 5% in 2027 from a projected 5.4% in 2026. Sources differ slightly on the current figure, which is the sort of discrepancy that matters less when every version is above the limit.

The budget must still pass a National Assembly where opposition lawmakers are resisting the plans. Debate on the tax measures resumes Oct. 27. Alain Durré of Natixis said the government could use Article 49.3, which pushes a bill through without a final vote unless a no-confidence motion succeeds, or executive ordinances. Matthew Ryan of Ebury was less sure, saying that even a passing budget may be so riddled with concessions to both left and right that the deficit stays insufficiently addressed. A budget that fixes nothing is still a budget, which is its own kind of progress.

Meanwhile the streets are busy. More than 500,000 people marched last weekend over declining public services and high living costs, CNBC reported. More demonstrations are planned for Oct. 24, with a union-backed national day of strikes and protests on Nov. 5. ING's Charlotte de Montpellier noted that past episodes cost little: the Yellow Vest movement may have shaved 0.1 percentage point off fourth-quarter 2018 GDP, and the 2023 pension strikes about the same. The danger, she said, is a lasting loss of confidence. ING had already expected growth of just 0.3% in 2026.

Companies are feeling it. The CAC 40 has fallen 4.5% over the past month, and Carrefour shares dropped more than 14% on Wednesday after it cut its profit outlook, citing the unrest and rising energy costs. CEO Alexandre Bompard called the situation unprecedented. ING, citing chambers of commerce, said more than 2,000 businesses were directly affected. Luxury is better insulated: France is only 8% of LVMH's 2025 revenue, though tax-free tourist purchases could suffer.

Natixis's Durré argued that the recent bond selling has come mainly from tactical reallocations, while traditional buy-and-hold investors are still sitting on the sidelines. That is mildly reassuring, in the way it is reassuring that the people who might leave the building have not yet left. Watch Oct. 27 for the budget and Nov. 5 for the strikes. Carmignac's Kevin Thozet said a further six months of political vacuum is a very long time for markets.
