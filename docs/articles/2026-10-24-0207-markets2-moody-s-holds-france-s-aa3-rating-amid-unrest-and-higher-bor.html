Moody's Keeps France at Aa3, Declines to Join Everyone Else in Downgrading

The agency held France's rating but kept a negative outlook, as 10-year yields passed 5% for the first time since 2002 and protesters filled the streets.

The wire said: Moody's holds steady on France's credit rating as country grapples with turmoil and higher yields · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 24, 2026 02:07 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

France has been hit by political fragmentation, a deficit well above EU limits and a week of protests, pushing its borrowing costs sharply higher. S&P Global and Fitch already cut their ratings on France in 2025.

Why it matters

France is the euro zone's second-largest economy, so its borrowing costs affect the whole currency bloc, including the euro's value and what other governments pay to borrow. If investors lose patience, the bill eventually reaches taxpayers as spending cuts, tax rises or both.

Moody's said on Friday that it would keep France's credit rating at Aa3, which it still calls "high grade," while retaining a negative outlook. In ratings terms, this is the equivalent of a doctor telling a patient he may leave the building but should not make any long-term plans.

Translation: a negative outlook means the agency thinks a future downgrade is more likely than an upgrade. Moody's explained that the outlook reflects the risk that the fragmentation of French politics will keep impairing how the legislature functions. Moody's added that political instability, "also visible in the current social tensions," could weaken France's credit metrics faster than it currently expects, according to CNBC.

The numbers explain the caution. CNBC reported that France's 10-year bond yield rose above 5% this week for the first time since 2002. Translation: the French government now pays investors 5% a year to lend it money for a decade, which is a reasonable price only if you are not the borrower. The deficit is roughly 5.5% of GDP against an EU limit of 3%, and more than $1 trillion of debt matures by 2030. The finance ministry warned a month ago that debt could reach 121.7% of GDP in 2027.

The yield gap with Germany matters as much as the yield itself. CNBC reported that the spread between French and German 10-year bonds topped 170 basis points on Thursday, the first time since the early-2010s euro zone debt crisis. Translation: a basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point, so investors now demand 1.7 percentage points more to lend to Paris than to Berlin. Credit default swaps, the price of insuring against a French default, are also at their highest since that period.

Meanwhile, more than 500,000 people marched last weekend over declining public services and the cost of living, per CNBC. This creates a familiar bind: bond markets want spending restraint, voters want services, and the legislature that would have to settle the argument is the very thing Moody's is worried about.

Analysts are divided on how scary this is. ING's Carsten Brzeski said, "We are deep into French crisis territory," though he saw no imminent liquidity problem. Forvis Mazars' George Lagarias argued France is already in a debt crisis, with debt at 119% of GDP, but put the chance of a broader spillover like the early 2010s at 10% to 20%. His reasoning: French debt levels are worrying but known, and markets can handle known risks. As he put it, they hate surprises.

The reassurances rest on structure. Aberdeen's Alex Everett pointed to stronger banks and more tools to limit fragmentation than in the early 2010s, and expects contagion to be short-lived. Ninety One's John Stopford said spreads would probably need to reach the 200s before this looks like a genuine crisis, though he said France "doesn't look great." Spreads are already wider than at the peak of the earlier peripheral crisis, but well below the extremes Spain, Italy, Greece and Ireland hit then.

Moody's said it could return the outlook to stable if institutions manage to narrow the deficit. That gives Paris a clear target and a legislature that has struggled to agree on lunch. The next thing to watch is whether the spread crosses 200 basis points.
