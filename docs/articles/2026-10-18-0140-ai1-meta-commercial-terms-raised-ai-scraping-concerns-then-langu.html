Meta Clarifies It Never Meant What Its Terms Said, Then Deletes What Its Terms Said

After legal experts warned that new business terms could let Meta train AI on publishers' sites, the company removed the language and said nothing had changed.

The wire said: Meta’s new terms may allow it to train AI on news sites for free · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 18, 2026 01:40 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Meta updated its commercial terms, set to take effect October 30th, to let it crawl websites associated with business accounts and use their content to "train" its AI products, according to Nieman Lab. After criticism, Meta removed the sentence.

Why it matters

Publishers rely on robots.txt files and licensing deals to control whether AI companies can use their work. The episode shows how much of that control rests on contract wording that can change with a few paragraphs of legal text.

Meta has deleted a sentence from its commercial terms that legal experts said could have let it scrape news sites to train AI, The Verge and TechCrunch reported. The sentence let Meta use any content from domains, web pages, or URLs associated with a business account to "develop, improve, research," and "train" its products. Meta now says the terms never changed how it uses web crawlers, which raises the question of why the sentence was there to begin with.

Nieman Lab first flagged the language, which applied to businesses promoting content across Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, and Messenger. Translation: if you buy ads or run a business account, you authorize Meta's bots to visit websites tied to your products or services. The terms listed the bots generously: scrapers, spiders, crawlers, and user agents, which is most of the insect kingdom of the internet.

The worry was robots.txt, the small text file where a website tells crawlers what they may not touch. James Grimmelmann, a professor of digital and information law at Cornell, told Nieman Lab the terms were clear permission for Meta to train AI on any content from publishers' sites. Legal experts also said Meta could argue its commercial terms outrank a site's blocking instructions. Translation: the sign on the door says keep out, but the contract you clicked says come on in.

Meta's first response was that nothing had changed. Spokesperson Daniel Roberts said that "any suggestion to the contrary is false." Andy Stone, the company's vice president of communications, wrote on X that the update was meant to pull accurate product information from third-party websites for ad recommendations, not to scrape news sites. That is a plausible purpose, and it is also the kind of explanation that works better when it appears in the contract.

Context made the wording harder to wave away. Meta has struck AI licensing deals with CNN, Fox News, USA Today, and People Inc., so some publishers are being paid for what the terms might have granted for free. Last month Meta also launched Meta One, a subscription that requires businesses and creators to pay to include links in posts and reels. Nieman Lab summed it up: Meta charges users to post links to content it would be allowed to crawl for free.

By October 17th, Meta had removed the training sentence. Roberts said, "We've heard you and want to clarify," and that the crawling terms applied only to ads and business products. He added that the updates do not give Meta new rights that supersede robots.txt preferences. TechCrunch noted that Meta won a high-profile copyright lawsuit last year, though other cases against AI companies, Meta included, continue.

The sentence is gone, and Meta says it was never doing anything. Publishers now have a company statement promising that, which is useful the next time they read the terms.
