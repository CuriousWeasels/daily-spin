Russia Proposes Peace Summit on Ukraine, Invites Everyone Except Ukraine, Europe

Moscow wants Trump and Xi at a Shenzhen table on November 18-19, with Kyiv and Brussels left to read about it afterward.

The wire said: Trump, Putin and Xi at one table, without Ukraine. What Moscow wants from the proposal and why Beijing might agree. · DW

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 23, 2026 02:08 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The Trump administration has spent the autumn trying to revive stalled talks to end Russia's war in Ukraine, offering Moscow concessions including a diesel export deal and temporary sanctions easing. Moscow has responded with a counterproposal about who should be in the room.

Why it matters

A deal on Ukraine's future negotiated without Ukraine or the EU would set a precedent for how wars are settled, and Europe, which funds much of Kyiv's resistance, would be asked to pay for a result it didn't help write.

The Wall Street Journal reported this week that Russia has proposed a three-way meeting of Donald Trump, Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping on the sidelines of the APEC summit in Shenzhen, China, scheduled for November 18-19. Ukraine and the European Union are not on the guest list. This is the sort of peace conference where the country being discussed is treated as a topic rather than an attendee.

According to the Journal, Putin sees the gathering as a possible new Yalta, a reference to the 1945 meeting of the American, British and Soviet leaders on the postwar order. Translation: Moscow would like history to remember this as a conference of great powers deciding things, and would prefer it not mention the smaller country involved. Kremlin aide Yury Ushakov said Putin shared thoughts on the idea with US envoy Steve Witkoff in St. Petersburg on October 21, and that Beijing is already preparing the meeting.

The same meeting produced a more modest announcement. Ushakov said Putin agreed to resume US-mediated talks after November 10, possibly in Abu Dhabi, with a focus on what he called "building the security system for Russia and Ukraine after the end of the war." DW noted that the Kremlin appears in no hurry for meaningful talks, and that the next round would be technical, which in diplomacy means people with folders in a room while the leaders decide whether to start.

The Journal reported that Chinese officials signaled through diplomatic channels that they would not object to the format if Trump agrees to attend. Trump, whose relations with Kyiv have deteriorated significantly in recent weeks, according to DW, has expressed openness. Volodymyr Zelenskyy said in Oslo on October 20 that talks on Ukraine's future are acceptable only if Ukraine has a seat at the table, a position that is, at least, consistent with how tables work.

Why would Beijing say yes? The experts DW consulted disagree. Temur Umarov of the Carnegie Russia Eurasia Center argues China would be a symbolic participant: "Beijing has no intention of taking responsibility for Ukraine's future." He expects China to seek vague, noncommittal language in any joint statement so it could describe almost any later development as consistent with it. Translation: a communique drafted to be reread favorably in any weather.

Yurii Poita of MERICS sees more overlap. He argues Moscow and Beijing share an interest in reducing Western military presence and weakening NATO, and that persuading Washington to cut military aid to Ukraine would hand Russia battlefield advantages and erode European trust in the US. Michael Kimmage of the Kennan Institute adds that what matters most to China is Putin's political survival, since Russia supplies cheap energy and a partner that rarely stands in Beijing's way. He does not expect China to press Putin to end the war.

The excluded parties have their own theory of the case. Kateryna Krasina of the New Geopolitics Network reads the format as a sign that China doesn't regard Ukraine as an independent player and considers Europe relatively weak. Kimmage counters that Europe's financial aid and defense industrial cooperation give it leverage, and says European efforts around the 2025 Alaska summit helped head off outcomes unfavorable to the continent. In his words: "And that support equals leverage."

NPR meanwhile noted that Witkoff visited Moscow after meeting Zelenskyy, and asked former US ambassador to Ukraine William Taylor about Trump's embrace of Russia. The shuttle itinerary is its own summary of the format: Kyiv gets a meeting, Moscow gets the planning. Whether Shenzhen happens will depend on whether Trump accepts an invitation to a conference whose main feature is an empty chair he is not obliged to fill.
