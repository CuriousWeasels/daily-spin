Saudi Arabia Postpones 'Davos in the Desert' as Houthi Missiles Disrupt the Networking

The Future Investment Initiative, due to open October 26, is pushed to 2027 while Riyadh's government workers are told to stay home and keep working as normal.

The wire said: Saudi Arabia cancels 2026 ‘Davos in the Desert’ amid Houthi attacks · Al Jazeera

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 20, 2026 02:16 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Fighting between a Saudi-led coalition and Iran-backed Houthi forces in Yemen has escalated since July, when a four-year informal ceasefire began to unravel. The Houthis have since hit Saudi airports, energy sites and military bases.

Why it matters

Saudi Arabia is trying to raise billions from foreign investors to move its economy beyond oil, and that pitch is harder to make while the capital's airport is a target. For investors and employers, the postponement is a data point on how the kingdom's own officials rate the risk.

Saudi Arabia has postponed its Future Investment Initiative conference, informally called 'Davos in the Desert', until 2027, according to a statement on the event's website. The gathering was scheduled for October 26 to 29, and a new date will be announced 'very soon'. The statement did not use the word 'missiles'.

The organizers' explanation, per Al Jazeera, came from FII Institute chief executive Dr Maha Bint Mishari Al Saud, who cited a commitment to partners and delegates. Because this is the 'landmark tenth edition', she said, the event should deliver 'the full scale, extraordinary quality, and unforgettable impact' attendees expect. Translation: a tenth anniversary is a poor occasion for a security briefing in the lobby.

The conference matters because it is the kingdom's main sales event. It is a state-backed showcase for plans to attract the billions needed to shift the economy away from hydrocarbons and toward tourism, sport and technology. According to the FII website, expected speakers included Ruth Porat of Alphabet, David Solomon of Goldman Sachs and Laurence Fink of BlackRock. Few investors will give a keynote about stability when the venue's airport is on the target list.

The backdrop is a deteriorating war. Al Jazeera reports that the Houthis, also called Ansar Allah, have hit military, energy and civilian infrastructure across Saudi Arabia, including Riyadh's international airport, killing at least 18 people and injuring hundreds. The BBC puts the toll from attacks on the kingdom's airports at least 19 since the start of October, a discrepancy that likely reflects differing counts and dates rather than any shortage of attacks.

Riyadh is also acting as if the risk is real. Government agencies in the capital told staff to work from home, according to a worker and two consultants who spoke to AFP. The Kingdom Centre tower, which houses the International Monetary Fund's regional office, asked workers to stay away until further notice. Firms in the King Abdullah Financial District, home to offices of major US companies, did the same, per other sources.

One government message announced remote work from Monday to Thursday, then added: 'We ask everyone to continue their activities as normal.' This is a well-known administrative technique: declare the situation exceptional and ordinary in a single sentence, so that nobody can later say they were not informed.

The human cost is heavier elsewhere in the conflict. The BBC reported that local authorities in Yemen's Shabwa province said a Houthi missile strike on a security training camp killed more than 50 recruits and injured 200. The Houthis said they targeted 'Saudi enemy concentrations'. Yemen's government called it a war crime and promised a 'firm response'. The Houthi-run health ministry says 141 people have died in Saudi strikes over seven weeks, and a reported strike in Saada killed at least 10.

Meanwhile Al Jazeera's live coverage shows the wider regional war is not helping the mood. President Trump said the US is hitting Iran 'economically and militarily', and an Iranian Revolutionary Guard spokesman threatened undersea communication cables in the Strait of Hormuz. For a conference selling a stable, connected investment destination, undersea cable threats are not ideal copy.

The kingdom says it will return in 2027. Watch for the new date: when it arrives, it will be a more reliable indicator of how Riyadh judges the war than any statement about 'unforgettable impact'.
