Disney Hires Character.AI's Staff, Licenses Its Code, Leaves the Lawsuits Behind

Court filings show Character.AI's bankruptcy is part of a deal in which Disney takes the people and technology while the company keeps the legal liabilities.

The wire said: Disney found a way to buy Character.AI without buying its lawsuits · Ars Technica AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 20, 2026 02:16 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Character.AI, a chatbot startup, has faced lawsuits over chatbot safety and investigations by state attorneys general. It filed for chapter 11 bankruptcy on October 12, and Disney had already hired most of its staff.

Why it matters

If this structure holds up, buyers could take the valuable parts of a troubled AI company and leave injured plaintiffs and regulators dealing with the remainder. It also tests whether antitrust and corporate-law safeguards can handle a deal built to avoid being called a merger.

Character.AI filed for chapter 11 bankruptcy on October 12, and court filings show the filing was part of a deal with Disney. According to Ars Technica, Disney has hired most of the startup's employees, including its former CEO, Karandeep Anand, and hopes to license much of its technology. Disney is not buying the company, which turns out to be the point.

The deal comes in two linked transactions. In September, Disney paid Character.AI $5.5 million, put down a refundable $2 million good-faith deposit, and hired the majority of its staff. TechCrunch reported, citing a filing spotted by Bloomberg, that Disney would make offers to at least 60 employees at an estimated $60 million in annual compensation, and that the vast majority accepted. Anand is now Disney's first CTO.

The second transaction happens in bankruptcy court. Disney wants a perpetual, worldwide, nonexclusive license to Character.AI's code, documentation, tools and other artifacts, including its trust and safety moderation stack, training infrastructure and age-prediction technology. Translation: Disney gets the tools for keeping teenagers away from the chatbots, and the company that built them keeps the teenagers' lawyers. User data and the website are not on the list.

Because this is a stalking horse bid (an opening offer that sets the floor for an auction), other bidders can still appear. If Disney wins the auction, the creditors' committee agrees, and the court approves, Disney pays another $12.5 million at closing. If it loses, it gets its $2 million deposit back plus a breakup fee. TechCrunch put the total price of the assets at $20 million.

Disney also drafted a court order that would state the deal is not a merger and that Character.AI stays responsible for any liability or other obligation, including legal claims. Character.AI's lawyers wrote that Disney required the deal to be fully completed through bankruptcy because of the startup's shrinking cash and its litigation and regulatory risks. Disney declined to comment to Ars Technica.

The structure resembles what some call a reverse acquihire: a buyer hires the key people and licenses the technology, and the target becomes a husk. Companies often argue these are not mergers, so they need not notify federal antitrust regulators. The practice has become more common in AI and has drawn the ire of Congress, according to Ars Technica.

John Newman, a University of Memphis law professor and former FTC competition official, told the outlet that decades of corporate law exist for buyers who want all the assets and none of the liabilities. This deal, he said, is structured in a way those safeguards were not built to guard against. He and other experts also doubt that employees can realistically move to another company within months if a rival wins the auction.

Character.AI has done this before. In August 2024, Google reportedly paid it $2.7 billion to license its technology and hired its founders, though a spokesperson said the vast majority of employees stayed. Google was later named in lawsuits alongside Character.AI, including one alleging a 14-year-old boy died by suicide after a chatbot cultivated an emotionally dependent and sexually exploitative relationship. A judge was not persuaded by Google's argument that it was a separate company, and found the aiding-and-abetting claim sufficiently alleged.

Google and Character.AI agreed in principle in January to settle that case and similar ones. A California probate court approved the overall settlement in July, and a referee approved each individual settlement on October 11, a day before the bankruptcy filing. Character.AI's new CEO said the bankruptcy aims for a consensual resolution with injury claimants and a group of investigating states while maximizing value for shareholders.

Character.AI says it struggled with profitability at all times. It hired an investment banker in August 2025 and was told its product and user base were impressive, but that litigation and regulatory overhang made it unbuyable. The solution was to make it unbought in the legal sense and buy everything around it. Watch the auction, and whether the court agrees that this is not a merger.
