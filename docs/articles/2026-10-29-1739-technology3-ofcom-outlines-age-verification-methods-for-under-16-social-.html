Ofcom Tells Social Media Firms to Work Out Who Is 16, Declines to Say How

The regulator listed four 'highly effective' age checks for the UK's March under-16 ban, then left the tech companies to do the actual work.

The wire said: Ofcom outlines age check methods ahead of social media ban for under-16s · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 29, 2026 17:39 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The UK will bar under-16s from ten major platforms, including Instagram, TikTok and Snapchat, from March next year. Australia introduced a similar ban last year, and many children there found ways round it.

Why it matters

Many adults will likely have to prove they are not children to keep using their accounts, and Ofcom concedes some children will still get through. Who ends up responsible for the checks, platforms or phone makers, is still unresolved.

Ofcom, the UK communications regulator, has published its recommendations for how social media companies should work out which users are over 16, ahead of a ban on under-16s due to take effect in March next year, the BBC reported. The measures still need government approval, with a decision due by the end of the year. The regulator has, in effect, produced a menu and told the restaurants to cook.

The menu has four items it calls 'highly effective'. Photo ID matching compares a passport or similar document with a live image. Banking-based checks use what a bank already knows about your age. Face age estimation guesses your age from your face, a technology Ofcom argues has recently improved. Digital ID services let you prove something about yourself, such as your age, without handing over everything else. Translation: your phone, your bank or your face is about to become a bouncer.

The problem is that 16 and 17 year olds are awkwardly placed. According to the BBC, they typically lack credit cards and full driving licences, and may not have passports. They can also look much older or younger than they are, which is a hard fact for an algorithm that estimates age from a face.

It is not only teenagers who will be queuing. The BBC reported that many adults will probably have to prove they are not under-age too. Ofcom has no firm figure for how many, but conceded it would be a 'large number of accounts'. The regulator, then, is confident the system will inconvenience millions of adults and is less sure it will stop every child.

On who does the work, Ofcom chief executive Dame Melanie Dawes said that countries which told companies exactly how to verify age 'were very prescriptive and it didn't work'. Ofcom's approach is to say what can work, insist on privacy and data protection, and leave the rest to the platforms. Translation: the regulator will grade the exam but has declined to write the answers.

The platforms have views on who else should help. Meta wants age checks at device level, so an under-age user would be blocked from installing social media apps through the app store. Meta told the BBC this would spare people from repeatedly verifying their age on multiple platforms. It would also move responsibility to Apple and Google, which between them run most of the world's smartphones. Ofcom replied that social networks would still have to do their own checks, so the burden is being shared in the sense that everyone gets some.

Ofcom also admitted that 'the risk of circumvention cannot be entirely eliminated'. Australia's experience supports the point, since many children there are still on the platforms. Rani Govender of the NSPCC said the UK must accurately know the age of child users, and that tech firms must be held accountable. Andy Burrows of the Molly Rose Foundation went further, citing Ofcom's 'dire track record' of enforcing the Online Safety Act.

Dawes pitched the upside as today's eight to 10 year olds reaching secondary school without it being normal to sit in a Snap class group chat or to have sports fixtures organised on Instagram. That is a real social benefit if it works, and one that depends on a system whose own regulator says it will leak.

The next test is the government's decision by the end of the year, and then whether, in March, a determined 15 year old can beat a face-scanning system that every adult in the country has also been asked to pass.
