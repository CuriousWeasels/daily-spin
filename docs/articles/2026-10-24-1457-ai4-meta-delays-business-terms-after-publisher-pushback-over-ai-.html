Meta Delays Contract Terms Until Publishers Can Be Reassured About Them

The company pushed its new business terms from October 30th to November 20th after news publishers warned the fine print could let it scrape their sites for AI.

The wire said: Meta delays new business terms after publisher pushback on AI scraping · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 24, 2026 14:57 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Meta updated the terms for business accounts to say it could use content from websites associated with those accounts to develop its products. Publishers and legal experts read that as a possible back door for AI training, and Meta has said that is not the intent.

Why it matters

Whether a platform's commercial contract can override a site's robots.txt settings will help decide who controls news content in the AI era. If publishers lose that argument quietly, the cost shows up in their traffic and their negotiating power.

Meta is delaying enforcement of its new business terms until November 20th, three weeks after the original October 30th start date, The Verge reported. Spokesperson Daniel Roberts said the extra time would "allow more time for us to explain to our partners and stakeholders what's changing and what's not." Translation: the document needed a second document explaining what the first one meant.

The trouble began when publishers read language saying Meta "may use any content" from websites associated with a business account to "develop, research" and "train" its products. Legal experts told Nieman Lab this could let Meta argue that its commercial terms outrank other blocking instructions, such as robots.txt. Translation: robots.txt is the small text file where a website tells automated crawlers which rooms they may enter. It works on the honor system, which is a delicate arrangement when the visitor is a trillion-dollar company that is hungry for data.

After the backlash, Meta removed the word "train." Legal experts were not soothed. Cornell Law professor James Grimmelmann told Nieman Lab the edit "does nothing to restrict" the phrase "associated with your products or services," which he said could let Meta scrape entire sites. Attorney Matt Topic of Loevy & Loevy added that the terms still permit use of the content to develop any "business product," an undefined term. In his view, "develop" is broad enough to include "train." Deleting one verb while leaving a larger verb in place is a bit like removing the word "speeding" from a ticket while keeping the car.

The pressure was not subtle. The News/Media Alliance sent a letter to Mark Zuckerberg on Thursday demanding revisions, warning of "severe harm" to publishers. Digital advertisers and publishers in the European Union and the UK also urged regulators to force Meta to halt the terms. Meta's response was to move the date, not the text, which The Verge's reporting suggests it has not yet revised again.

Meta maintains the terms are not a plot. Roberts said content owners can still use robots.txt to signal preferences, and that the update "is not about scraping news sites" and does not give Meta new rights that supersede those preferences. That is a clear assurance, though it appears in a statement and not in the contract, and lawyers tend to read the contract.

The practical gap is where the dispute sits. If Meta means what it says, the cleanest fix is a sentence in the terms saying so. Publishers now have until November 20th to find out whether the explanation will arrive as a clarification or as another reason to keep the lawyers on retainer.
