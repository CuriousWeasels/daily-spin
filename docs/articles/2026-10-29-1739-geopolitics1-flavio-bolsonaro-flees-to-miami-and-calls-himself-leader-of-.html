Defeated Brazilian Candidate Flees to Miami, Declares Himself 'Leader' of Country That Just Voted Otherwise

Flávio Bolsonaro lost Sunday's runoff, left via Argentina and Ecuador on a private jet, and published a letter claiming a title he then edited down within hours.

Catch up

Flávio Bolsonaro, a far-right senator and son of jailed former president Jair Bolsonaro, lost Brazil's presidential runoff to Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva on Sunday. He faces criminal investigations, and Brazilian police had asked the supreme court to take his passport.

Why it matters

A losing candidate who alleges fraud without evidence, then leaves the country as investigators close in, tests Brazil's institutions and its relationship with Washington. It also shows how little a title matters once the electoral court, the courts and your own party say you did not win.

Flávio Bolsonaro landed in Miami on Wednesday morning after losing Brazil's presidential election, and published a letter calling himself the country's "true president," according to The Guardian. He later deleted it and posted a revised version in which he was merely "the leader of Brazil," DW reported. Translation: the demotion took about seven hours and required no vote.

The facts of the election are not in much dispute among people with the authority to dispute them. Bolsonaro's own party said on Monday, "We recognise the presidential result." Brazil's electoral court, supreme court and the presidents of both houses of congress rejected his fraud claims, which he made without evidence, and international observers endorsed the vote. Brazil's three largest newspapers called his declaration "coup-mongering," The Guardian said.

The route was elaborate. Bolsonaro vanished for more than 24 hours after the vote, then entered Argentina by land on Tuesday night, according to Argentine migration authorities. He boarded a private Gulfstream with his wife and two teenage daughters, flew to Guayaquil in Ecuador, reportedly to avoid Brazilian airspace, and continued to Miami-Opa Locka, a private airport favored by executives and the wealthy. How he reached the border remains unclear, and Brazil's federal police are being criticized for failing to stop him.

The passport question deserves explaining. Brazilian police had asked the supreme court to retain his passport, citing the risk that he would flee, in a case involving an alleged money-laundering scheme worth about $905 million tied to betting companies. On Monday Bolsonaro said on Instagram he would "hand over my passports." AFP reported he also holds Italian citizenship, so cancelling his Brazilian and diplomatic passports did not stop him. Translation: a promise to surrender a passport is more convincing when it is the only one.

O Globo reported the escape had been planned for weeks, with his brother Eduardo, who lives in self-imposed exile in the US, as main organizer. Eduardo had said on a podcast days earlier that Flávio should not concede if he lost and should go to the US. Contingency planning is usually a virtue, though it is more reassuring when it is not for the loss you say is impossible.

Washington's role is awkward. Trump said Tuesday night he would approve the family's move, and the State Department said it would grant visas. On Wednesday he declined to comment on the "leader" claim, saying he would not get involved, while adding it was "a very strange election." Lula, speaking at his 81st birthday event, called the flight "shameful." He had accused the Trump administration during the campaign of colluding with the Bolsonaro family, and DW says the episode could deepen the rift between the Americas' two most populous countries.

Distance has a legal logic too. Bolsonaro loses his immunity from prosecution in February when his Senate term ends, and he is under investigation in at least two criminal cases. He calls them a political witch hunt by left-leaning justices. His father, Jair, is jailed after being sentenced to 27 years for leading a plot to stay in power after the 2022 election, and The Guardian notes about a dozen allies have also left Brazil to avoid charges.

Next to watch: whether Brazil's courts act on the passport order, and whether the new title survives longer than the old one.
