Google Releases Gemini 4 Carbon Two Weeks After Argon, Lets Almost Nobody Use It

The new model tops an independent intelligence index with 59 points, but access is limited at first to selected cybersecurity experts.

The wire said: Google launches Gemini 4 Carbon with a ‘significant leap’ in cybersecurity · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 29, 2026 03:01 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Google released Gemini 4 Argon earlier this month and has now followed it with Gemini 4 Carbon, a frontier model it says is stronger at coding, long-running agentic tasks and cybersecurity. Carbon is going through a staged rollout and the U.S. government's voluntary pre-release testing.

Why it matters

Models that are good at finding and exploiting software bugs help defenders and attackers alike, which is why access is being rationed. For businesses that pay per token, Carbon also shows that a top ranking and a reasonable price can coexist, for now.

Google DeepMind announced Gemini 4 Carbon on Wednesday, about two weeks after Gemini 4 Argon, and says it improves on its predecessor in coding, agentic tasks and cybersecurity. According to The Decoder, Carbon scores 59 points on version 5.0 of the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, up from Argon's 50, which puts it first. Ars Technica's headline described it as a frontier model "that you can't use," which is the briefest accurate summary available.

The access problem is deliberate. Google says Carbon's cybersecurity abilities are advanced enough that it is going through the U.S. government's voluntary pre-release testing process, and the model is available first only to selected security experts. Access will widen through Google DeepMind's Fairwind Program, and developers, businesses and consumers come later, after Google collects feedback and adjusts the safety guardrails. Translation: guardrails are the restrictions that stop a model from doing what it is very good at doing for the wrong person.

The security numbers explain the caution. The Decoder reported that on SEC-bench Pro, which measures autonomous discovery of real-world software bugs, Carbon scored 88.2 percent and led all tested models. On ExploitGym, which tests whether a model can turn a discovered vulnerability into a working exploit, it reached 54.8 percent. Google ran several of these tests without safety guardrails, which is a useful way to learn what a product can do and a less useful way to reassure anyone.

Google also cites internal results that cannot be independently verified. It says Carbon produces secure code more than twice as often as Argon, and that its Chrome team found it more than ten times better at writing security patches than an unnamed competitor's frontier model. Ars Technica noted that Google's evidence comes mostly as vague assessments backed by charts, and speculated that Carbon could be a post-training upgrade of Argon rather than a wholly new model. Google, naturally, presents it as new.

Outside the security tests, the picture is more mixed. In agentic coding on Antigravity, Carbon trails Claude Code with Opus 5.5 by three points, according to The Decoder. On the Artificial Analysis agentic knowledge-work test it improved by 126 Elo points but still sits behind Kimi K3.1 Pro, Muse Wonder and Grok 4.7. Translation: Elo is a chess-style rating in which models are compared head to head, and the points mean who wins more often, not who is right.

Carbon also gets things wrong in a new way. Its answer accuracy rose from 48 to 58 percent, but its hallucination rate climbed from four to 11 percent, which The Decoder noted is still the second-lowest in the ranking. Hallucination means confidently stating something false. Google, for its part, says Carbon cuts "unsupported claims" by half compared with Argon, so the sources disagree on how to measure candor, which is itself a finding.

Pricing is the part ordinary buyers can follow. Carbon launches at $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, matching Argon, with cached input tokens discounted 95 percent. A token is a small chunk of text, roughly a word fragment. Artificial Analysis uses the regular list price of $4 and $20, ignoring a 50 percent introductory discount running through the end of 2026, and calculates $7.76 per task, about 23 percent below Claude Opus 5.5 at $10.04.

The speed of the release cycle matters as much as any single score. Ars Technica observed that every frontier model lands in the same neighborhood on these tests, with Carbon ahead of GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5 by only a few points in most. When the lead is a few points and the product cycle is two weeks, the real competition is who ships next. Watch for when Carbon leaves the security-experts-only waiting room, and for whether anyone outside Google gets to check its numbers.
