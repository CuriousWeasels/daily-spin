Treasury Adviser Says IMF Forecasts Are Messy, Cites Debt Warning It Would Prefer Not to Hear

A new Treasury counselor attacked the IMF's record after it warned growth alone won't fix U.S. debt, which has passed $40 trillion with 10-year yields above 5%.

The wire said: U.S. Treasury dismisses IMF debt warning and 'messy' forecasting (CNBC Economy)

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & Economy Oct 15, 2026 13:06 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

U.S. government debt topped $40 trillion this year, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has said the country can "grow our way out" of it. IMF chief Kristalina Georgieva said in Bangkok that strong growth alone would not make the debt sustainable.

Why it matters

The debt argument is already visible in household budgets: long-term Treasury yields drive mortgage rates, now around 7.5%, and higher rates make the deficit itself harder to shrink. Neither party has a plan to change that before the midterms or after.

David Zervos, newly appointed counselor to the Treasury Secretary, told CNBC on Thursday that the IMF should check its own forecasting record before criticizing U.S. fiscal policy. "Take it all with a grain of salt," he said. This is a standard rebuttal in economics: if the argument is inconvenient, discuss the arguer.

Zervos, a former chief market strategist at Jefferies, offered a list. By his account, the IMF had world growth at 3% in 2008 before the first global contraction in post-war history, was upbeat on Southeast Asia before 1997, and was too gloomy on Russia in 2022. He also cited Argentina, which he said was nearly in default again by 2020 after a $57 billion bailout. Translation: forecasters are sometimes wrong, which is true, and which also describes the Treasury's own forecasts, the Fed's, and most people's about their fantasy football team.

The list does not address the arithmetic. According to CNBC's analysis, the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget calculates that balancing the budget through growth alone would take a decade of 7.2% growth. Second-quarter GDP was recently revised up from 1.5% to 2.2% annualized. Closing that gap is what CNBC called an almost unimaginable transformation of the economy, though artificial intelligence is the favored candidate.

Meanwhile, the bond market is conducting its own review. The 10-year Treasury yield closed at 5.19% on Wednesday and stood at 5.21% in the latest official data, up 123 basis points since February. Translation: a basis point is a hundredth of a percentage point, so investors now demand about 1.2 points more annual interest to lend to the government for a decade. CNBC attributed the rise to the war with Iran, which began Feb. 28, higher oil and diesel prices, and heavy government borrowing competing with corporate debt issued to build AI infrastructure.

The deficit is the other problem. The Congressional Budget Office estimated $1.993 trillion for fiscal 2026, roughly 6.0 to 6.2% of GDP, according to CNBC. Bessent has argued for a deficit below 4% of GDP by the end of Trump's term, a goal CNBC said the country is almost certain to miss. Higher rates make it worse: the CBO says rates a half-point above its forecast would add $1.9 trillion in deficits over ten years, and 1.5 points above would add $6 trillion.

Ordinary people are already paying the interest in a different form. The average 30-year mortgage is around 7.5%, and Redfin reported pending home sales fell 4% in September, the largest monthly drop in three years. CNBC noted that mortgage rates track the 10-year yield, which is a polite way of saying the Treasury's borrowing costs and your kitchen renovation share a landlord.

The political outlook offers little relief. Republicans are widely forecast to lose at least the House in the Nov. 3 midterms, and CNBC reported that Trump pledged $5,000 checks to every adult citizen if they swept. Rep. Hakeem Jeffries, the likely next House speaker, wants to reverse $1 trillion in healthcare cuts. CNBC's analysis concluded that economics alone cannot solve the problem and that the politics must be fixed too, which is the one forecast nobody has been accused of getting wrong because nobody has made it with a date attached.
