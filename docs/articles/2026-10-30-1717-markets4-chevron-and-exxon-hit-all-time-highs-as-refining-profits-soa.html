Exxon and Chevron Hit Record Highs After Discovering That Fuel Shortages Are Profitable

The two oil giants posted combined third-quarter profits of $25.3 billion as refineries ran flat out and diesel reached $6.32 a gallon.

The wire said: Chevron and Exxon hit all-time highs as refining profits soar in reaction to Iran and Ukraine wars · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 30, 2026 17:17 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Since March, when the U.S. and Israel attacked Iran, attacks by Ukraine, Iran and Houthi militants on refineries in Russia and the Middle East have cut global refining capacity by millions of barrels a day. Importers have turned to U.S. Gulf Coast refineries for diesel.

Why it matters

Diesel moves freight, farm equipment and construction, so a 70% jump in its price filters into the cost of nearly everything else. Meanwhile the companies selling it are posting record share prices.

Chevron and ExxonMobil reported sharply higher third-quarter profits on Friday, and both stocks touched all-time highs, according to CNBC. Exxon's profit doubled to $15.4 billion, while Chevron's net income rose nearly 200% to $9.9 billion. Both beat Wall Street expectations, which is the traditional way a company confirms that other people's problems are going well.

The mechanism is simple. Ukraine, Iran and Houthi militants have attacked refineries in Russia and the Middle East, removing millions of barrels per day of capacity. Importers therefore need diesel from somewhere else, and the somewhere else is the U.S. Gulf Coast. Exxon's refineries there ran at 100% capacity utilization. Chevron's U.S. refineries ran at 98% and processed just over 1 million barrels a day, a record.

Translation: when a product is scarce and everyone must buy it anyway, the people who still have a working factory get to name the price. Exxon CEO Darren Woods put it more formally, telling CNBC that diesel is the crunch point because it is critical to growth, with "very inelastic demand." Translation: customers will keep paying whatever it costs, because trucks do not run on good intentions.

The refining divisions did most of the work. Chevron's refining profit rose more than 300% to $4.9 billion. Exxon's refining earned about $7.2 billion in the quarter, edging out its production business at about $7.1 billion. Exxon's refining profit for the first nine months reached $11.4 billion, against $4 billion a year earlier.

Pumping oil, by contrast, was not the story. Chevron's production slipped to 3.9 million barrels a day from about 4 million, partly because of the Middle East conflict. Exxon's output this year is down more than 2%. The companies are producing slightly less and earning far more, which is a business model economists usually call a market, and everyone else calls a bill.

That bill is visible at the pump. AAA put the U.S. national average for diesel at $6.32 a gallon on Friday, up more than 70% from a year earlier, after a record high in September. Brent crude stood at $123.10 in the latest data, and inflation was running at 3.74% as of September, so the fuel costs are not confined to truck stops.

The politics are awkward. CNBC noted that President Donald Trump said in August that the two companies "were making too much money based on a shortage." The companies appear to have taken this as a forecast rather than a criticism. Chevron CEO Mike Wirth, for his part, told CNBC that "the system is under stress" and that the environment is "fairly fragile."

Fragile is a useful word here. Record profits depend on refineries elsewhere staying broken and Gulf Coast plants staying at full tilt. Watch whether the shortage eases, and whether Washington's displeasure ever turns into something other than a remark.
