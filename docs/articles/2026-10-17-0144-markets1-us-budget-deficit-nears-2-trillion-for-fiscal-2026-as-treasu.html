U.S. Posts $1.992 Trillion Deficit, Plans to Grow Out of It by Growing Seven Percent for a Decade

The fiscal 2026 shortfall rose 12% to nearly $2 trillion, 10-year yields sit at 5.1%, and the Treasury's reply to the IMF is that the IMF has been wrong before.

The wire said: U.S. budget deficit swells to almost $2 trillion for fiscal 2026, up 12% in a single year · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 17, 2026 01:44 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The Treasury reported that the government spent about $1.992 trillion more than it collected in fiscal 2026, the third-largest gap on record. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has said the U.S. can 'grow our way out' of its debt, which passed $40 trillion this year.

Why it matters

Heavy government borrowing helps keep long-term yields high, and mortgage rates follow those yields. That makes homes costlier to finance now, and it leaves less room in the budget for anything except interest.

The U.S. ended fiscal 2026 with a budget deficit of $1.992 trillion, up about 12% from $1.775 trillion a year earlier, the Treasury Department reported Friday, according to CNBC. It is the third-largest deficit ever, behind only 2020 and 2021, when the government was fighting a pandemic. This year had no pandemic, though the sources do mention a war with Iran, which began Feb. 28.

Translation: a deficit is the gap between what the government collects and what it spends in a year. The government has now run one for 25 straight years, having last posted a surplus in 2001. Receipts and outlays both hit records this year, which means the country collected more money than ever and still managed to fall short by nearly $2 trillion.

The biggest items are familiar. Social Security cost nearly $1.7 trillion and Medicare almost $1.1 trillion. Net interest, which is what the government pays on its debt after subtracting interest it receives, topped $1 trillion for the year, up from $970 billion. CNBC separately put gross interest on the public debt at more than $1.3 trillion. The government is now spending more than $1 trillion on the privilege of having already spent money.

Revenue did not collapse. Individual income taxes brought in more than $2.8 trillion, up about 7%. Corporate income tax fell about $70 billion to $381 billion, which CNBC attributed to last year's tax law. Tariffs set a record for gross customs duties, but refunds ordered after the Supreme Court ruled many of them unconstitutional sent billions back to retailers. The tariffs were pitched as a way to shrink the debt, and the refund line is a less advertised part of that plan.

The bond market has noticed. The 10-year Treasury yield was 5.1% as of Oct. 15, and CNBC's analysis said it has risen 114 basis points since the end of February. Translation: a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so the 10-year now pays more than a full point above where it started the war. Analysts cited the war, higher oil and diesel prices, and traders' worry that the Fed might raise rates. They also cited the sheer volume of government bonds for sale, plus companies borrowing hundreds of billions to build AI infrastructure. The Treasury is competing for investors' money with data centers.

Ordinary people feel this through mortgages, which track the 10-year yield. The average 30-year fixed rate is around 7.5%, and Redfin said pending home sales fell 4% in September, the biggest monthly drop in three years. The Congressional Budget Office expects the 10-year to average 4.3% over the next decade. If rates run a half point higher, it estimates deficits grow by $1.9 trillion. If they run 1.5 points higher, the added cost is $6 trillion.

Bessent's answer is growth. The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget calculated that growth alone would need a decade of 7.2% annual expansion to balance the budget. Second-quarter GDP was recently revised up from 1.5% to 2.2% annualized, so the plan requires roughly tripling the economy's pace and holding it there. CNBC's analysis called the needed transformation almost unimaginable.

The IMF's Kristalina Georgieva warned in Bangkok that strong U.S. growth would not be enough. David Zervos, newly appointed counselor to the Treasury secretary, replied that the Fund's forecasts have been 'messy' and cited misses on 2008, Russia and Argentina. It is a fair point about forecasts, though it does not change the arithmetic of the deficit, which is not a forecast. It already happened.

Politics offers little help. Republicans are widely forecast to lose at least the House in the Nov. 3 midterms, and CNBC noted that the parties start far apart, with Democrats wanting to reverse $1 trillion in healthcare cuts. Trump has pledged $5,000 checks to adult citizens if Republicans sweep the election. CNBC stressed that expensive borrowing is not yet a debt crisis, since higher yields also attract buyers. Next up is the midterm result, which will show who gets to disagree about the deficit for the next two years.
