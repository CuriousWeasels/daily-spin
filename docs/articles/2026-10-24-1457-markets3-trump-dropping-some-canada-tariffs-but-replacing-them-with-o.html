White House Swaps Canadian Tariffs Like Items in a Fantasy Football Trade, Four Days Before Election

Trump removes some 50% duties on Canadian goods and adds valves, carpets and waste products, as Sen. Susan Collins claims credit for the exemptions in a tight Maine race.

The wire said: Trump dropping some Canada tariffs but 'replacing' them with other products · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 24, 2026 14:57 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Trump imposed 50% tariffs on a range of Canadian goods over the summer using Section 338, a 96-year-old trade law, as part of an escalating trade war. Maine, which imports more from Canada than from any other country, has felt the strain, and the tariffs are a top issue in its Senate race.

Why it matters

The net tariff burden on Canadian goods is not obviously shrinking, so businesses that import the newly covered products face higher costs even as others get relief. Consumers in border states and everywhere else should expect the tariff list to keep shifting.

The White House said Friday it would drop tariffs on some Canadian goods and replace them with tariffs on other Canadian goods, according to CNBC. President Donald Trump signed a proclamation removing certain products from the list he hit with 50% duties this summer. A fact sheet says the list will instead include "certain valves, carpets, waste products, and others." The administration describes this as an effort to refine the scope of the tariffs. A tariff list that is refined and not reduced is a bit like a diet in which you give up bread and take up pasta.

The sequence of events is the interesting part. Sen. Susan Collins, R-Maine, announced that she had secured exemptions on nine categories of products she called crucial to Maine's agricultural industries, a claim The Wall Street Journal reported first. Her press release did not mention that new tariffs were coming. The White House announced the additions less than an hour later. Translation: the senator announced the good news, and the administration supplied the rest of it.

The exempted items, according to Collins, include corrugated paper, plastic boxes and packing bags, refrigeration equipment, multiple forms of machinery, and seaweed and algae products. Her spokeswoman, Phoebe Keller, told CNBC that Collins "requested exemptions for products that are important to Maine and will continue to do so." Asked what Collins had agreed to in her conversations with the administration, that is the answer CNBC received. The fact sheet does not mention Maine.

The timing carries its own message. The changes take effect Oct. 30, four days before the Nov. 3 election, and voters in Maine have already started casting ballots. Collins is fighting for reelection against Democrat Troy Jackson, a fifth-generation logger, and the tariffs are unpopular in the state. Jackson said Collins enabled the policy and now wants credit for "bringing a cup of water to put out the fire."

This is a rerun. At the Oct. 6 debate, Collins took credit for removing items such as road salt from the tariff list. Jackson replied, "What about everything else? We can't eat road salt." It is a fair point about a product that is useful mainly in winter and not at all for dinner.

The White House has a different framing. Spokesman Matthew Middleton told CNBC the proclamation responds to Canada's "continued discrimination" and retaliation against U.S. commerce, with the goal of restoring reciprocity. Translation: reciprocity means each side's tariffs are supposed to be matched by the other's, and the arrangement is that the matching never ends. Collins, for her part, said she would keep seeking relief and encourage a broader trade agreement with Canada.

For markets, the swap is a footnote. SPY closed at 759.97 on Oct. 23 and the VIX stood at 19.03 on Oct. 22, which suggests investors have decided that tariff reshuffling is the weather. Inflation was 3.74% year over year as of September, and tariffs on inputs such as valves and packaging tend to show up eventually in the prices of things that contain them. Watch what happens after Nov. 3, when the political value of an exemption presumably drops and the next proclamation arrives.
