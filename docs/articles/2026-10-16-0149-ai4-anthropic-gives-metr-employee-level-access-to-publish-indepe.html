Anthropic Gives Outside Auditors Laptops, Desks, and Right to Publish Without Asking

Six METR researchers get employee-level access to the AI lab, with Anthropic keeping only a narrow right to redact, not to veto.

The wire said: Anthropic is letting METR researchers look under the hood with employee-level access · TechCrunch AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 16, 2026 01:49 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

METR is a nonprofit that tests frontier AI models for risky capabilities. Anthropic announced Thursday it will embed six of its researchers inside the company, following a promise made by CEO Dario Amodei in a September essay.

Why it matters

Most AI safety claims today are made by the companies selling the AI, so independent access is the difference between a safety report and a safety advertisement. Whether the arrangement holds up will shape how much the public can trust what labs say about their own systems.

Anthropic said in a blog post on Thursday that it is giving six researchers from METR employee-level access to assess its models, safety practices, and risk management, according to TechCrunch. The researchers receive Anthropic-issued laptops, office space, and internal tools roughly on par with those used by Anthropic's own risk assessment teams. It is, in effect, a company hiring an auditor and then handing the auditor a badge.

The timing is not subtle. TechCrunch reported that incidents of AI agents acting outside their instructions have been disclosed every week or two since late August, which has sharpened calls for independent checks. Translation: when software keeps doing things nobody asked it to do, the public becomes less willing to accept the software's maker saying everything is fine.

Anthropic compared the arrangement to banking, where regulators place resident examiners inside the largest banks. The analogy is instructive, though one difference is worth noting: bank examiners hold legal authority, while METR is a nonprofit working under a voluntary agreement. The company says it wants to be publicly accountable and to close the gap between what evaluators understand and what the company itself understands.

The notable term is publication. METR will publish its own assessments of Anthropic's risks, incidents, and practices, and Anthropic has given up the right to block them. It can still require redactions, but only for narrow, pre-defined categories: customer data, attorney-client privileged material, and information about model architectures. METR may publicly characterize those redactions and say whether it thinks they were justified, provided it does not reveal what was blacked out. Translation: the auditor can say the company blacked out a lot, but not what was under the marker.

Anthropic is also building a three-tier system for outside evaluators. At the bottom, groups such as the biosecurity nonprofit SecureBio run narrow reviews and need Anthropic's approval to publish. The middle tier gets more information and can publish without approval. The top tier adds employee-level access, and METR is the only occupant so far. Every tier above the bottom involves the company agreeing to be criticized, which is rarer than it sounds.

The move fulfills a promise from Amodei's September essay, which proposed embedding outside evaluators inside AI labs as a first step toward slowing the technology's development. TechCrunch reported that OpenAI quickly pledged the same, and that both companies, along with Google, Meta, Nvidia, and xAI, have since signed a White House accord committing to independent review.

So the industry has agreed, nearly unanimously, to be inspected. What remains to be seen is what the first inspection report says, and how many pages of it are redacted. That number will be public, which is the whole point.
