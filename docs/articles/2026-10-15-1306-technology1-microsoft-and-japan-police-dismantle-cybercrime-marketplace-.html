Microsoft and Japan Shut Down Scam Marketplace That Ran Like a Normal Online Store

Microsoft says it disabled more than 20,000 accounts tied to YAM Store and two phishing kits blamed for over a billion malicious messages since January 2026.

By The Daily Spin Desk · Technology Oct 15, 2026 13:06 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Microsoft, working with Japan's National Police Agency, traced widespread phishing activity to a marketplace called YAM Store and two phishing kits, CoGUI and Iron Man. The company says it disabled accounts, blocked domains and seized web addresses powering all three platforms.

Why it matters

If the takedown holds, some of the fake parking tickets and phantom package notices reaching phones and inboxes may become less frequent. It is also a reminder that phishing is run less by lone hackers than by suppliers who sell tools to other criminals.

Microsoft said it helped dismantle a marketplace that supplied scammers with the materials needed to send phishing messages, according to The Verge. The investigation, run with Japan's National Police Agency, led to YAM Store and two phishing kits called CoGUI and Iron Man. Phishing, for the uninitiated, means a message pretending to be a bank, delivery firm or government office in order to get you to hand over a password or payment details. Translation: the parking ticket you did not earn was part of a supply chain.

The numbers are large. Microsoft says that since January 2026 the three platforms collectively enabled more than one billion malicious messages aimed at over 75,000 organizations in more than 150 countries. More than 750 million of those messages were directed at Microsoft customers alone. Targets included consumers, healthcare organizations, financial institutions and other businesses, and the operators earned tens of millions of dollars, according to the company's report.

What stands out is how orderly the operation was. Scammers could buy stolen or fraudulent Microsoft and Google accounts on YAM Store, then use the phishing kits to send their messages. In other words, crime had a storefront, an inventory and a checkout process, which is more than can be said for several legitimate startups.

Microsoft says the marketplace also hijacked expired web domains that once belonged to real organizations and built websites for organizations that do not exist, then used them to obtain Microsoft accounts it could resell. Translation: a domain is a web address, and when a company lets one lapse, someone else can pick it up and borrow its credibility. The scheme turned forgotten renewal reminders into a business line.

Iron Man, meanwhile, offered more than 2,300 templates for impersonating organizations such as banks and police departments. That is a catalog of costumes, including the police. Customers did not need to design a convincing fake bank page; they could pick one from a menu.

On the enforcement side, Microsoft says it disabled over 20,000 accounts linked to YAM Store and blocked more than 100,000 web domains used for impersonation and fraud. Together with Health-ISAC, a health sector cybersecurity nonprofit, it also seized and redirected the domains powering all three platforms. The sources do not say whether anyone has been arrested or charged.

Whether your phone gets quieter is an open question. Marketplaces like this are, by design, replaceable, and the 2,300 templates presumably exist elsewhere in some form. For now, the fake parking ticket has lost one supplier, and the market will presumably open applications for a new one.
