Israel Votes, Counts, and Discovers Nobody Has 61 of the 120 Seats

With about 85 to 90 percent of ballots counted, Netanyahu's bloc leads with 53 seats to Eisenkot's 48, and 19 Arab-party seats hold the arithmetic.

The wire said: Netanyahu's bloc leading Israel election but short of majority, partial results show · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 29, 2026 03:01 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Israel held its first general election since the Hamas-led attacks of 7 October 2023 and the wars that followed in Gaza, Lebanon and Iran. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu faced former military chief Gadi Eisenkot in a vote widely seen as a referendum on Netanyahu's leadership.

Why it matters

Weeks of coalition bargaining, and possibly another election, will decide who runs a country at war and what happens to its judiciary, its military draft rules and its relations abroad. Israeli politics is again in the hands of the arithmetic, not the voters' stated preferences.

Partial results show Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc leading Israel's election without a majority. With about 85 percent of ballots counted, the BBC reported, Likud and its far-right and ultra-Orthodox allies were on track for 53 seats in the 120-seat Knesset. Eisenkot's Yashar party and three other Jewish opposition parties were set for 48. A majority requires 61, a number neither side is near, which is the sort of detail that tends to matter in elections.

Both men have said they will form a government. Translation: both men intend to be asked, and both have told the public that the public has already asked. Netanyahu declared a "great majority" at Likud headquarters. Eisenkot said the public had "spoken in favour of change." The two claims cannot both be right, though the count suggests they can both be wrong.

The exit polls did not help. Three broadcasters first projected 52 or 53 seats for Eisenkot's bloc against 50 for Netanyahu's. Updated versions had two showing a 51-51 tie and one giving Netanyahu a three-seat lead. As counting went on, Netanyahu's bloc outperformed the polls at 53, though the BBC noted that is still 15 seats fewer than it once controlled in the last Knesset.

The real story is the 19 seats projected for Arab-led parties, the United Arab List and the Joint List, an unprecedented figure that nearly doubles their representation. The BBC attributed this to a surge in turnout among Arab Israelis, who are about a fifth of the population. In theory, that makes them kingmakers. In practice, all but one of the Jewish parties have pledged not to work with them, so the kingmakers have been invited to the coronation on the condition that they not attend.

Netanyahu has warned against a government relying on "anti-Zionist Arab parties." Eisenkot has previously attached conditions to such cooperation, including recognition of Israel as Jewish and democratic, and the right-wing parties in his own bloc have flatly rejected it. Al Jazeera reported that Joint List leader Yousef Jabareen said the alliance could back an anti-Netanyahu government from outside but would not join it. He also said the opposition "cannot dictate any conditions." That is a hard negotiating position for a party the other side says it will not negotiate with.

Likud, meanwhile, said the election was "stolen," alleging bribery, threats, violence, fraud and prohibited software, which Al Jazeera's account directs at Arab parties. The BBC noted the claims came without evidence, and the Central Elections Committee said it would examine them in a post-election review. Likud said it was passing hundreds of testimonies to the police, the Shin Bet and the committee. This is a notable position for a bloc that leads the count.

What happens next is procedural. Between roughly 430,000 and 480,000 absentee ballots, from soldiers, diplomats and hospital patients, remain uncounted (Al Jazeera put it above 500,000), so seat totals could still move. After official results, President Isaac Herzog will consult every faction and invite the leader he thinks has the best chance to try forming a government, a process that can take weeks. Al Jazeera noted no party has ever won an outright majority in Israel, so coalition building is not a malfunction but the system working as designed, if slowly.

Analysts quoted by Al Jazeera predict Netanyahu, as head of the largest bloc, may try a unity government that could include Eisenkot, though that is far from certain. Netanyahu stays caretaker prime minister meanwhile. Herzog said everyone should avoid another election and come "with an open mind," which is the political equivalent of asking a room full of coalition negotiators to leave their coalitions at the door. If they fail, the fallback is another vote.
