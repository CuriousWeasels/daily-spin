US Bans DeepSeek From Civilian Agencies, Keeps It Open for Pentagon Browsing

Homeland Security ordered DeepSeek's AI removed from civilian government systems, though the notice reportedly did not say exactly what the security concern was.

The wire said: US bans China's DeepSeek from government in race for AI dominance · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 21, 2026 02:09 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

DeepSeek is a Chinese AI company whose free, open-source models rattled US tech stocks after it said it trained its debut model for about $6m. The Trump administration regards China as America's main rival in AI.

Why it matters

The ban shows that cheap, widely used AI models are now treated as a security question, and it could pressure US companies that build DeepSeek into their products. It also hints at how much the expensive US AI industry would like its cheaper rival to be less available.

The US Department of Homeland Security has banned all use of DeepSeek within civilian government agencies, along with any technology built with the help of its AI models, the BBC reported. The notice was made public on Monday, but the ban took effect at the end of July. Evidently the memo took a while to travel from the policy to the press, which is a pace familiar to anyone who has dealt with government.

The order covers civilian departments such as Treasury, State and Justice. Agencies within the Department of Defense are excluded. Translation: the ban applies to the people who file the paperwork, not necessarily to the people with the weapons, which is a distinctive way to define a security threat.

According to the BBC, the DHS notice did not detail the exact security concerns about DeepSeek. The White House has, however, criticised Chinese AI firms this year. Michael Kratsios, the White House tech lead, wrote in an internal memo that "malicious exploitation" of American AI firms had been occurring, mainly by rival firms in China.

American companies have made a related complaint. Anthropic and OpenAI, both of which the BBC says are poised to list on the public stock market in the coming months, have claimed that some Chinese AI firms, including DeepSeek, essentially copied their models. Translation: two companies about to ask investors for large sums would like it known that their product is valuable enough to be copied. The sources do not say how the copying allegedly took place.

The commercial backdrop is cost. DeepSeek is open source, meaning it is free to access and cheaper to use, and developers pay a fraction of what closed models such as Google's Gemini, Anthropic's Claude or OpenAI's Codex would cost. Last year DeepSeek said it took about $6m to train its debut model, against roughly $100m for OpenAI's GPT-4. Such revelations sent tech stocks tumbling, which is one way for a spreadsheet to become a geopolitical event.

The ban sits awkwardly with how popular the tool is. DeepSeek's free chatbot app ranks among the most downloaded in the Apple App Store, and Snowflake, Vercel and OpenRouter all incorporate its models in their offerings. Its servers have gone down at times because of demand. Its newest version was released last week, and users online say it beats OpenAI's tools on common tasks such as software coding, according to the BBC.

The BBC said it had contacted the DHS, DeepSeek and the Chinese embassy in Washington for comment. Watch for whether the companies that build on DeepSeek are asked to choose between their government customers and their cheapest ingredient.
