Apple Updates App Store Rules to Say 'Nudify' Apps Are Not Allowed, Again, More Clearly

The revised guidelines require developers to proactively prevent and remove nonconsensual intimate images, months after Grok, protests and a city attorney made the point first.

The wire said: Apple’s updated App Store rules go after ‘nudify’ AI apps · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 17, 2026 14:30 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Earlier this year, X users used the Grok AI tool to make nonconsensual, sexualized deepfakes, including images of minors. Apple faced criticism for leaving X and Grok in the App Store, and a San Francisco city attorney later ordered it to remove 13 'nudify' apps.

Why it matters

App Store rules decide what hundreds of millions of phone owners can download, so explicit wording gives Apple a firmer basis to remove apps that generate fake intimate images of real people. Whether it uses that basis consistently, including against large and powerful apps, is the open question.

Apple has updated its App Store rules to more explicitly ban so-called 'nudify' apps, which use AI to generate intimate images of people without their consent, according to The Verge. The changes were announced Friday. Apple has therefore concluded, after careful review, that this was worth writing down more clearly.

The new language says developers 'must proactively prevent and remove' nonconsensual sexual images as part of Apple's content moderation requirements. Translation: it is no longer enough to react when someone complains. The app maker is expected to see the problem coming, which is a reasonable standard for a product designed to produce the problem.

Enforcement follows the usual escalating scale. Developers who 'fail to implement adequate safeguards' or 'don't remedy violations after notice' may be removed from the App Store. Note the word 'may.' It gives Apple room to act, and also room to deliberate, which companies tend to find equally useful.

Apple also clarified its rule on 'bait-and-switch' apps, meaning apps that add or change features after approval, including 'nudification.' Translation: an app can no longer pass review as a photo editor and then quietly become something else once nobody is looking. The review process checks the app on the day it is reviewed, a limitation that the clarified rule now acknowledges.

Separately, the rules now more clearly state that apps that 'facilitate human trafficking or the exploitation of children' will be pulled from the App Store and 'blocked from alternative distribution.' That last phrase matters, because alternative distribution means ways of getting apps onto iPhones outside Apple's own store. Apple would like it understood that its objections follow the app wherever it goes.

The backdrop is a string of pressure points, as The Verge reported. After Grok was used to make sexualized deepfakes, Apple did not remove X or Grok from the App Store, though it told senators it had threatened to remove Grok. Protesters at Apple's WWDC developer event in June called on it to remove Grok, X and all nudify technology. In July, a San Francisco city attorney ordered Apple to remove 13 such apps, and Apple said it did.

So the sequence ran from public harm, to protest, to a legal order, to a rewritten rulebook. It is a familiar order of operations in the technology industry, where policy tends to arrive as a patch note.

The test is not the text but the enforcement. Apple has now written a rule broad enough to cover a general-purpose AI tool that can be misused, and a threat of removal is only as useful as the willingness to carry it out against an app with millions of users. Watch whether the next violation is handled by a notice or by a deletion.
