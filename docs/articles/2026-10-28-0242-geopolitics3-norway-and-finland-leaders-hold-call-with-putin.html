Finland and Norway Phone Putin for 90 Minutes, Agree Mainly to Phone Again

The two Nordic leaders say they spoke only for themselves, a distinction Europe's other leaders were eager to note, and the Kremlin called the talks constructive.

The wire said: Norway and Finland leaders hold rare call with Putin · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 28, 2026 02:42 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine began in 2022, and European leaders have largely been sidelined from peace talks since Putin's Alaska summit with Trump in 2025. The first trilateral US-Ukraine-Russia talks of the year are tentatively planned for early November in Abu Dhabi.

Why it matters

Europe is trying to find a seat at a negotiation about its own continent, and the first attempt shows how hard it is to do that without appearing to freelance. Any settlement will shape European security and sanctions policy for years.

The presidents and prime ministers of Finland and Norway held a 90-minute call with Russian President Vladimir Putin, according to the BBC, making it rare direct contact between Moscow and European leaders. Finnish President Alexander Stubb and Norwegian Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre said they wanted to "convey messages directly regarding future negotiations." Neither side suggested the war had moved any closer to ending, which is the diplomatic equivalent of a successful dental appointment: it happened, and nothing was fixed.

Stubb called the conversation "frank and substantive." Translation: diplomatic code for both sides saying what they already believed, at length. Støre was more direct, noting "big differences in views." The Kremlin's account was that a "just and lasting peace" would be possible only if Russia's interests were considered, and Putin repeated what he calls the "root causes" of the war. The BBC notes this is his usual shorthand for Ukraine's desire to exist as a sovereign state.

The two leaders took pains to say they were not speaking for Europe. "We've not set out to speak on behalf of anyone other than ourselves," Støre said. This is a useful position for a call that was not coordinated with the rest of the continent. The Danish and Swedish prime ministers said they had not been consulted, and Sweden's Ulf Kristersson said Europe "should stick together rather than going solo." There was no official EU reaction, which in Brussels counts as a form of reaction.

Stubb said the call was prepared in consultation with Ukraine and the US, following a meeting in Oslo last week between Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky, the Nordic leaders and US envoy Steve Witkoff. Zelensky said he was debriefed afterward and was grateful for their "active support." His foreign minister, Andrii Sybiha, called it "the right approach." So the lone-wolf mission came with the approval of the people it was supposedly not coordinating with.

The background explains the urgency. The BBC reports that relations between Washington and Ukraine and its European allies have soured since President Donald Trump agreed to lift some sanctions on Russia, allowing Moscow to export oil to the US. Translation: sanctions are restrictions meant to make Russia's economy pay for the war, and some of them have been loosened. As Washington softened, several European leaders began arguing that Europe should talk to the Kremlin itself. Stubb and Støre are the first European leaders to call Putin since Emmanuel Macron did so in July 2025.

Moscow's reaction was a small masterclass in having it both ways. The Kremlin regularly mocks European leaders and accuses the EU of prolonging the war by arming Ukraine. Yet spokesman Dmitry Peskov said last weekend that a European negotiator at the table would be "good," and on Tuesday called the Nordic talks "very constructive and practical" with leaders of "two key neighbouring states." Europe is apparently ridiculous as a bloc but useful by the neighbour.

All three sides reportedly agreed to keep talking by phone. Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk said Stubb told him Europe's outlook on the Abu Dhabi talks was "moderately optimistic." That is a precise sort of optimism, calibrated to commit no one to anything. Watch early November to see whether a phone line turns into a chair.
