Congressman Proposes That Buying a Video Game Should Mean Owning It

Rep. Ritchie Torres's Power To The Players Act would have the FTC guarantee ownership of paid-for games, and require offline modes before servers go dark.

The wire said: Video game ownership bill introduced in Congress · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 19, 2026 01:58 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Many modern games need a connection to company servers, so when the servers close, the game can stop working. Rep. Ritchie Torres of the Bronx has introduced a federal bill to address this, citing NBA 2K25 as a recent example.

Why it matters

If the bill passed, a purchased game could not simply vanish when a publisher stops paying its server bill. It would also test whether the word 'buy' on a storefront has to mean what shoppers assume it means.

Rep. Ritchie Torres, who represents New York's 15th congressional district in the Bronx, introduced the Power To The Players Act this week, The Verge reported. The bill would direct the Federal Trade Commission to write regulations guaranteeing that a consumer who pays for a game owns it, rather than holding a licensing agreement. In other words, Congress is being asked to confirm that the button labeled 'Buy' should describe a purchase.

Translation: a license is permission to use something, granted by the company and revocable under the company's terms. Ownership is the older arrangement in which you hand over money and the object is yours. The distinction rarely matters until the day the permission ends, which is the day the game stops launching.

Torres points to NBA 2K25 as a recent example. According to The Verge, 2K will delist the game from storefronts at the end of the month and shut down its servers on December 31st, which limits how much of it can be played. Many games now require a server connection even when a player is mostly alone with the game, so ending the servers can end the product.

Torres put it this way in his statement: "A game that can be taken away the moment a company pulls the plug is not a purchase. It is a rental disguised as a sale." The sentence is notable mainly because it is an accurate description of the business model, delivered by a legislator rather than a footnote.

Beyond ownership, the bill would require developers to build an offline mode before shutting down the servers that support a game. That is a notable demand for an industry that has found server connections very useful for reasons that have little to do with gameplay.

The bill follows concern over ownership, resale and transfer of games, which The Verge says was sharpened by Sony's announcement that it plans to phase out physical game discs in 2028. A disc is at least an object a person can put on a shelf and argue about. Without it, the licence becomes the only thing the customer holds.

There is a state-level cousin. Pennsylvania State Sen. John Kane introduced the Protect Our Games Act, which would require companies to offer an offline mode, an online mode independent of the company's servers, or a minimum 25 percent refund when a title is being sunset. The refund option is a modest admission that a quarter of the price is roughly what an abandoned game is worth.

The sources do not say whether the bill has co-sponsors or any scheduled hearing. Most bills introduced in Congress never become law, so the practical effect for now is a public record of the complaint. Players hoping for ownership might first want a copy of the bill saved locally.
