Microsoft Reports $36.6bn Profit Days After Offering Buyouts to 60,000 Staff

The company says record cloud growth and a voluntary exit program are unrelated chapters of the same story: spending on AI.

The wire said: Microsoft profits soar days after announcing thousands of layoffs · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 29, 2026 03:01 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Microsoft said on Monday it would offer voluntary buyouts to around 60,000 employees in Canada, India, the UK and the US as it spends heavily on AI infrastructure. On Wednesday it reported quarterly results for the three months to the end of September.

Why it matters

Investors are trying to judge whether the hundreds of billions going into AI chips and data centres will pay off, and workers are learning that strong profits do not guarantee their jobs. Microsoft's results suggest the market will tolerate the spending when the cloud business grows, and punish it when profit shrinks.

Microsoft said its profit for the three months to the end of September jumped to more than $36.6bn (£27.5bn), the BBC reported, just days after the company announced it would offer buyouts to around 60,000 workers. Revenue rose 19% from a year earlier to $92.3bn. The company, in short, is extremely profitable and would like fewer people to be on the payroll while it is.

The buyouts are voluntary, which is a useful distinction. Translation: a voluntary redundancy offer is a layoff in which the company asks politely first. Microsoft said the offer, extended to eligible staff in Canada, India, the UK and the US, would create "greater flexibility" for the firm and give departing workers financial support and career planning resources. The BBC noted the company employs more than 220,000 people, and the number of people eligible is more than a quarter of that.

The reason is not that business is bad. Microsoft Cloud, the company's range of enterprise technology services, grew revenue by 30%, and shares rose almost 2.9% in after-hours trading in New York on Wednesday. The reason is that AI is expensive. Microsoft, like its peers, is pouring billions of dollars into the infrastructure that powers AI, meaning chips and data centres, and money spent on buildings full of computers is money not spent on employees.

The scale is considerable. According to the BBC, Amazon, Meta and Alphabet are expected to spend more than $700bn this year on AI chips and infrastructure, a figure that makes a 60,000-person buyout look like a rounding error with a human face. Microsoft itself flagged "substantial investments" in AI among the risks that could affect its finances. This is the corporate equivalent of a homeowner noting that the new swimming pool may affect the household budget, while selling the furniture.

Chief executive Satya Nadella said AI development has helped grow adoption of the technology and has delivered "real-world impact across every industry." Some of that impact, it seems, has landed on the human resources department.

The market's reaction depended on who was reporting. Alphabet, the owner of Google, disappointed investors on the same day: its shares fell by 9.75% after it reported a 25% drop in quarterly net profit from a year earlier and said it would keep spending heavily on AI. So the lesson of the day is that investors do not object to AI spending as such. They object to AI spending that shows up on the profit line without a cloud business growing 30% to explain it.

That is the real test the sector faces. The BBC noted that questions remain over whether such huge spending will pay off, and each earnings season is a small referendum on the matter. For now, Microsoft has won this round, and 60,000 employees have been invited to consider what that means for them. They have been given career planning resources to help.
