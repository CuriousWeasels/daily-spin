India Detains Man Holding National Flag, Cites Concerns About National Flag Gathering

Police in Delhi detained the founder of the Gen Z 'Cockroach' party ahead of a protest demanding the election chief's resignation over a sweeping revision of voter lists.

The wire said: Crowds throng New Delhi and wave Indian flags amid escalating protests · NPR World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 17, 2026 01:44 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The Cockroach Janta Party, a youth-led movement that began online in May, has moved from protesting leaked exam papers to demanding the resignation of Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar. It accuses the Election Commission of manipulating voter lists to favour the ruling BJP; both deny this.

Why it matters

A contested revision of voter rolls goes to the basic question of who gets to vote, and the response to the protests shows how much room India's youngest voters have to object. The outcome could shape trust in the next elections.

Police in Delhi detained Abhijeet Dipke, founder of India's 'cockroach' movement, and a few others on Friday as authorities tried to stop a protest demanding the election chief's resignation, the BBC reported. Hundreds still gathered in Connaught Place, one of the capital's busiest commercial districts, waving Indian flags and shouting slogans. The detention of a man carrying the national flag was, in the words of the opposition politician hosting him, 'a bit of a joke.'

The backstory is short, because the movement is. According to the BBC, the Cockroach Janta Party is barely five months old. It began as an online campaign in May after India's chief justice likened some unemployed young people to cockroaches, and Dipke adopted the insult as a rallying cry. Translation: when someone calls you a pest, you can either object or print the T-shirts, and the second option has better reach.

By July the group was drawing thousands into protests over leaked exam papers, and unrest eventually forced the education minister to resign. It has since moved on to the electoral system, demanding the exit of Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar over the Special Intensive Revision, or SIR, a sweeping overhaul of voter lists. Translation: officials are checking who is on the voter rolls, and critics fear the checking will also remove people who are perfectly real and eligible.

The Election Commission says the exercise targets ineligible, dead and duplicate voters, and denies favouring the BJP. NPR reported that critics call the review politically motivated and a threat to disenfranchise voters, while the commission and Modi's administration reject that. Both sides agree that voter lists should be accurate. They disagree on whether the accuracy is being applied evenly, which is the part that matters.

The policing has become its own plot. Police removed Jantar Mantar, an 18th-century observatory near Parliament and a long-standing venue for dissent, from the list of approved protest sites and named three alternatives. Das and Ranka, two other leaders, returned there on Tuesday and were sent to jail for five days on Wednesday after police said they had breached their release conditions. With Dipke detained, the BBC noted, the movement's three main leaders were again in custody.

Friday's logistics were instructive. Authorities closed Central Park for two days citing 'administrative reasons' and offered a small group the chance to wave the flag and sing the anthem elsewhere. The party countered with a plan for supporters to enter in groups of fewer than five, unfurl the flag, take a photograph and leave. The BBC said police and journalists far outnumbered demonstrators for much of the evening, and one bus held about 50 female officers guarding far fewer detainees.

Police largely held back until dark, after which the BBC saw some force used and two people injured. Police have not publicly confirmed the grounds for Dipke's detention. The party said it lost contact with him and accused police of denying him lawyers. Spokesperson Mehr Pasricha told the BBC: 'They may detain three people, but how will they stop 30 tomorrow?'

Opposition parties are protesting separately, and the BBC reported that Rahul Gandhi and other lawmakers were detained last week while marching to the commission's headquarters. The movement's strategy now rests on a legal point that is difficult to counter in public: waving the flag is not a crime. The test is whether the authorities can keep detaining people for it without making the point for them.
