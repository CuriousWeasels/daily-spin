Britain Asks Interpol to Notice That Argentina's Arrest Requests Are, Technically, About Politics

London says Buenos Aires is using red notices and a UN tribunal to scare investors away from the Sea Lion oil project, which could triple the Falklands' GDP.

The wire said: UK asks Interpol to reject Argentina’s ‘politically motivated’ requests for Falklands oil arrests · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 15, 2026 12:56 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Argentina's president, Javier Milei, has vowed to block the Sea Lion offshore oil project in the North Falkland Basin and has started criminal proceedings against the companies involved. Britain says the pressure is the most intense since the 1982 war.

Why it matters

If Argentina's legal pressure works, investors and workers tied to the Falklands could face arrest risk when traveling, and a project central to the islands' economy could stall. If it fails, it will be a useful lesson in what international bodies refuse to rule on.

Britain has written to Interpol's headquarters in Lyon asking it to reject Argentine requests for the arrest of people involved in the Sea Lion oil project, according to The Guardian. The letter argues that the requests, including those aimed at British nationals, are politically motivated. This is a bold position for a country to take about a dispute over who owns an archipelago.

The tool in question is the red notice. Translation: a request that police worldwide locate a person and provisionally detain them until extradition can be sorted out. Interpol circulates the notices, but each member country decides whether to act, and nearly 20,000 were issued last year. The system depends heavily on everyone agreeing that the request is about crime and not about a grievance with a long memory.

Interpol has reportedly replied that it will respect article 3 of its constitution, which forbids activities of a political character. Andrea Clausen, chief executive of the Falklands government, said: "One would hope that Interpol wouldn't necessarily take action." That is the diplomatic version of crossing your fingers, and she admitted islanders have been reading the constitution closely. Concerns remain that Argentina could argue its requests rest on domestic law, not sovereignty.

The reason for the exercise is the Sea Lion project, which has the potential to triple the islands' GDP. Milei has started criminal proceedings against the Israeli company Navitas and its British partner Rockhopper Exploration. A draft sovereignty bill in Argentina's congress would extend sanctions to affiliates, financial intermediaries, shareholders and suppliers, and has clauses that could reach the fishing industry, a large part of the islands' economy. Translation: the aim is to make working in the Falklands less appealing than not working in the Falklands, by way of airport arrivals halls in South America.

Workers in oil and fisheries are reportedly worried about being targeted if they travel, especially in South America, where there is support for Buenos Aires' claim. The islands sit 300 miles off Patagonia and have 3,600 residents. In a 2013 referendum, 99.8 percent voted to stay British, with three votes against. Argentina has dismissed the result.

The second front opens at sea. Argentina is expected to request arbitration under annex VII of the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, after a two-week deadline Milei set on 28 September passed on Monday. It will claim Britain breached a UN resolution urging both sides to negotiate and avoid unilateral modifications, and seek a binding order to stop drilling. UK sources are confident the tribunal will not touch a sovereignty dispute.

Falklands assembly member Dean Dent offered a precedent. He told constituents the UK had assembled KCs, and that four recent similar cases were disregarded because they were sovereignty issues, not maritime ones. That is a comforting statistic, though it also suggests Argentina is choosing a venue whose main known feature is declining to hear this kind of case.

Britain's own response is a mix of paperwork and posture. Foreign secretary Ed Miliband told MPs that protection of trading interests legislation will be used to shield Falklands businesses. Translation: the UK can order British firms not to comply with specific foreign laws, and those hit by foreign legal pressure may be able to claim damages in UK courts. Prime minister Andy Burnham said he raised the islanders' will to remain British with Donald Trump. Watch for whether Interpol's article 3 survives its first real test.
