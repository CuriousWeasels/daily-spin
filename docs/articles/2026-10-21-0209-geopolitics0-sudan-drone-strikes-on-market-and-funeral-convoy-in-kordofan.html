Drones Kill Hundreds in Sudan as Both Sides Say Civilians Were Not the Target

Strikes on a funeral convoy, a market and a fuel site killed more than 300 people, according to the BBC, and have pulled South Sudan closer to the war.

The wire said: Sudan army drones hit mourners and fuel market killing more than 300 people, sources say · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 21, 2026 02:09 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Sudan's army and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces (RSF) have been at war since April 2023, a conflict that has killed hundreds of thousands and displaced more than 14 million people. Fighting has recently intensified in Kordofan, a region rich in gold and oil.

Why it matters

Drones are now the main weapon of a war that has already produced one of the world's worst humanitarian crises, and a strike in the disputed Abyei region risks drawing in South Sudan. With 20 million people facing acute hunger, according to the UN, every market and transport hub destroyed makes the shortage of food and fuel worse.

Drone strikes in Sudan have reportedly killed more than 300 people over two days, the BBC reported, including dozens who were travelling to a funeral. The Sudan War Observatory, a monitoring group, put the toll at 150 for two locations in West Kordofan state on Sunday evening. The region's information minister said 153 more died on Monday in Abyei. Each side in the war says it targets only combatants, which leaves civilians as the one group no one admits to aiming at.

The accounts of the Sunday strikes differ in detail, as accounts of such events tend to. The Sudan War Observatory said a truck carrying around 200 mourners was hit as it arrived in Al-Dabab, killing 145, and that a funeral tent was also struck, killing five more. The Guardian, citing a witness and an anonymous medic, reported 80 dead at a transport hub in al-Dibab, where people gather to travel home after market day. The witness said the drone struck twice, ten minutes apart. Spelling of the town and the death toll both remain unsettled, which is common in places where communications are cut and the dead are being buried first and counted second.

The Abyei strike hit Amiet Market, about 10km from Abyei town, around midday Monday. Information Minister Yohana Akol Ngor said the toll rose from 73 to 153 in a day, and told AP the situation was "horrible and shocking." DW noted that he and South Sudan's government blamed the Sudanese army without providing evidence. A military source told the BBC the army carried out the attack, saying the market was a fuel supply site for the RSF. The army has not responded to the accusation over the Kordofan strikes, but says it does not attack civilians even when it believes RSF fighters are among them. Translation: the policy is that civilians are protected, except when someone nearby is not.

BBC Verify examined satellite images showing extensive burnt buildings in one corner of the market, which older images show is usually full of trucks. It also verified footage of a large smoke plume and at least one petrol tanker. A fuel depot ignited, and the fire spread to nearby homes and was still burning on Tuesday, according to DW. Roughly 8,000 people were displaced, the minister said. Doctors Without Borders said it was treating more than 20 wounded, including burn patients, at Ameth Bek Hospital.

Abyei is a bad place for this to happen, which is a high bar. Sudan and South Sudan both claim the oil-rich area, and a referendum on its status was planned but never held. It is jointly administered and has had a UN security force since 2011, with more than 2,800 uniformed personnel deployed, per the BBC. One trader told the BBC the area is a weapons-free zone under UN protection, yet was hit anyway. South Sudan has summoned Sudan's ambassador to protest, and there are fears of spillover.

The weapons themselves are not scarce. The BBC noted that a partial arms embargo on Sudan has not stopped foreign countries supplying drones to both sides. The Guardian reported that between January and April, drone strikes accounted for at least 880 civilian deaths, more than 80% of all conflict-related civilian deaths. UN official Rosemary DiCarlo told the Security Council last week there was "no breakthrough on the horizon," adding that the warring parties believe external support will let them pursue their aims by force. Translation: the arms are arriving, so the talks are not.

UN spokesperson Stéphane Dujarric said the organisation is alarmed by the continued drone strikes. That sentence has now been issued, in various forms, for three years. What to watch is whether South Sudan's protest stays diplomatic, since the one thing Abyei has never lacked is two governments willing to claim it.
