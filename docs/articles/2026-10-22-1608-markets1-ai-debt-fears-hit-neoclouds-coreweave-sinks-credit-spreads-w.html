CoreWeave Stock Sinks as Investors Discover AI Clouds Come With Debt

The biggest neocloud has lost roughly two-thirds of its peak value, carries 15% yield debt, and awaits a rescue from its largest shareholder, who says none is needed.

The wire said: CoreWeave’s market confidence erodes as bond yields spike and stock sinks · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 22, 2026 16:08 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

CoreWeave rents out Nvidia chips to AI customers and has funded its data center buildout largely with borrowed money. Rising interest rates and doubts about AI spending have hit the whole neocloud sector this month.

Why it matters

If the companies building AI capacity cannot borrow at tolerable rates, the buildout slows, and so do the earnings forecasts that have supported much of the stock market. Investors in tech debt and AI-linked shares are being reminded that leverage works in both directions.

CoreWeave shares are down 35% this month, according to CNBC, after an 8% drop on Thursday, leaving the company valued at about $31 billion. That compares with a peak of roughly $90 billion three months after its March IPO last year. Its unsecured bonds now yield about 15%, which is the market's way of saying it would like to be paid generously for its optimism.

Translation: a yield is the annual return a bond buyer demands, and when it climbs, borrowing gets more expensive for the issuer. CoreWeave's five-year credit default swaps, which are insurance against default, trade at 1,115 basis points, or 11.15%. Translation: a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, and 1,115 of them is what insurers charge when they have read the balance sheet.

The pain is not confined to one company. CNBC reported that Nebius is down 32% this month and IREN is down 39%. Australia's NextDC fell 40% in a day after cutting its earnings forecast, and Firmus scrapped its IPO citing market volatility. Nscale, which filed to list in New York last month, now has to decide whether to proceed. Investing.com reported that short sellers, who profit when stocks fall, are sitting on about $6 billion in unrealized gains.

The backdrop is not friendly. The 10-year Treasury yield stood at 5.12% on October 20, and the 2-year at 4.79%. Barclays credit analyst Andrew Keches told clients sentiment is "decidedly negative," citing how fast "AI vibes" shift. It is a notable development when a bank analyst puts the word vibes in a research note about corporate credit.

CoreWeave's exposure is specific. More than two-thirds of last year's revenue came from Microsoft, and Nvidia owns about 9% of the company. It has about $20.7 billion of debt due through 2030 and $14.9 billion after that, against $5.5 billion of cash at the end of the second quarter. Analysts surveyed by FactSet expect negative free cash flow of $10.1 billion in the third quarter, the worst since it began reporting. Baird's Rob Oliver estimates it needs roughly $30 billion in 2027.

Credit desk sources told CNBC the high-yield market is effectively on hold until after the midterm elections in two weeks, because of political pushback against data centers. One source said bond investors want permitting and regulatory questions "substantially resolved" before lending. Janus Henderson's Seth Meyer said pausing "makes sense." In other words, the money is waiting for a politician to say where the power plants may go.

CEO Mike Intrator sent employees a memo saying the company's financing mixes infrastructure-backed borrowing, corporate debt, equity and customer prepayments. He added that it has flexibility to raise equity over time. Translation: if borrowing gets too expensive, it can sell more shares, which existing shareholders tend to find less comforting than the CEO does.

Nvidia's own position is awkward. It invested $2 billion in January at $87.20 a share, and the stock now trades at $61.82. Jensen Huang said last week that CoreWeave does not need a backstop, and on Thursday told CNBC neocloud demand is "off the charts" but that "there's a scarcity of capital." That is a coherent view: customers are lining up, the money is not. It is also the view of a man who sells the chips those customers rent.

Not everyone is gloomy. Goldman Sachs upgraded CoreWeave to buy, with JPMorgan and Barclays also issuing bullish notes, pointing to its GPU-backed loans and investment-grade customers such as Microsoft and Meta. Goldman wrote that not all neoclouds are created equal. The market, for now, is treating them as if they are.

The next test is CoreWeave's third-quarter report next month, where investors will look for a financing plan. Oliver notes the capital is not needed until the first quarter, which gives management a few months to find buyers for its debt. The midterms, conveniently, arrive first.
