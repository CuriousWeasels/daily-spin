Russia Frees Jailed American, Declines to Read Envoy's Peace Papers Closely

Putin released 74-year-old Stephen Hubbard during Steve Witkoff's ninth visit, then called the envoy's proposals lacking in substance and agreed to talks after November 10.

The wire said: Russia frees jailed American Stephen Hubbard during Witkoff talks · DW

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 22, 2026 16:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Stephen Hubbard, an American, was captured by Russian forces and sentenced in 2024 to seven years in a closed trial on mercenary charges, which he denies. US envoy Steve Witkoff has been shuttling between Kyiv's side and Moscow to push a peace deal.

Why it matters

A freed prisoner is a real human outcome, but the Kremlin's cool reception of the US proposals suggests the war's core disputes remain untouched. Whether sanctions are paused or imposed shapes how much pressure Moscow feels to negotiate.

Russian President Vladimir Putin announced on Wednesday that Stephen Hubbard, a jailed American, had been released, doing so during talks in St. Petersburg with US special envoy Steve Witkoff. According to DW, Putin said Hubbard was already at the airport, and Witkoff said he was "going home to his family." NPR identified Hubbard as a 74-year-old Michigan native and said the release followed a request from President Trump. It is the rare diplomatic meeting in which the most concrete deliverable arrived before anyone sat down to discuss the actual war.

Hubbard was captured by Russian forces and convicted of being a mercenary, a charge he denies, and sentenced to seven years at a closed trial in 2024. Translation: a closed trial is one where the public is not invited, which tends to simplify the appeals process.

Mr. Trump said on Truth Social that he had asked Putin directly in a phone call last week. He wrote that the United States was "not required to give anything in return," and that no swap was needed. In a follow-up post, he said "numerous people" had called to apologize for criticizing his suspension of a new round of sanctions, which he described as a necessary concession. Translation: the United States gave nothing, except the thing he says it had to give. Readers are invited to hold both statements at once, as the administration evidently does.

The goodwill ended roughly where the paperwork began. Witkoff told Putin he had brought "some papers," which he described as ideas for a peace deal between Russia and Ukraine, to be read at Putin's leisure. According to DW, the Kremlin afterward said the proposals "lacked substance" and would not be of real use. Leisure, it appears, was brief.

Still, Putin agreed "in principle" to resume talks with Russian, Ukrainian and US delegates after November 10. Foreign affairs adviser Yuri Ushakov said they should focus on building a security system for Russia and Ukraine after the conflict ends. Translation: Moscow is willing to discuss what comes after the war, which is not the same as discussing how it stops. Ushakov also said a first-ever meeting of the leaders of China, Russia and the United States was being prepared, in Beijing.

This was the ninth Witkoff-Putin meeting, and it came a day after Witkoff met Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy in Norway. Zelenskyy told "Fox & Friends" that the front line should be frozen as a first step toward ceasefire talks. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said any capitulation by Ukraine would be "a capitulation by all of us." The European Union, which sent delegates from several member states to Oslo, said it expects Washington to brief it on the St. Petersburg talks, which is how Europe often learns the content of meetings about Europe.

Norwegian Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Store said Witkoff plans to return to Oslo, and that the Russians may be "looking for opportunities for diplomacy." That is possible. It is also possible that opportunities, like prisoners, can be released at no cost when the alternative is a new round of sanctions. Watch what happens after November 10.
