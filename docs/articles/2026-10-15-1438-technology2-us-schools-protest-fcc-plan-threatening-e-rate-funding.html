Schools Ask FCC Not to End Program That Pays For the Internet They Are Asked to Use Less

Libraries, principals and even the cable lobby tell the FCC that E-Rate, a roughly $2 billion-a-year connectivity program, is not finished just because the wires now exist.

The wire said: Schools across US protest FCC plan that could disconnect kids from Internet · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 15, 2026 14:38 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

E-Rate, created by Congress in the 1996 telecommunications law, discounts internet service and network equipment for schools and libraries. In June, FCC Chairman Brendan Carr led a 2-1 vote to seek comment on whether the program should be limited or sunset.

Why it matters

If E-Rate shrinks or disappears, schools and libraries would have to cover internet costs from local budgets, or cut programs. Because the program is funded by fees on phone companies that usually appear on consumer bills, the debate also touches what households pay.

Schools and libraries across the country told the Federal Communications Commission this week that they would prefer to keep E-Rate, according to Ars Technica. The program distributes more than $2 billion a year to help pay for broadband and network equipment. The FCC asked whether it should be limited or ended, and the public has now answered at some length.

Carr has argued that E-Rate drifted beyond its original purpose of basic internet access for educational use, and criticized districts for having "experimented with a massive increase in screen time for students." The proposal asks whether the program should be "limited or sunset to reflect today's extensive connectivity rates." Translation: the internet now reaches most schools, so perhaps the program that put it there can retire. The commenters replied that the bills for it still arrive monthly.

That is the core of the filings. Rock Valley Christian School in Iowa, per principal Paul Lapka, estimates its annual internet cost at about $3,120 and its E-Rate support at about $1,560. Half the bill, in other words, is the difference between reliable service and what Lapka called "higher local costs, delayed network upgrades" and pressure to redirect money from other priorities. The Texas Library Association added that only 35 percent of the state's public libraries receive E-Rate discounts, against 65 percent nationally, which it called evidence of unmet need rather than a need that has passed.

There is also a legal question. The American Library Association told the FCC that it lacks authority to terminate a program mandated by Congress, and that it is responsible only for administering it. Ars Technica noted that Carr could respond by arguing the original mission is complete, which would make ending the program a matter of paperwork rather than defiance. The FCC's own proposal asks whether the program has fulfilled that mission. Translation: the agency is asking whether a job can be declared done by the agency that would then be relieved of doing it.

The ALA also argued that the FCC lacks the authority and expertise to regulate screen time, and that US law leaves decisions about inappropriate content to school boards and libraries. Commissioner Anna Gomez, who voted against the proposal, put it more briefly: the FCC is not the nation's parent, teacher or school board. The Carr FCC has already cut E-Rate support for lending Wi-Fi hotspots and for Wi-Fi on school buses.

An unusual ally appeared in the docket: the cable industry. America's Communications Association argued that schools must keep paying for broadband and replacing equipment. Ending E-Rate would mean fewer purchases from the companies that sell the service, which Ars Technica said would upset broadband providers. It is rare for a lobbying group and a library association to agree that customers should continue receiving discounts.

Reply comments follow the first round, after which the FCC decides whether to proceed. The program has a funding cap of $5.2 billion, though actual payouts depend on demand and approvals. For now, the only consensus is that the internet is everywhere, and that someone still has to pay for it.
