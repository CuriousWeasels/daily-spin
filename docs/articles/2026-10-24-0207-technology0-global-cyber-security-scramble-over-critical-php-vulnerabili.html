Internet Discovers Its Foundations Have Had a Crack for a Decade, Turns Itself Off

Critical flaws in PHP, which Wiz says powers 70% of websites, sent governments and companies offline as a precaution, though no successful attacks have been reported.

The wire said: Race to update services' computer systems after cyber-security flaw found · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 24, 2026 02:07 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Researchers using AI tools from OpenAI found two serious vulnerabilities, nicknamed php2shell, in the PHP programming language, which runs behind a huge share of the web. Details leaked before a planned coordinated release, and patches were rushed out on Wednesday.

Why it matters

If your bank, council, garage or phone carrier has a PHP system lurking somewhere, it may be offline or awaiting a fix, and attackers are already probing for unpatched servers. Experts say the full impact could take months to show.

Much of the internet's back-end code turned out to contain two serious flaws, and large parts of it were switched off as a precaution this week. The BBC reported that the flaws sit in PHP, a programming language that has served web pages since the early 1990s. Security firm Wiz says it powers 70% of the world's websites, though the BBC noted it could not independently verify that figure. Prof Alan Woodward of the Surrey Centre for Cyber Security put the number of affected sites at about 30 million, which is a different number, and both are large.

Translation: PHP is the code that quietly assembles a web page when you visit it. Wikipedia, online shops and WordPress all rely on it. The flaws, according to the PHP Foundation, mean that under the right conditions an unauthenticated attacker could run code remotely on a server. Translation of that translation: a stranger could get your server to follow their instructions. Researchers rated the bugs 9.2 out of 10 for severity. Ryan Austin of Calif, who helped find them, called it "the most serious vulnerability I've ever found".

The bugs reportedly sat undetected for at least a decade, and what changed was the tooling. Researchers at Calif and Wiz used AI tools developed by OpenAI to analyse the code. Salil Kanhere of UNSW Sydney said the bugs were not new, and what changed was "our ability to find them". It is a reassuring account of progress, in that the same technology that found the problem is also expected to make exploiting it faster, according to several experts quoted by the BBC.

Then came the leak. The PHP Foundation had planned to publish its findings later this month alongside a complete fix, but details appeared on social media on Tuesday. Patches went out on Wednesday, with the Foundation urging all users to "update immediately". Amazon's security chief CJ Moses said it saw an attempt on a decoy system on 17 October and varied attacks after the leak, suggesting multiple actors. Cloudflare's Matthew Prince wrote that attempts went from thousands in the 10 hours before the announcement to millions afterwards.

Governments responded with the standard advice for a flaw you cannot instantly fix, which is to unplug the thing. The US National Security Agency and the UK's National Cyber Security Centre said organisations unable to patch should consider taking systems offline. Finland's government shut its public web services, the Dutch cyber-security agency pulled its own website, and the Office for Budget Responsibility took its site down. Wikimedia, Edinburgh council and some BBC pages also went dark. Pulling a cyber-security agency's website offline for cyber-security reasons is the sort of consistency that is hard to criticise.

The UK government said 32 services were unavailable and four partially so, according to tech minister Baroness Lloyd of Effra, who attributed this to precautionary measures. A spokesperson stressed this "does not mean they were hacked". Olly Whitehouse of the NCSC called the event "unprecedented" but said there were no indications of successful breaches. In Chepstow, garage owner Dean Presley told the BBC he could still carry out MOTs but could not print certificates, so he was writing everything on paper. That is the cost of a global security alert: one garage rediscovers the pen.

The effects reached beyond websites. Daniel Stewart of LRQA noted PHP is also found in network appliances, load balancers and embedded devices that tend to be overlooked. In Japan, local media reported that SoftBank temporarily suspended mobile emergency alerts for earthquakes and tsunamis, and customers were urged to arrange other ways to get warnings. The service is reportedly due back on Monday.

No successful attacks had been reported as of Friday morning, though experts expect more attempts now that technical details are public. Matt Hull of NCC Group said attackers once needed weeks to exploit a flaw and now need days or hours. He also warned that exhausted security staff need rest, which is advice the internet has historically ignored. Austin offered the optimistic view that organisations will now patch immediately. For many, the patching has begun, and the rest of the internet is waiting to see who was slower.
