SpaceX Throttles Ukraine's Starshield; Musk and White House Each Insist the Other Pressed the Button

Speed limits on military Starlink terminals disrupted Ukrainian drone defenses, and a reported two deaths and 17 injuries followed, while nobody in Washington claims the decision.

The wire said: Elon Musk’s dangerous grip on outer space · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 24, 2026 02:07 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Starlink has been a backbone of Ukraine's communications and drone operations since Russia's full-scale invasion, with Starshield serving as its military-grade version. On October 13, Ukrainian officials reported that terminals moving faster than about 99 mph were being throttled without warning.

Why it matters

A battlefield's connectivity now depends on a private company whose chief executive has shown he can restrict service, and the same concentration of power in launch and satellites touches GPS, banking and emergency response far beyond Ukraine.

According to The Verge and Ars Technica, SpaceX throttled Starshield, the military version of Starlink used by Ukraine, so that terminals traveling above certain speeds lost access. Ars Technica put the threshold at about 99 miles per hour (160 kilometers per hour) and cited analyst Michael Kofman saying service became unstable at even 80 mph. Ukraine's military reportedly received no advance warning. The Verge reported that the interceptors Ukraine uses against bomb-carrying Russian drones stopped working at high speed, which made attacks on energy infrastructure easier and led to a reported two deaths and at least 17 injuries.

Responsibility has since been passed around like a hot dish at a family dinner nobody wants to host. The Verge reports that Musk first said SpaceX has no control over Starshield and that the US military makes the decisions, while the White House denied taking action. Later reports said Musk suggested the slowdown to President Trump, who accepted it, and then that Musk ordered the restrictions on his own. Ars Technica says the restriction was imposed reportedly at Musk's order and possibly in consultation with Trump, and that both sides deny responsibility.

Translation: a throttle is a deliberate speed limit on a service. It is not an outage, and it does not happen by accident. Somebody chose a number, and a number was chosen. Whoever it was, the choice landed mostly on drones and interceptors that move fast, which is a considerable portion of the things a war is fought with.

President Zelenskyy, according to The Verge, called it a direct message from the US amid rising tensions with Trump. The Verge also noted that Ukraine must keep on good terms because it depends so heavily on American technology. It added that Starlink access has been restricted before, including in 2022, when coverage was not extended to a counterattack in Crimea. Ars Technica noted that SpaceX confirmed steps to block drone use in February 2023 and that it cut off non-whitelisted terminals in Ukraine in February 2026, which also hurt Russian units using smuggled ones.

The practical response is to rely on Starlink less. Ars Technica reported that Ukrainian firms were already shifting before October 13, using fiber-optic-tethered drones, ground LTE or 5G networks, laser links and radio mesh networks. An executive at Buntar Aerospace said the disruptions did not affect its operations because it had planned for this scenario. BlueBird Tech said Starlink is a small share of its products, though some configurations have no direct replacement. Which is how a company describes a dependency it hopes to retire.

The Verge argues the larger issue is concentration. Experts it quoted said SpaceX holds a de facto monopoly on launch, and that Falcon 9 is the most successful rocket in history. Starlink has over 10,000 satellites, launched cheaply on SpaceX's own rockets. Analyst Andrew Reddie called it almost an antitrust issue, since SpaceX has little incentive to launch rival constellations. Two experts also said they had heard complaints of rising launch prices, and SpaceX, which recently went public, did not respond to a request for comment.

Wendy Whitman Cobb told The Verge that a GPS failure would affect gas pumps, ATMs, financial transactions and ambulance dispatch. The experts did not claim Musk is about to switch off the world. Their point is that when one company is the gatekeeper to orbit, the policy question stops being whether he would and becomes why anyone must hope he won't.

For now, the speed limit stands, the denials continue, and Ukraine's engineers keep unspooling cable. The most reliable satellite backup appears to be a very long piece of string.
