Ukraine's Military Satellite Link Restored, Ownership Dispute Remains Unresolved

Kyiv's Starshield connection is back after a two-day outage, but nobody in the chain of command will say who switched it off.

The wire said: What is Starshield, and who limited Ukraine’s access to it? · Al Jazeera

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 15, 2026 14:38 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Ukraine uses Starshield, the military version of SpaceX's Starlink, to guide drones and relay battlefield communications. Its access was disrupted between Tuesday and Wednesday, amid growing friction between Kyiv and Washington over Russian oil facilities.

Why it matters

A wartime army discovered that its communications depend on a private company, a foreign government and a political mood, none of which it controls. For everyone else, it is a reminder that critical infrastructure is only as reliable as the least predictable person with access to it.

A Ukrainian Ministry of Defence source says the country's military Starshield connection has been restored, days after the service was reportedly restricted, according to Al Jazeera and NPR. Reuters, cited by Al Jazeera, reported that Kyiv is still asking SpaceX why the service was disrupted between Tuesday and Wednesday. So far, the explanation is that someone did it, and that someone is not the person asked.

Some background. Starlink is SpaceX's network of about 11,000 satellites in low Earth orbit, meant to deliver fast internet almost anywhere. Starshield, launched in 2022 and provided to the US government, is the military edition with deeper encryption. Translation: it is Starlink for people who would prefer that the enemy not read the messages. Ukraine relies on it especially to guide the drones it uses to attack Russia.

On Tuesday, Ukrainian officials said they lost access to important satellite communications, and that the disruption hurt military missions. That came after a heavy Russian strike of more than 100 drones and five ballistic missiles, for which Ukraine said it received no advance warning. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy wrote on X that early warnings of ballistic launches help save lives, and that this time there was none from partners.

Serhiy Beskrestnov, a defence adviser to Zelenskyy, said SpaceX had also imposed a speed limit on Starlink terminals, capping them at 100mph (160km/h). Translation: any drone or missile moving faster than a moderately ambitious motorist lost its guidance link. He said Ukraine's forces received no warning and that several missions failed.

Elon Musk, SpaceX's chief executive, denied involvement. He wrote that Starshield systems are owned and controlled by the US government, and that any limits are decided by the US military and ultimately the president. Al Jazeera noted that President Trump has insisted Washington is still sharing intelligence with Kyiv. Both statements can be true only in the sense that each party is describing a different part of the same elephant, and each is pointing at another part.

The context is not subtle. Zelenskyy told the Financial Times that US military officials had earlier warned Kyiv that intelligence would be blocked unless attacks on Russian energy infrastructure stopped. Al Jazeera reports that Washington has pressed Ukraine to stop hitting Russian refineries, and that Trump has blamed those attacks for rising diesel prices ahead of November's midterm elections. Zelenskyy rejected the idea of a glitch. In his words: 'It was not a technical problem; it was the will.'

The diplomacy has been brisk. Trump announced an 'energy ceasefire' over the weekend that Zelenskyy said was 'news to me', and Russia did not confirm it. Ukraine says it held off on refinery strikes while Russia continued hitting its energy grid, which Zelenskyy said caused blackouts for 600,000 people. 'We're done,' he told the FT, confirming Kyiv has resumed attacks on Russian oil infrastructure.

Musk, for his part, wrote that 'the slaughter continues' and that it is 'time for Zelensky to go'. That is an unusual statement from a man who says he does not control the switch. The service is back, but the question of who holds the switch, and under what conditions, is not.

What to watch next is whether SpaceX or Washington answers Kyiv's questions, and whether the next warning arrives before the next missile does.
