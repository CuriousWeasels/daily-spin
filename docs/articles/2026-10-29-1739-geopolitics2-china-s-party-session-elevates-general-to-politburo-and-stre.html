China Expels Record 33 Party Members, Fills Empty Seats by Emptying Others

A Communist Party plenum with the lowest attendance of Xi Jinping's rule ended with a record purge, 16 military figures among the expelled, and a call for more loyalty and more initiative.

The wire said: China elevates a military general to the Politburo and seeks to strengthen party discipline · NPR World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 29, 2026 17:39 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

China's Communist Party held its fifth plenum, a four-day closed-door meeting of its Central Committee, in Beijing this week. It ended Thursday with 33 expulsions, a new Politburo member from the military, and a renewed push on party discipline ahead of a leadership reshuffle expected in 2027.

Why it matters

The world's second-largest economy and its largest standing army are run by a small circle that keeps getting smaller, which affects military planning, policy predictability and succession risk. Investors and governments will read each purge for what it says about who actually decides things in Beijing.

China's Communist Party expelled a record 33 members as it wrapped up its fifth plenum, according to the BBC, citing a communique issued Thursday. Sixteen of those expelled came from the military. The party gave no specific charges beyond "serious disciplinary and legal violations", a phrase that has become routine under Xi Jinping's anti-corruption campaign and covers everything from bribery to being insufficiently enthusiastic.

The meeting also had the most empty seats ever recorded. The BBC reported that 279 Central Committee members attended, down from 315 at the fourth plenum in October 2025 and 364 at the third in July 2024. Translation: a plenum is a meeting where the party's senior members gather to approve decisions, and attendance is usually the easy part. It is the first time in Xi's 14 years that turnout fell below 300, and the committee began its term in 2022 with more than 370 members.

The cumulative numbers explain the thinning. The latest expulsions bring the total removed during Xi's third term to 51, compared with 35 in his second term and five in his first, the BBC said. Separately, the party's anti-corruption watchdog reported that 680,000 people across the party apparatus were disciplined in the first nine months of this year. That is roughly the population of a mid-sized city, all of whom presumably had a lanyard.

The military has taken the heaviest losses. Among those expelled were ex-generals Zhang Youxia and Liu Zhenli, former Xinjiang party chief Ma Xingrui and former diplomat Liu Jianchao, the BBC reported. Zhang had been vice-chairman of the Central Military Commission, which controls the armed forces; the seven-member body is now down to two, Xi as chairman and Zhang Shengmin as vice-chairman. Analysts at the Asia Society Policy Institute said the purge of the top brass is "in a category of its own" and raises questions about combat readiness, even as China unveils new hardware.

NPR reported that the party also elevated a military general to the Politburo. That was Zhang Shengmin, which restores the 24-seat body to 22 members after it was at one point left with 21. Translation: the party is refilling the top table with people who have not yet been removed from it.

The official message was that discipline must be tightened further. The party said it would "turn the knife inwards" to curb disorder, and a resolution praised Xi's "enormous political courage", according to DW. Xi has also warned against "two-faced" officials who profess loyalty while following their own interests, and against "nice guys" who use harmony as an excuse to avoid problems. Chong Ja Ian of Carnegie China told the BBC the campaign now serves "an effort to consolidate and maintain authority", and will expand depending on how secure Xi feels rather than on any real corruption issue.

This produces a tidy contradiction, which Kerry Brown of King's College London described to DW. Officials are told to show initiative and courage in solving problems, yet also to submit to strict party leadership, and the party is, in his words, "very, very risk averse". Chong said the proactivity Xi wants is limited to implementation, not decision-making. In short, officials are asked to take bold initiative within a perimeter set by someone else, and to be punished if they misjudge where the perimeter is.

The calendar adds urgency. The 21st Party Congress is due next year, and many analysts expect Xi, 73, to secure a fourth term. Term limits were removed in 2018, and DW noted he could stay on until 2032. Both analysts told DW that succession is a source of risk: naming a successor could weaken Xi, while not naming one invites a vacuum later.

Brown said the approach "kind of works" for now, noting that the economy is not great but not disastrous, and that the outside world is a mess. The next test is who fills the vacancies, and how many seats are empty at the next plenum.
