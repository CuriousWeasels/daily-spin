Investors Discover Portfolio Without AI Performs About Same as Portfolio With AI, Which Is Awkward

A Goldman Sachs index that strips out AI enablers briefly beat the S&P 500 this year, as tech slides and money flees to utilities, insurers and cruise ships.

The wire said: Investors are doing just as well this year by shunning AI. How to invest if the tech trade keeps fizzling · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 26, 2026 17:43 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

AI-related stocks have powered the S&P 500 for years, and now make up about 45% of it. This month tech has sold off, borrowing-heavy AI infrastructure firms have come under pressure, and investors have started looking elsewhere.

Why it matters

If your retirement fund is an index fund, nearly half of it is a bet on one theme, whether or not you remembered making that bet. The recent rotation shows what diversification looks like when it is suddenly needed.

A Goldman Sachs index that tracks the S&P 500 while excluding companies it classifies as AI enablers was up 12.4% through Oct. 22, CNBC reported. The regular S&P 500 was up 12%. The two are now roughly level after tech rallied Monday. In other words, for a brief moment, owning less of the most important technology in a generation paid slightly better than owning all of it.

The reason this is possible is concentration. Tech and AI-related companies make up about 45% of the S&P 500, according to CNBC. Translation: the index you bought to own America's 500 largest companies is closer to a very large position in a few companies that buy chips from one another. That arrangement is wonderful on the way up. This month the S&P 500 tech sector is down about 7%, while other sectors have held up.

The mood has soured most in the parts of the story that run on borrowed money. CNBC reported that investors have turned against debt and equity issued by companies like CoreWeave and Oracle. Meta fell almost 7% on Thursday after outlining more heavy capital spending and plans to issue additional shares. Translation: capex means spending on buildings and equipment, and issuing shares means asking investors to fund it, which they enjoy less when the bill keeps growing.

Elon Musk offered a colorful forecast on X. He said debt markets have almost entirely shut off money to any company without substantial revenue independent of AI data centers, and that more than 200 so-called neoclouds face 'guppy-level death and consolidation.' Translation: neoclouds are firms that borrow money, buy Nvidia chips, and rent out the computing power. CNBC noted that SpaceX, which Musk runs, sells computing capacity and competes with them, so the prophecy and the sales pitch happen to point the same way.

The numbers behind the worry are specific. CoreWeave and Nebius are down more than 30% over the past month and Iren is down 40%. The selloff began after the spread on CoreWeave's five-year credit default swaps reached levels generally viewed as signs of distress. Translation: a credit default swap is insurance against a company failing to repay its debt, and when the price of that insurance jumps, the people selling it are saying something.

The companies disagree with the diagnosis. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, whose company invests in several neoclouds, told CNBC demand is 'off the charts' and that the missing piece is working capital. Nebius's Marc Boroditsky said it is not carrying CoreWeave's kind of debt. Iren's Daniel Roberts said customers are discussing multiples of all the capacity it can bring online. Note that what is missing, by Huang's own account, is money, which is the one thing a leveraged company cannot do without.

Meanwhile the money has gone somewhere duller. CNBC said State Street's consumer staples, health and utilities sector ETFs are up 7% to 10% this month. Energy is up 10%, though that is tied more to the war in Iran. Oppenheimer's Ari Wald said the 'AI pendulum has swung back in favor of non-AI exposure,' and the firm upgraded commercial services and insurance. Berkshire Hathaway's Class B shares are up 8% this month, though they still trail the S&P 500 for the year.

Strategists are not unanimous on the sequel. MarketWatch reported that TS Lombard calls the AI theme a bubble destined to pop, but advises against trying to time it. That is useful guidance in the way that being told a storm is coming, date unknown, is useful guidance. The market data tells a mixed story: the S&P 500 proxy SPY sat at 764.9 on Oct. 26, and the 10-year Treasury yield was 4.96% on Oct. 22, which makes borrowing for data centers a more expensive hobby.

The kicker is that the safest trade of the moment includes a cruise line. Viking says much of its 2027 capacity is already booked, and Barclays upgraded it. For anyone who spent two years learning what a GPU is, the new advice is to own a boat.
