Eight States Sue OpenAI, Alleging ChatGPT Was Marketed as Safer Than It Is

Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, West Virginia, Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho and Montana filed separate suits accusing the company of putting profits ahead of child safety.

The wire said: Eight states sue OpenAI, saying ChatGPT isn’t safe for kids · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 15, 2026 14:38 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

OpenAI already faces lawsuits from families who say ChatGPT played a role in the deaths of loved ones. Eight US states have now filed their own consumer protection cases, according to The Verge and Reuters.

Why it matters

If the states win, chatbot makers could face civil penalties and court-ordered limits on how they market safety and handle minors' data. Every company selling a talking computer to teenagers will be reading the complaints closely.

Eight US states have sued OpenAI, alleging that ChatGPT is not safe for children and that the company misled the public about it, The Verge reported, citing earlier Reuters coverage. The suits were filed separately by Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, West Virginia, Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho and Montana. It is rare for eight states to agree on anything without a federal holiday attached, so the coordination is itself a data point.

The legal theory is consumer protection. Translation: the states are not claiming that chatbots are illegal, only that you cannot tell customers a product is safe if you allegedly knew otherwise. According to the complaints, OpenAI presented ChatGPT as a "safe, youth-appropriate tool that puts safety first," when it was in fact "dangerous to vulnerable users."

The list of alleged harms is long. The states cite "degrading critical thinking skills, encouraging paranoias and delusions, offering dangerous medical advice, and promoting unhealthy behaviors and relationships." They also point to lawsuits from family members of teens and adults who allege the chatbot played a role in their loved ones' deaths. Those cases are allegations, not findings, and the sources do not say how any of them will be resolved.

There is also a data angle. The states accuse OpenAI of collecting data from minors and not doing enough to keep underage users off the service. In practice, that means the age gate on a very popular product is being described as more of a suggestion. The suits additionally cite recent rogue AI hacking incidents as evidence of what they call recklessness, though the source does not detail those incidents.

The rhetoric is pointed at OpenAI's origins. Arizona's complaint says the company has "devolved" from a safety-minded nonprofit into "a cautionary tale" driven by a "profit-at-all-costs mindset." This is a fairly direct way of saying the mission statement and the business plan have stopped speaking.

OpenAI, in an emailed statement from spokesperson Drew Pusateri, said it has built "industry-leading age-specific protections" and pointed to parental controls, break reminders and age-appropriate model protections. It added that no system can be perfect. It also said it wants to work with states on policies that apply to "the entire AI industry - not just one company." Translation: please regulate our competitors too, ideally at the same time.

What the states want is concrete: civil penalties, a court order to stop what they call misleading statements about ChatGPT's safety, and in some states the deletion of personal data they say was unlawfully collected. Civil penalties are fines, and fines scale with the number of violations, which in a product with a very large user base is a number worth watching.

Next up is whether the cases are consolidated, how OpenAI responds in court, and whether other states pile on. The company has promised additional safeguards. The attorneys general have promised to read them as exhibits.
