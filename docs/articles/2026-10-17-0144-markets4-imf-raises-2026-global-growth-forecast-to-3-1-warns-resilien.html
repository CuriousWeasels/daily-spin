IMF Lifts Growth Forecast to 3.1%, Credits AI Boom, Warns Everything Else Is Running Out

The fund nudged 2026 growth up from 3% as chip and data center spending cushions a war-driven energy shock, but says the cushion is rapidly narrowing.

The wire said: IMF sees milder global slowdown as AI boom offsets war hit — warns resilience is nearing limits · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 17, 2026 01:44 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The IMF released updated forecasts at its annual meetings in Bangkok, with the Middle East war and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz still shaping energy markets. Its July report had assumed the strait would reopen by mid-July.

Why it matters

Growth is being held up by a narrow set of economies and a single investment theme, while oil reserves are drawn down and government borrowing costs sit near multidecade highs. If the AI spending story disappoints, the fund says U.S. stocks could fall 20%.

The International Monetary Fund raised its 2026 global growth forecast to 3.1% from 3%, CNBC reported, while keeping 2027 at 3.4%. Last year's growth was 3.5%, so the upgrade is best described as a smaller disappointment than previously expected. The fund credited AI-related investment for the improvement and then spent the rest of the report explaining why that may not last.

The fund's own phrasing is that the AI boom delivered a "demand impulse." Translation: companies are spending enormous sums on semiconductors, data centers and power generation, and spending counts as economic activity whether or not anyone has yet figured out what it produces. The IMF called this a large, front-loaded demand shock arriving before broad productivity gains have materialized. That is the economist's way of noting the invoice has come before the product.

The war is the other half of the story. The IMF now expects the Strait of Hormuz to reopen in March next year and conditions to return to pre-war levels by October. In July it had expected a mid-July reopening and a return to normal by March 2027. Gulf crude exports averaged 88% of pre-conflict levels in September as traffic picked up, which is progress in the sense that a partly open strait beats a closed one. Brent crude was last recorded at $126.55 a barrel, so ordinary people are not yet feeling the progress at the pump.

Governments have so far coped by diversifying fuel sources and tapping strategic reserves. Those are buffers, meaning stockpiles built for emergencies and now being used for the emergency. The IMF says global oil reserves are being drawn down by about 2.7 million barrels a day and the cushion is rapidly narrowing. Energy constraints could in turn impair the AI momentum that is currently holding up the forecast, a loop in which the thing doing the propping needs the thing running short.

Growth is also lopsided. Thirty-two economies now account for 95% of global growth, down from 54 in April. Taiwan is forecast to grow 10.7% and South Korea 3.3%, up from 1.1% in 2025, on chip demand. The U.S. is projected at 2.3% in both 2026 and 2027, and India at 7.0%, upgraded by 0.6 percentage point for this year. The euro zone is expected to manage 1.1%, and the Middle East and Central Asia region is projected to contract 0.3%, with Bahrain, Iraq, Kuwait and Qatar facing double-digit declines before double-digit rebounds in 2027. China is forecast at 4.5% this year and 4.0% in 2027, with CNBC noting a correction that the IMF cut its outlook.

Inflation is forecast at 4.7% this year, unchanged, and 4.1% in 2027, up from 3.9% in July. The fund puts the odds of global inflation above 5% in 2027 at about 35%, up from 30% in April. Chief economist-level advice follows: keep monetary policy "sufficiently restrictive," meaning interest rates high enough to hurt a little on purpose. Silvana Tenreyro also stressed central bank independence as governments face mounting financing pressure, a polite way of noting that finance ministries would like cheaper money. U.S. Fed funds sit at 3.88% against CPI inflation of 3.74% as of the latest data.

The bill for governments is arriving too. Benchmark yields in advanced economies have climbed to multidecade highs, and the U.S. 10-year Treasury yield stands at 5.1%. Translation: it costs more for a government to borrow, and the fund projects advanced-economy public debt rising to 115% of GDP by 2031 from 108% in 2025. It urges support for households facing higher energy and food costs to be "temporary, targeted, and nondistortionary," which is the fund's recurring request that governments help people without anyone noticing it has been done.

Markets, for their part, are unbothered. The VIX, a gauge of expected stock swings, was 16.46 and the SPY, a fund tracking the S&P 500, closed at 763.25. The IMF suggests this calm may partly reflect "AI-related exuberance." In its downside scenario, where AI productivity gains fail to materialize, U.S. equities fall 20% and global growth ends up 1.1% below baseline by 2028.

The kicker is that the fund's best case depends on a strait reopening on schedule and a technology paying off on faith. Last time it assumed a July reopening, and it has now moved the date by eight months.
