Brazil Votes Twice in One Month to Decide Whether It Meant It the First Time

Lula trails Flavio Bolsonaro by a few points or fewer in run-off polls, with the margin of error doing most of the work.

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 23, 2026 15:58 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Brazil's October 4 first round ended with Senator Flavio Bolsonaro at 47 percent and President Lula at just over 45, short of the majority needed to win outright. The two meet in a run-off on October 25, 2026.

Why it matters

The winner will lead Latin America's largest country for four years and shape whether the region's rightward shift continues, with consequences for trade, crime policy, Amazon protection and relations with Washington.

Brazilians return to the polls on Sunday, October 25, for a presidential run-off between Lula and Flavio Bolsonaro, according to Al Jazeera. Nobody cleared 50 percent in the first round, so the country has been granted a second election, as a kind of extended-play edition of the first.

The first round was closer than anyone could comfortably call a mandate. Bolsonaro took 47 percent to Lula's just over 45, with the rest scattered among mostly conservative candidates who did not advance. Lula had been slightly favoured, so the result caught many of his supporters off guard. Translation: the polls said close, and then it was close, but in the wrong direction for the incumbent.

The candidates offer a study in contrasts and inheritance. Lula is 80 and seeking a fourth, non-consecutive term. Al Jazeera credits him with sharp drops in unemployment and poverty and with progress against Amazon deforestation, balanced by rising household indebtedness. Flavio Bolsonaro is 45, runs for the Liberal Party and has become the de facto leader of the right because his father, former president Jair Bolsonaro, is serving 27 years for a coup plot after losing to Lula in 2022.

Flavio promises a more polished version of his father's politics, including a fight to free him from prison. He also pledges a militarised approach to organised crime in close partnership with the Trump administration, plus lower inflation and a debt ceiling, according to an adviser. After the first round he said he would 'redemocratise' Brazil by reforming a constitution written when democracy returned in the 1980s. Translation: the plan to restore democracy involves rewriting the document that restored it. There is heavy speculation, Al Jazeera notes, that this could mean judicial reform, which would be a convenient coincidence for a man whose father lost in court.

Lula has answered with campaigning and policy offerings. He has claimed credit for a constitutional amendment aimed at shortening the six-day work week and promised free public transport on weekends. Pro-Lula rallies have drawn millions, per Al Jazeera, and the polls have tightened. A campaign that offers a shorter work week and free buses has at least identified what voters, in the abstract, tend to like.

The polls disagree on how much tightening. Nexus on Monday had Bolsonaro at 46.5 percent to Lula's 45.9, down from a 47.9 to 45.1 lead a week earlier. AtlasIntel and Bloomberg had it at 49.8 to 47.6, compared with 51.1 to 45.7 on October 9. Quaest, on October 21, showed 45 to 44. In every case Bolsonaro leads and in every case the lead is within the margin of error. Translation: the pollsters have agreed that someone is ahead, but not that it matters.

Outside influence is a theme. Al Jazeera reports allegations that Meta restricted pro-Lula activity online, and notes the Trump administration's earlier tariffs and penalties on Brazilian judicial officials over the elder Bolsonaro's case. Observers worry Washington could back fraud claims should Bolsonaro lose. A corruption scandal around banker Daniel Vorcaro, meanwhile, has implicated figures across the spectrum, including Bolsonaro himself and several Supreme Court members.

A Bolsonaro win would extend a streak of far-right victories in Latin America, with Brazil the largest prize. A Lula win would keep the country as a holdout. Either way, the real test may come on Monday, when everyone finds out whether they agree on the result.
