Pennsylvania Orders AI Ads to Confess, Provides Several Exits

Governor Josh Shapiro signed a law requiring labels on AI-generated advertising, though The Verge reports its key terms are subjective enough to be negotiable.

The wire said: Pennsylvania will require labels on AI-generated ads · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 25, 2026 01:53 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Pennsylvania has enacted the Artificial Intelligence Deceptive Advertising Act, one of the first state laws requiring disclosure of AI-generated content. Governor Josh Shapiro signed it on Thursday, according to The Verge.

Why it matters

If you see an ad in Pennsylvania, or one made elsewhere that might air there, a label could tell you a machine had a hand in it. How often you actually see one depends on how courts and regulators read some very elastic wording.

On Thursday, Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro signed the Artificial Intelligence Deceptive Advertising Act, which requires disclosures on AI-generated ads, The Verge reported. Any ad that "has been significantly modified or generated by artificial intelligence" must carry a label. The state has thus asked advertising, an industry built on emphasis, to begin disclosing things.

The label must appear in the same format as the ad itself. Translation: a video ad needs a video label, an audio ad needs an audible one, and a text ad needs text. This covers still images, video, audio and text, so nobody can claim their particular medium was left out.

The law applies broadly. According to The Verge, it reaches TV commercials from multi-billion-dollar companies and also restaurants' crowded flyers about wing specials. In principle, the national brand and the sports bar are held to the same standard of candor, though only one of them has a legal department to read it.

It also applies regardless of where an ad was created. Because of that, viewers in other states may see disclosures on commercials that are also expected to air in Pennsylvania. Pennsylvania has, in effect, exported a footnote. Advertisers will probably find it cheaper to label everything than to build a separate Pennsylvania version.

Then come the exceptions. The Verge notes that "significantly modified" is subjective, so the line between a touched-up photo and a generated one is left to interpretation. The law also exempts "parody, satire, commentary or criticism," and anything "evidently fictional." What counts as evidently fictional is unclear, which is a notable gap in an advertising law, since much of advertising involves a cheerful animal discussing insurance.

The wording also suggests a disclosure is required only when content uses synthetic material in a way that creates a false consumer perception about a product or service. The Verge points out this raises the question of what qualifies as false and who decides. Translation: the duty to disclose may depend on whether the ad was deceptive, which is the very thing the label was supposed to help you figure out.

The office of State Senator Nick Pisciottano, who introduced the bill, did not immediately respond to The Verge's request for comment. Watch for the first enforcement disputes, which will define these terms far more than the statute did. Until then, a missing label could mean no AI was used, or only that the lawyers found an exit.
