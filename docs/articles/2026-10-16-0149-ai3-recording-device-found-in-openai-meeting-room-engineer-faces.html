OpenAI Finds Spy Device in Toilet Paper, Takes Two Days to Notice Room Had Guest

The FBI says a former engineer wrapped a USB recorder in tissue and plugged it into a conference room television; he faces up to five years in prison.

The wire said: Recording device hidden in toilet paper found in top-secret OpenAI meeting room · Ars Technica AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 16, 2026 01:49 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The FBI announced the arrest of Abdulrahman Alfozan, a 35-year-old Saudi engineer and US permanent resident, who worked at OpenAI for more than a year. Authorities allege he hid a recording device in a restricted executive meeting room.

Why it matters

AI labs now discuss technical progress, government deals and commercial partnerships in the same rooms as their televisions, and the case shows how much of that secrecy rests on who is allowed through a door. It also shows that a company's most sensitive conversations can be only a USB stick away from a wider audience.

According to Ars Technica, citing an FBI press release issued Tuesday, OpenAI discovered a recording device hidden in a restricted executive conference room where leadership discusses technical progress, government engagements and commercial partnerships. The FBI believes the device was planted by Abdulrahman Alfozan, a 35-year-old engineer from Saudi Arabia who became a permanent US legal resident in 2020. The company's first reaction to a secret room containing a secret microphone was, reasonably, to find out whose it was.

The method was modest. Alfozan allegedly wrapped a USB device in toilet paper and plugged it into the back of the room's television. Translation: the security measure being tested was the assumption that no one looks behind the TV, and apparently that assumption held for about two days. The FBI says the device was likely planted on September 28 and OpenAI found it on September 30.

The contents were encrypted, the FBI said, which meant OpenAI knew something had been recorded but not what, a situation familiar to anyone who has read a terms-of-service agreement. On October 5, the company installed a camera in the room. CCTV footage allegedly showed Alfozan trying to retrieve the device that same day, which suggests the camera was among the faster security upgrades in recent corporate history.

The FBI alleges that chatbot logs and the CCTV footage indicated Alfozan sought to spy on OpenAI and risked leaking trade secrets in a way that threatened national security. Translation: the company's own chatbot records are now evidence, a development that should make every employee reconsider what they type into a work tool. If convicted of violating laws against unlawful recordings, he faces up to five years in prison and a $250,000 fine. He has been charged, not convicted.

What OpenAI did next was patient. It did not immediately eject him. Instead, it gradually cut off his access to systems while working with the FBI to analyze the device's contents. Once enough evidence had been gathered, OpenAI timed his firing on Friday to coincide with the FBI's arrest. It is the corporate version of letting someone finish their coffee while the paperwork arrives.

The sources do not say what, if anything, was on the recording, who the intended audience was, or what motive the FBI will present beyond its allegations. Those are the questions to watch as the case proceeds. Meanwhile, expect the television at the back of that conference room to be inspected more carefully than most quarterly earnings.
