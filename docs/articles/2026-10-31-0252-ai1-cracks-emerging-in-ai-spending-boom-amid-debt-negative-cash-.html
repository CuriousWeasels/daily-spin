Big Tech Spends $740 Billion on AI, Investors Ask Where the Money Went

Alphabet, Amazon and Meta are burning cash, Nvidia is weighing co-signing a customer's loans, and Intel just lost 28% in a day.

The wire said: Cracks are emerging in the AI spending boom · TechCrunch AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 31, 2026 02:52 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft have said they will spend more than $740 billion in capital expenditures this year, mostly on AI data centers and chips. After years of rewarding such outlays, investors are now selling off the companies and their suppliers.

Why it matters

If the AI buildout runs on borrowed money and circular guarantees, a stumble could hit retirement portfolios holding these stocks, and rising memory prices are already making PCs more expensive.

Wall Street spent the past week discovering that spending $740 billion is not, by itself, a business plan. According to TechCrunch, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft laid out capital expenditure plans above that figure for this year. Translation: capital expenditure is money spent on things like data centers and chips that you hope will someday pay for themselves. On Friday the Nasdaq fell 2.9%, and Nvidia, Broadcom and Qualcomm fell 4%, more than 5% and 11% respectively.

Alphabet supplied the clearest case study. Its stock fell 12% on Thursday, its worst day on record, erasing $500 billion in market value, and then fell again. The company reported negative free cash flow of $9.1 billion in the third quarter, its second straight quarter in the red. Translation: free cash flow is what is left after paying for everything, and negative means the company spent more than it earned. Capex rose to $51.4 billion for the quarter, and Alphabet expects to spend up to $205 billion this year, with more to come in 2027.

Google's problem has a certain elegance. Its AI Mode chatbot is a hit, and TechCrunch reports it is eating into Search, the business that supplies roughly half of revenue. Search ad growth slowed to 12%, its weakest in five quarters. The company is spending billions to build the thing that may undercut the thing that pays for it.

Amazon reported negative free cash flow of $42.4 billion over twelve months, versus positive $14.8 billion the year before, and expects $220 billion in capex. Its cloud arm AWS grew 39%, and the stock still fell 7%. Meta reported negative free cash flow of $2.1 billion and guided to as much as $145 billion in capex. Its ad revenue grew 26%, and the stock fell 10% anyway. Microsoft was the exception, with $18.6 billion in positive free cash flow, partly because capex of $45.2 billion came in under its $50 billion projection. It blamed delayed capacity from third-party providers, not a change of heart.

Cash from operations is not covering the bills, so borrowing is. S&P Global Market Intelligence figures cited by TechCrunch show Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Oracle raised a combined $326 billion in debt in 2026, triple last year's amount.

Further down the chain, the arrangements get creative. The Information reported, and Bloomberg corroborated, that Nvidia is in talks to guarantee billions in debt for Nscale's planned West Virginia data center for Anthropic. Translation: the company that sells the chips would co-sign the loan of a customer building the facility that will buy the chips. Under a residual value guarantee, Nvidia would step in if Nscale defaulted on construction payments, take over the site temporarily and help find a new tenant.

Nscale is seeking an IPO valuation of up to $35 billion. It had about 25,000 advanced chips running in August against a plan of nearly 500,000. Runoff from its West Virginia construction site has flooded nearby homes more than once, and Microsoft pulled out of a planned English site after grid power slipped into the 2030s. Nvidia has extended roughly $170 billion in investments and credit support to neoclouds and large customers, according to The Information. Neoclouds, which rent out computing power without a cash-rich core business, saw CoreWeave, Nebius and IREN each fall more than 30% in October, per CNBC.

Anthropic's contract for the first building alone is $44.6 billion, according to Nscale's IPO filing. Anthropic itself filed to go public this week, and the Financial Times reported that Blackstone warned investors it may not get an investment-grade rating. Translation: a junk rating means higher borrowing costs, and more dependence on stronger partners to back its debt.

Intel offered the week's bluntest bill. Its stock fell around 28% Friday after it guided fourth-quarter revenue of $15 billion to $16 billion, against analyst expectations of about $17 billion, and a gross margin falling to 37% from 43%. The cut $12 billion from the value of the government's 10% stake, per CNBC. The stake is still up, as the stock has gained more than 70% this year.

The cause is a neat bit of ecosystem accounting. AI demand is absorbing memory and other components, raising PC prices and hurting PC sales. CEO Lip-Bu Tan said customers signaled a slowdown because of memory prices and availability. Intel's data center revenue grew 78%, but its next Arizona fab is not expected online until 2028.

The industry's central claim remains that demand for computing is strong, and the cloud numbers support it. The open question is whether anyone can be paid back at the prices being charged. Watch Anthropic's credit rating, because it will show how much faith lenders have in a business that needs a chipmaker to co-sign.
