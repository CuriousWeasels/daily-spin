Ukraine's Drones Get Their Satellite Link Back, Pending Whoever Owns the Switch

Kyiv says Starshield access is restored after a reported speed limit on drone terminals, a reminder of who controls the infrastructure of its war.

The wire said: Ukraine says its Starshield access is back (NPR World)

By The Daily Spin Desk · Geopolitics Oct 15, 2026 13:06 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Starlink and its military-grade Starshield system carry communications for Ukrainian forces, including drones. This week Ukrainian officials said SpaceX had limited terminals on fast-moving drones, amid separate worries about reduced US intelligence sharing.

Why it matters

Ukraine's ability to intercept missiles and strike deep inside Russia depends partly on systems controlled by a private company and the US government. If either changes the settings, civilians and soldiers feel it within seconds.

Ukraine said on Thursday that its access to SpaceX's Starshield has been restored, according to NPR. Starshield is the system Ukraine uses for offensive drone attacks on Russia. The restoration followed reports that access had been temporarily restricted, which is the kind of sentence that sounds like a software update and functions like a front-line event.

The restriction was described by Ukraine's drone czar, Serhiy Beskrestnov, who accused SpaceX of throttling Starlink terminals that are part of Starshield, Al Jazeera reported. Translation: throttling means deliberately slowing or capping a service, usually done to customers who have exceeded their data plan, not to armies. Beskrestnov said SpaceX limited service, without warning, on terminals mounted on drones flying faster than 160km/h (100mph). The cap hit interceptor drones, which are meant to be fast.

Who decided is, helpfully, disputed. Presidential adviser Serhii Sternenko said the limit followed a request from President Donald Trump. Elon Musk said the White House owns and controls the Starshield systems and that any speed limits are Trump's decision. In other words, the company, the government and the customer each have a different account of who holds the dial, and only the customer is being shot at.

NPR's Michel Martin discussed the reports with Democratic Rep. Mike Quigley of Illinois. The reports are not new in spirit. Reuters reported that in September 2022 Musk secretly ordered engineers to switch off Starlink coverage in the Russia-occupied Kherson region, thwarting a Ukrainian push to retake it. In 2023 he threatened to cut off military access unless the US defence department took over the costs. The Pentagon, Poland and other European allies later covered them.

The satellite episode lands alongside a separate worry. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said Ukraine's partners did not provide data from NATO's early-warning satellites this week, without naming the United States. Trump has denied that Washington stopped sharing intelligence. These satellites detect the heat of ballistic missile launches and can give up to 10 minutes of warning. Ground radar, by contrast, only sees missiles travelling 6,000 to 12,000km/h once they are already close.

Russian strikes that night killed at least 15 people and wounded dozens, Al Jazeera reported, with sirens sounding only seconds before or after missiles hit. Retired Lt-Gen Ihor Romanenko said the missing data hurts air defence because there is no time to respond, and that without satellite data Ukrainian drones and missiles lose precision deep in Russia. Ukrainian analysts link the pressure to Trump's push for an 'energy ceasefire' and quick peace talks. Moscow has not said it backs the ceasefire and kept hitting energy sites.

Ukraine says it prepared for this. 'We're ready for any turn of events,' Andriy Pronin told Al Jazeera. That is sensible planning, and also a tidy summary of modern warfare: the contingency plan is for the day the vendor, the ally, or the owner of the vendor changes its mind. For now the link is back. Watch who holds the dial next.
