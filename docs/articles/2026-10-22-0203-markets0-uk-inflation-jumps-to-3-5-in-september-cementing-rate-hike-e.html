UK Inflation Hits 3.5% as Fuel Soars, Making a Rate Rise Almost a Formality

Prices rose 3.5% in the year to September, and markets now give the Bank of England a 98% chance of a hike next month.

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 22, 2026 02:03 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

UK inflation has climbed from 2.9% in July to 3.1% in August and now 3.5% in September, driven by fuel costs tied to the U.S.-Iran war and disruption in the Strait of Hormuz. The Chancellor, John Healey, presents his first Budget next week.

Why it matters

A likely rate rise would make mortgages and other borrowing costlier just as fuel and living costs bite. It also narrows the Chancellor's room to offer relief without adding to the problem.

UK consumer prices rose 3.5% in the 12 months to September, the Office for National Statistics said on Wednesday, slightly above the 3.4% economists polled by Reuters had expected. It was the highest annual figure in almost a year, and the third consecutive monthly increase. In a rare show of consistency, the number went up again.

The main culprit was motor fuel. Prices at the pump rose 29.8% over the year, according to the ONS, and the rate of fuel inflation was the highest since August 2022, when Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine rattled energy markets. Translation: the cost of filling a tank has risen by nearly a third, which is the sort of statistic that does not need a spreadsheet to be felt.

Britain is a net energy importer, which CNBC noted makes it vulnerable to outside shocks, including the U.S.-Iran war and the struggle to move oil through the Strait of Hormuz. Translation: the country buys more energy than it sells, so when a distant waterway gets awkward, the UK receives the invoice.

The Bank of England is unlikely to stop at the headline number. Scott Gardner of J.P. Morgan Personal Investing noted that services inflation has accelerated, raising concern about what economists call second-round effects. Translation: the fear is that higher energy costs leak into wages and the price of everything else, so that inflation keeps going even if oil calms down. Gardner said recent growth in headline inflation had "undone the progress we saw in the spring."

Markets have responded with something close to certainty. According to LSEG data, traders price a 98% chance that the Bank raises its key rate by 25 basis points to 4% next month, with most also expecting another increase of the same size in December. Translation: a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so 25 of them is a quarter-point, a small number that arrives on millions of mortgage statements. The pound and UK government bond yields barely moved, suggesting markets had done their worrying in advance.

The data also lands on the Chancellor's desk at an awkward moment. Separate figures showed national debt estimated to have passed £3 trillion for the first time in September, while the cost of servicing it has risen sharply over the past year and the economy is struggling to grow. Healey, who presents his first Autumn Budget next week, has promised to maintain "fiscal discipline" while also giving businesses and families "some breathing space."

Those two promises sit in tension, as Suren Thiru of the Institute of Chartered Accountants in England and Wales observed. He called the Budget "a perilous high-wire act," noting that inflation increases pressure for cost-of-living support while pushing up borrowing costs and shrinking what is known as fiscal headroom. Translation: headroom is the gap between what the government plans to borrow and the limit set by its own rules, and it is currently getting shorter.

The practical upshot is a Chancellor asked to relieve price pressures without adding to them, and a central bank preparing to raise rates to cool prices while the government borrows more expensively. Both are acting sensibly, and both are likely to be criticized. Watch the Budget next week, then the Bank's decision in November.
