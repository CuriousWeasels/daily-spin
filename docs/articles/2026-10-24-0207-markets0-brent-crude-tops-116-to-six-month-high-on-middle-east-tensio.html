Brent Hits $116 as Markets Price In a War With a Post-Election Start Date

Oil reached a six-month high as traders bet on fresh U.S. strikes on Iran, while Qatar, unable to ship its own gas, agreed to buy American gas through 2054.

The wire said: Brent crude oil tops $116, a six-month high, as tensions swell in the Middle East · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 24, 2026 02:07 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The U.S.-Iran conflict is in its eighth month, with Iran's attack on Qatar's Ras Laffan facility in March and the blockage of the Strait of Hormuz having already disrupted energy flows. Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia have now added to the pressure.

Why it matters

Higher crude feeds into gasoline, shipping and food costs at a time when CPI inflation is already running at 3.74% a year. Meanwhile, the deal between Qatar and Cheniere suggests that the energy map is being redrawn for decades, mostly in America's favor.

Brent crude rose 3.1% on Friday to settle at $116.72 a barrel, its highest close since April 29, CNBC reported. U.S. West Texas Intermediate gained 3.8% to $103.27. Brent is up 6.2% on the week, its third straight weekly gain, which suggests traders have stopped treating the situation as a rumor and started treating it as a calendar item.

The calendar item is the midterm elections. According to MarketWatch, oil is near an Iran-war high and could climb further if the U.S. and Israel strike Iran after voters go to the polls. CNBC cited U.S. officials telling The New York Times that the two countries have plans for fresh strikes after the elections. Translation: the war has been scheduled around the news cycle, and the market is merely reading the schedule.

President Donald Trump added to the mood on Wednesday, saying the U.S. was not negotiating with Iran and that something would happen "fairly soon." Iran's army chief, per the state agency Tasnim, said the military is fully prepared for "asymmetric warfare." Translation: fighting a stronger opponent by means other than a fair fight. Neither statement is a forecast, but both are priced like one.

The supply side is getting no calmer. Houthi militants, allied with Iran, have kept striking Saudi Arabia. Saudi state media said a drone attack on the Shaybah oil field on Wednesday killed two people and injured two, and at least two people were killed and eight injured in an attack on King Fahd International Airport in Dammam. The State Department has ordered non-essential personnel out of Saudi Arabia, and Reuters reported that Saudi Aramco told staff at its Dhahran headquarters to evacuate and work remotely.

Elsewhere in energy, Qatar illustrated how a war can turn a market leader into a customer. QatarEnergy agreed Thursday to buy 4 million metric tons a year of liquefied natural gas (LNG: gas chilled into a liquid so it can travel by ship) from U.S. exporter Cheniere through 2054. The price is tied to the U.S. Henry Hub benchmark plus a fixed fee, and deliveries are expected to start in the 2030s.

The reason is plain. Iran's attack on Ras Laffan in March damaged two production lines, about 17% of Qatar's LNG export capacity, and Qatar's energy minister says repairs will take about three years. Qatar has declared force majeure every month since. Translation: a legal notice saying it cannot deliver what it promised, for reasons it did not cause and cannot fix. The minister also said Qatar is exporting "very little" because it cannot ship through Hormuz.

Analyst Alex Munton of Rapidan Energy said the contract suggests Qatar sees a risk that freedom of navigation may never fully return to Hormuz. LNG has no pipeline workaround. "They're just stuck," he said. UBS noted that Qatar also secured a $3 billion loan from Chinese banks that could help it buy more U.S. LNG to meet its delivery commitments, and Reuters reported Venture Global is in advanced talks to supply it a further 3 million tons a year.

Investors have noticed who is benefiting. Cheniere shares are up 26% since the war began and Venture Global shares are up 40%. Nearly 90% of analysts covering Cheniere rate it a buy. The deal is under 1% of the global LNG trade, but Munton called it about as big as an individual U.S. deal gets.

For now the sequence is simple: wait for the elections, then see whether the schedule holds. Oil traders will be watching both the ballot count and the Hormuz shipping lanes, and will presumably be wrong about at least one.
