Meta Fined $25 Billion by New Mexico After Declining Group Discount

A judge ordered the maximum penalty under state law after a jury found nearly 44 million violations, and Meta says it will appeal.

The wire said: Meta shocked by $25B fine after N.M. refused to join multistate settlement · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 23, 2026 02:08 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

New Mexico sued Meta over its public statements about Facebook, and a jury found the company had repeatedly misled users. Other states joined a multistate settlement, but New Mexico went to trial and the judge has now set the penalty.

Why it matters

The ruling shows that a state willing to go to trial can extract a far larger penalty than a negotiated deal, which may change how other attorneys general weigh settling against suing. Whether it survives appeal will signal how much consumer-protection law can actually cost a tech giant.

A US district court judge, Francis J. Mathew, ordered Meta to pay $25 billion for deceiving Facebook users, according to Ars Technica. New Mexico had asked for up to $40 billion, so the state technically left money on the table, which is the closest anyone in this story comes to modesty.

The judge said there is no "bright line rule" for setting such a penalty, then chose the highest level the state's Unfair Practices Act allows. The reasoning rested on a jury finding of nearly 44 million violations, which, according to the judge, arose from repeated public statements over more than 11 years. Translation: this was not one bad press release. It was a long-running communications strategy.

The trial record, Mathew wrote, showed that top leadership, including Mark Zuckerberg, knew the company was making misleading representations. One internal message quoted in the opinion described a plan to "Give them a chance to change their privacy settings but obviously don't encourage it." That is a rather precise description of how a privacy setting can exist without anyone using it.

The opinion also said Meta flagged only two of 2,000 unique apps tied to data misuse, after deciding to narrow its internal definition of misuse to avoid the operational burden of notifying people. Translation: the easiest way to reduce the number of data misuse incidents is to redefine data misuse. Mathew also found that Meta buried internal research on false and misleading content, and told users they received equal treatment while some celebrities and influencers got warnings before strikes and more decisions overturned.

Meta had hoped for something closer to its $5 billion Federal Trade Commission settlement over Cambridge Analytica, and expected to pay at most $3.4 billion, AP News reported. The judge replied that this settlement "cannot be viewed in a vacuum," and that penalties and consent agreements have not deterred Meta from deceptive practices. In other words, the earlier fine was treated as evidence that fines like it do not work, which is an awkward thing for a defendant to have cited as a benchmark.

Meta plans to appeal and argues the penalty is unconstitutional. Legal experts told Reuters the company has a decent chance of cutting the fine on those grounds. The company's spokesperson also said the case seeks "to impose a tax on speech," and that the ruling makes it "all but impossible to do business" in New Mexico, a state of about 2 million people. The statement closed with a nod to helping the country keep its competitive edge, which Ars read as a signal to the Trump administration that an AI-minded ally might be useful on appeal.

Critics say the fine is a drop in the bucket for a company that earns $200 billion a year. Meta's own accounting is less relaxed: the Verge noted that its third-quarter report showed close to $10 billion paid for the multistate settlement, and that without that expense operating income would have been $22 billion higher. A one-time charge that large tends to get noticed even by companies that consider such sums rounding errors.

Attorney General Raúl Torrez, whom the source identifies only as Torrez, called the ruling historic and said he will use the case as a blueprint for future enforcement. Nidhi Hegde of the American Economic Liberties Project told AP News that states should stay watchful as AI products grow, citing Meta's Muse assistant. For now the lesson for attorneys general is simple: settling gets you a check, but a trial gets you a larger one, at least until the appeals court weighs in.
