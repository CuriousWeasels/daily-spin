OpenAI Engineer Charged With Bugging Boardroom, Allegedly Using a Chatbot to Build the Bug

Prosecutors say Abdulrahman Alfozan hid a toilet-paper-wrapped recorder in an executive conference room, then asked a chatbot, via his wife's account, how to make it better.

The wire said: Alleged OpenAI spy vibe-coded a surveillance device using his wife's chatbot account · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 15, 2026 14:38 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The Justice Department says Abdulrahman Alfozan, a 35-year-old Saudi national employed as an OpenAI engineer since July 2025, was arrested on October 9 for allegedly planting a recording device in a restricted executive conference room. OpenAI has fired him and says it has no indication that model weights, source code or customer data were taken.

Why it matters

The case shows that even the companies building the most closely guarded AI systems can be undone by a physical gadget and a USB port, and it raises the stakes of AI firms' growing ties to Gulf investors. It also shows that chatbot logs are now evidence, which may concern anyone who asks software for help with a bad idea.

A Saudi national working as an engineer at OpenAI was arrested on October 9 for allegedly hiding an audio recording device in a conference room used by the company's leadership, according to a Justice Department press release cited by The Decoder. The room was used for confidential talks about research, product plans and business strategy. Alfozan did not have access to it, which is the sort of detail that usually matters in a room designated as restricted.

According to Ars Technica, the device was wrapped in toilet paper and plugged into the back of a television. The FBI believes it was planted around September 28. OpenAI found it on September 30 and determined that its contents were encrypted. Translation: someone had gone to the trouble of making the recordings private, which is a thoughtful touch for an alleged eavesdropper.

The indictment's most modern detail is the toolkit. Prosecutors say a chatbot account linked to Alfozan's wife was used between September 24 and 30 to enter prompts about building a "continuous audio recorder," adjusting Wi-Fi connectivity, improving "audio and live listening," and encrypting the SD card. The Decoder reports the account shared several identifiers with his own, and that some prompts referred directly to the recovered device, including its MAC address. Translation: a MAC address is a device's unique network ID, so the chatbot was apparently being asked about the exact gadget now sitting in an evidence bag. Neither source says the chatbot was ChatGPT. The Decoder summarized the arrangement as vibe-coded spy gear, meaning software written by describing what you want to an AI and hoping.

The timeline is where the corporate response gets interesting. Ars Technica reports that on October 5, OpenAI installed a camera in the room, and that footage allegedly showed Alfozan trying to retrieve the device the same day. OpenAI did not fire him immediately. It reportedly cut his access to systems gradually while working with the FBI to analyze the device, then timed his firing on Friday with the arrest. Nobody who has watched an HR department work will be surprised that the termination was scheduled carefully.

OpenAI said there are "no indications of this individual inappropriately accessing or exfiltrating model weights, source code, or customer data." FBI Director Kash Patel called protecting American "super intelligence innovations" "one of our highest counterintelligence priorities." If convicted of violating laws against unlawful recordings, Alfozan faces up to five years in prison and a $250,000 fine. Ars Technica notes he became a permanent US legal resident in 2020. The sources do not state a motive, and the charges are allegations.

The background is awkward for the industry. The Decoder notes that OpenAI's appetite for capital and infrastructure has pulled it toward Gulf states, with the UAE's MGX appearing as a partner or backer, including in the Stargate project. A May 2025 announcement with G42 covered Stargate UAE, enabled by a deal with the Trump administration allowing up to 500,000 high-performance AI chips per year. The sources draw no link between those arrangements and the arrest, and the reader should not either. Still, it is a lot of courtship for a company that also needs to know who is in its meeting rooms.

What to watch next is the court record, in particular whether prosecutors identify the chatbot or say who, if anyone, the recordings were meant for. Until then, the lesson is modest: the world's most valuable secrets can be protected by billions in security spending, and then by a television with an open USB port.
