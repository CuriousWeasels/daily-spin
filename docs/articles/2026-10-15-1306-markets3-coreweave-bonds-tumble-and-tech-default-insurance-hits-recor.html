CoreWeave's AI Bonds Fall to 81 Cents, Offering Investors a Discount on Optimism

Debt issued only months ago now yields above 15%, and insurance against US tech defaults has hit record highs, according to Investing.com.

The wire said: CoreWeave’s AI bonds tumble, raising red flags for the wider market (MarketWatch)

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & Economy Oct 15, 2026 13:06 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

CoreWeave is a company that rents out computing power for artificial intelligence and has financed its expansion largely with borrowed money. Its bonds, sold a few months ago, are now trading well below face value.

Why it matters

When lenders demand 15% to fund an AI data center company, it suggests the market is starting to price the AI buildout as a credit risk rather than a certainty. That matters to anyone whose pension, index fund or tech-heavy portfolio depends on the borrowing continuing.

CoreWeave's bonds, issued only a few months ago, fell to 81 cents on the dollar earlier Wednesday, yielding more than 15%, MarketWatch reported. Investing.com separately reported that the company's shares and bonds slid further as fears about AI debt mounted. The bonds were designed to be a way to fund the future, and the future has now been marked down 19%.

Translation: a bond trading at 81 cents on the dollar means a buyer pays 81 cents today for a promise to receive 100 cents at maturity, plus interest along the way. The yield, the annual return that price implies, rises as the price falls. A yield above 15% is the market's way of saying it would like to be paid generously for the possibility of not being paid at all.

For scale, the official data show the 10-year Treasury yielding 5.21% and the 2-year at 4.79% as of October 13. That is what the US government pays to borrow, and it is the baseline against which every other borrower is judged. CoreWeave is therefore being asked for roughly ten percentage points more than Washington, a gap known as the spread. Translation: the spread is the price of doubt.

The wider concern is in a second Investing.com report, which said AI credit jitters have pushed the cost of US tech default insurance to record highs. That insurance is the credit default swap, a contract in which one party pays out if a borrower fails to repay its debts. Translation: investors are paying record premiums to insure against tech companies not paying their bills, which is an unusual way to express confidence in the sector.

The mechanism is simple enough. Building AI infrastructure requires enormous amounts of capital up front, and the revenue is expected later. Debt bridges that gap, and it works well while lenders believe the later revenue will arrive on schedule. When they start to doubt it, borrowing costs rise, and the companies most dependent on new borrowing to fund old borrowing feel it first.

The macro backdrop does not help. The Fed funds rate stands at 3.88%, inflation at 3.74% year over year as of September, and unemployment at 4.2%. Long-term yields above 5% while the Fed's policy rate sits lower means borrowing money for years remains expensive, whatever central bankers say about short-term rates. The sources do not say what caused this week's slide in CoreWeave's bonds, and we will not guess beyond what they report.

It is worth noting what has not happened. The sources do not report a default, a missed payment or a bankruptcy filing. A bond at 81 cents is a warning, not a verdict. But the warning is arriving from the credit market, where lenders tend to read the fine print, rather than from the stock market, where participants tend to read the press releases.

What to watch next is whether the insurance costs keep climbing and whether other AI-linked borrowers see their bonds follow. If they do, the question will shift from how much the AI boom can earn to how much it can afford to owe.
