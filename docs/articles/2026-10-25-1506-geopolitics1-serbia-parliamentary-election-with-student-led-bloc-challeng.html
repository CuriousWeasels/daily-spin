Serbia Votes on Whether 14 Years of Vucic Deserve a 15th

A student-led bloc that began as a protest movement is now on the ballot, hoping to end the populist leader's long run in parliament.

The wire said: Serbia votes as student-led bloc challenges longtime leader Vucic · Al Jazeera

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 25, 2026 15:06 UTC · 1 min read

Catch up

Months of student-led protests in Serbia over corruption and accountability have now moved from the streets to the ballot box. Sunday's parliamentary election tests whether that energy can turn into seats.

Why it matters

The result will show whether a protest movement can beat an entrenched incumbent through ordinary voting, which matters for Serbia's direction and for how other European student movements judge their own chances.

Serbians voted on Sunday in a parliamentary election that could end the long hold on power of Aleksandar Vucic, according to Al Jazeera. The outlet reported huge turnout, which in any democracy is the sort of sentence that makes incumbents check their schedules.

The challenger is a student-led bloc. NPR reported that the students selected their own candidates to run for parliament and are aiming to end 14 years of government by Vucic, whom it describes as a populist leader. Translation: a populist is a politician who claims to speak for the people, a claim that gets awkward when the people turn up at the polls with other ideas.

The vote follows months of anti-government protests over corruption and accountability, Al Jazeera said. Protests and elections are different tools. A protest shows that people are unhappy, while an election counts them, and the counting is where movements historically discover how many of the unhappy actually registered a preference.

NPR noted that students have been protesting across Europe, with Serbia's election drawing particular attention. That makes Serbia something of a test case. If a movement born in lecture halls can win seats against a leader of 14 years, others will take notes. If it cannot, they will take different notes.

The sources are brief and give no results, polling figures or turnout percentages, so what the huge crowds mean remains unknown. Parliamentary systems also add a step: winning votes is not the same as forming a government, and the arithmetic of seats and coalitions decides who actually governs.

The students have done the hard part of getting onto the ballot. What remains is the part where the country decides whether it wants the person who has been running things for 14 years to keep doing so. Watch the final count, and then the coalition talks.
