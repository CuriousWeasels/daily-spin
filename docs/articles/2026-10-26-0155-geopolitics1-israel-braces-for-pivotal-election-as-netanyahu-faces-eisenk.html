Israel Heads to Polls Tuesday, Where Coalition Math Matters More Than Winning

Polls show Netanyahu's bloc and the opposition almost level, with a former army chief leading the challenge and half a million first-time voters deciding how close is close.

The wire said: Deeply divided Israel braces for pivotal election · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 26, 2026 01:55 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Israel votes Tuesday in its first general election since the 7 October 2023 Hamas-led attacks, after years of fighting in Gaza, Lebanon and Iran. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, the country's longest-serving leader, faces a challenge from former military chief Gadi Eisenkot.

Why it matters

The result will shape how Israel handles the war's aftermath, its courts, and the military draft of ultra-Orthodox Jews. It will also affect the wider region, where Israeli policy has been moving fast.

Israel holds a general election on Tuesday, and according to the BBC, the final polls, published Friday as Israeli law requires, put the governing coalition and the main opposition bloc almost neck-and-neck. About half a million Israelis are voting for the first time, and roughly 70,000 are reportedly flying home from abroad just to cast a ballot. This is what is known as civic engagement with a baggage allowance.

The central figure on the challenger side is Gadi Eisenkot, a former military chief of staff whose centrist Yashar party has regularly edged Netanyahu's Likud for first place in recent polls, the BBC reported. Translation: his party might get the most votes, which is not the same as getting the job. Israeli politics, the BBC notes, is the art of coalition-building, so the winner is usually whoever can assemble 61 seats in the 120-seat parliament. Finishing first earns a lead in the race and a lot of phone calls.

Eisenkot is described as calm, low-drama and relatively new to politics, with some calling him a non-politician. This is an unusual résumé for a man trying to unseat a media-savvy incumbent, and possibly the point. He has presented himself as coming from the periphery rather than the wealthy urban elite, and the BBC reports that earlier this month a pro-Netanyahu speaker used a racist slur about his Moroccan background during a TV debate.

His pitch is that the election is about the identity of the state. He promises a state commission of inquiry into 7 October, which a recent survey suggests more than 70% of Jewish Israelis support, according to the BBC. Netanyahu has refused one, arguing the Supreme Court would be biased against him. Eisenkot also vows to enforce court rulings on drafting ultra-Orthodox Jews into military service, a position the BBC says is popular with both right-wing and opposition voters. When a proposal polls well on both sides and still isn't law, that tells you something about how coalitions work.

Netanyahu is running on security under the slogan of No Surrender. He argues the wars in Gaza, Lebanon and Iran have reduced existential threats, and he casts rivals as soft, hinting that Jewish opposition parties might lean on Arab parties, who represent about a fifth of the population, to reach a majority. A supporter in Jerusalem told the BBC the prime minister bears some responsibility for 7 October but then changed the Middle East. That is the campaign in one sentence: accountability, with an asterisk.

The complication for Netanyahu is on his right. The BBC reports his base is projected to thin, with some voters drifting to the opposition and others to harder-line parties such as Itamar Ben Gvir's Jewish Power and Bezalel Smotrich's Religious Zionism. A Yedioth Ahronoth poll suggested more than 16% of first-time voters would pick far-right parties. Translation: voters leaving Likud for parties further right do not necessarily take votes away from the government bloc, they just move them to a different part of it.

Less than 10% of voters are undecided, and very few of them are considering crossing between blocs, the BBC says. So the outcome may turn on turnout, the new voters and the overseas arrivals. After three years of war, Israelis know the fight is coming. What they do not know is who will be able to form a government afterward, and on current polling that negotiation may take longer than the campaign.
