Google Releases Gemini 4 Carbon Two Weeks After Argon, Lets Almost Nobody Use It

The new model tops an independent intelligence index at 59 points, but access is limited to selected security experts while it goes through U.S. government pre-testing.

The wire said: Google Deepmind releases Gemini 4 Carbon just two weeks after Argon with gains across the board · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 29, 2026 17:39 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Google DeepMind released Gemini 4 Argon earlier this month and has now followed it with Gemini 4 Carbon, a further upgrade it calls its next frontier model. Carbon is being rolled out in stages because of its cybersecurity capabilities.

Why it matters

The release shows how fast the top AI labs are iterating, and that the most capable models may now reach security specialists well before the public. For businesses paying per token, the pricing and the extra reasoning Carbon consumes matter as much as the leaderboard.

Google DeepMind announced Gemini 4 Carbon on Wednesday, two weeks after Gemini 4 Argon. According to The Decoder, Carbon scores 59 points on version 5.0 of the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, up from Argon's 50, which puts it first. The Verge reported that Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra trail it. The model is available to almost no one, which is one way to stay undefeated.

Google says Carbon is built for long-horizon engineering work. Its internal teams reportedly ran it for days, during which it worked through dead ends on its own. Translation: an AI that does not quit when the first approach fails, which is a quality employers have long sought in humans and have now decided to buy by the token.

On coding, the lead is less clear. The Decoder reports that Carbon scores 59 on Artificial Analysis's agentic coding test, up from Argon's 53, but still trails Claude Code with Opus 5.5 by three points. It also falls behind Kimi K3.1 Pro, Muse Wonder and Grok 4.7 on one knowledge-work benchmark. Being first overall and not first at everything is the standard result for a leaderboard with nine separate exams.

The security numbers are why the rollout is cautious. Google says Carbon scores 88.2 percent on SEC-bench Pro, which tests finding vulnerabilities in real software, and 54.8 percent on ExploitGym, which tests turning those vulnerabilities into working exploits. Some of these tests were run without safety guardrails. Translation: the model was first asked what it could do at its worst, then asked to behave.

Because of this, Carbon is going through the U.S. government's voluntary pre-release testing process. Selected cybersecurity experts get access first, and the Fairwind Program will add more trusted partners later. Wider release to developers, businesses and consumers will wait for feedback and adjusted guardrails. Ars Technica summarized the situation as a frontier model you can't use.

There are costs behind the gains. Answer accuracy on Artificial Analysis rose from 48 to 58 percent, but the hallucination rate climbed from four to 11 percent. Translation: the model is right more often and also confidently wrong more often, which is how many careers have gone. On Vals.ai, Carbon uses 2.3 times as many reasoning tokens as Argon, and some finance and tax tasks take more than twice as long.

Pricing is unchanged at an introductory $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens. Artificial Analysis uses the list price, double that, and puts the cost of its index at $7.76 per task, about 23 percent below Claude Opus 5.5 at $10.04. The introductory discount runs through the end of 2026, so the bargain comes with an expiration date.

Ars Technica offered the skeptical view. Google calls Carbon a new model, but for all outsiders know it could be a post-training upgrade of Argon. Every frontier model scores in roughly the same neighborhood, and the gap to rivals is a few points. Whether those points matter will be settled once someone outside a security lab gets to try it.
