Accused OpenAI Bugger Stays in Jail; Folder Named 'Saudi Gov Docs' Did Not Help

A court granted prosecutors' request to keep a former OpenAI engineer detained, citing one-way tickets, Saudi bank accounts and a very candidly labeled iPhone folder.

The wire said: Accused OpenAI spy had folder called “Saudi Gov Docs,” FBI says · Ars Technica AI

Catch up

Abdulrahman Alfozan, a former OpenAI engineer, is accused of bugging a top-secret conference room at the company to steal its secrets, either for personal business gain or to benefit Saudi Arabia. He is a Saudi national and US permanent legal resident.

Why it matters

The case shows that frontier AI labs now face the kind of espionage risk once associated with defense contractors, and that governments treat model secrets as strategic assets. It also tests how courts weigh flight risk when a defendant allegedly has foreign state backing.

On Monday, a court granted the US government's request to keep Abdulrahman Alfozan in custody, according to Ars Technica. Alfozan is a former OpenAI engineer accused of bugging a top-secret conference room at the company. Prosecutors say he did it to steal secrets, and the only open question in their telling is who the beneficiary was: Alfozan himself or the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia.

In a Sunday filing, US attorney Craig Missakian argued that Alfozan should not be released while the case proceeds. Translation: when the government says a defendant is a flight risk, it is making a prediction about travel. Here, the prediction had considerable supporting paperwork.

Prosecutors say Alfozan had been chatting on WhatsApp with a member of the Saudi royal family while pursuing a permanent move back to Saudi Arabia, during what the government called the country's biggest AI push yet. Shortly before his arrest, the FBI says, he opened a folder on his iPhone called "Saudi Gov Docs." The same phone allegedly held photos of a second bug planted in the offices. Organization is a virtue, though rarely one that prosecutors say they are grateful for.

Then there are the tickets. The government says the FBI arrested Alfozan days before he could board a flight, and that he had booked a one-way ticket to Riyadh for himself, his wife and their baby. That ticket was allegedly "paid for by the Saudi government." After the arrest, his wife and son booked an earlier flight and left the US without him. Prosecutors then said a member of the Saudi military booked and paid for yet another one-way ticket for them, business class to Doha, Qatar.

The government also argued that Alfozan has "ample resources" after a decade of high-tech employment in the Bay Area, plus "at least two bank accounts in Saudi Arabia." In bail hearings, money is usually a reason to trust a defendant's ties to the community. In this one, it was presented as a reason to believe he has somewhere else to be.

To make the case that Saudi help can turn a legal problem into a travel itinerary, prosecutors pointed to an earlier matter in which former Twitter employees were charged with spying for the Saudi government. Charges remain pending against one of them, Ali Alzabarah, the government said, partly because a Saudi foreign official helped coordinate a next-day flight to Saudi Arabia shortly after Twitter confronted him. Prosecutors are essentially citing precedent for the airline schedule.

These are allegations from a prosecution filing, and Alfozan has not been convicted. The ruling only means the court agreed he should stay detained while the case moves forward. What to watch next is whether the government shows what, if anything, was actually taken, and whether OpenAI's conference rooms get a sweep for devices that were not on the agenda.
