AI Giants Agree to Honor One 'No' Instead of Four Separate Ones

OpenAI, Google, Microsoft and Anthropic back a shared robots.txt token that lets publishers opt out of AI training once, with most of them promising to comply sometime soon.

The wire said: The biggest names in AI are backing a new way to opt out of scraping · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 31, 2026 02:52 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Websites use a file called robots.txt to tell automated crawlers what they may and may not collect, but AI training preferences currently must be declared company by company. A proposed standard at the Internet Engineering Task Force would create one shared signal.

Why it matters

If the companies follow through, publishers get a single switch for refusing AI training instead of a growing list of crawler-specific rules. Whether Meta honors it, and how quickly the others adopt it, will decide how useful that switch is.

OpenAI, Google, Microsoft and Anthropic said they will adopt a new standard that lets publishers opt out of AI scraping across multiple companies at once, The Verge reported, citing earlier coverage by Search Engine Roundtable. Until now, a publisher wanting to say no had to say it separately to each company, like a homeowner posting a different No Trespassing sign for every neighbor.

The mechanism is a shared "policy token," proposed by Kevin Kelley, a standards technical lead at Anthropic, through the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF). Translation: the IETF is the body that writes the technical rules of the internet, and a token is a single agreed-upon phrase that crawlers are meant to recognize. It lives in robots.txt, the plain text file that has long told crawlers whether they may index, archive or train on a site.

The companies say they will keep their AI crawlers away from any site that carries the token. Microsoft added that it will offer finer controls, letting publishers opt out of AI training, of AI grounding, or both. Translation: grounding means pointing a model at a verified source to reduce hallucinations, so a publisher can now object to being used for training while still being cited as the answer key, or vice versa.

Microsoft also noted that sites will still appear in Bing search results regardless of their preferences. Google, The Verge noted, already lets sites control whether content appears in AI search features or is used for training without affecting search visibility. The arrangement reflects a long-standing industry principle: you may leave the AI, but you may not leave the index.

The rollout schedule is where the enthusiasm meets the calendar. Anthropic has already adopted the signal. Google and Microsoft plan to support it next year, and OpenAI says it will implement it in the "coming months." The proposal's author, in other words, is the first company to comply with it.

Meta's position is more careful still. Communications head Andy Stone signaled support, saying a common signal for multiple providers is "an important step," but the company did not say whether it will honor the token. Support for the idea of a standard and obedience to the standard are, as any publisher knows, separate line items.

The backdrop is a publishing industry facing a steady flow of AI crawlers. Other defenses already exist: the Really Simple Licensing standard lets publishers charge crawlers that visit, and Cloudflare blocks AI crawlers by default. The new token is the polite option, relying on the honor system, which robots.txt has always done.

Watch for the dates. A single universal opt-out is only as good as the last company to implement it, and at present that company has not named one.
