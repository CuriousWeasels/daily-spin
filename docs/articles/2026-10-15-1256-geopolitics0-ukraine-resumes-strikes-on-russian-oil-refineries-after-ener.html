Ukraine Ends Three-Day Energy Ceasefire That Only One Side Had Noticed

Zelensky says refinery strikes will resume, accusing Washington of cutting intelligence and Starlink support, while Moscow reports a fire at Ryazan and Kyiv demands Patriot missiles as the price of another pause.

The wire said: Russia accuses Ukraine of attacking oil refinery after Zelensky says strikes to resume · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 15, 2026 12:56 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

President Trump announced an "energy ceasefire" on Sunday, which Kyiv said it would honor if Russia did too. The Kremlin never said it agreed, and Russian strikes on Ukrainian power infrastructure continued.

Why it matters

Fuel prices in the US are driving American policy toward Ukraine, and Kyiv's air defenses depend on US intelligence and satellite services that Washington can adjust. If refinery strikes continue, Russian fuel output and the diesel supply deal both come under pressure.

Russia said Ukraine attacked an oil refinery in Ryazan, about 180 kilometres south of Moscow, early Thursday. According to Russia's defence ministry, four missiles and dozens of drones were intercepted, and falling debris started a fire at the site. The attack came hours after Volodymyr Zelensky told the Financial Times that Ukraine's restraint was over: "We're done."

The restraint in question was a three-day pause in strikes on Russian energy sites, which Zelensky says Ukraine observed at the request of the United States. Translation: an energy ceasefire is an agreement in which both sides stop hitting each other's power and fuel infrastructure. This one had a notable design feature, in that Trump announced it on Sunday and Moscow never confirmed it. Zelensky said Russia ignored it within hours, with strikes leaving hundreds of thousands of Ukrainians without power.

Ukraine's account of the rest of the week is less about the ceasefire than about what Washington stopped doing. Zelensky told the FT that US officials warned Kyiv, "if you will not stop [the strikes], we will block intelligence." Ukraine relies on US early-warning alerts of incoming Russian aerial attacks to organize its air defenses. Trump denied halting intelligence-sharing, so the claim currently rests on one side's word and the other side's denial.

Then there was Starlink. On Tuesday, the speed at which Ukrainian drones could fly while keeping their satellite connection was suddenly limited, and some suggested a technical glitch. Zelensky rejected that: "It was not a technical problem, it was the will." Elon Musk appeared to deny it was a glitch too, suggesting the Trump administration was responsible. Al Jazeera reported that Musk wrote that only the US military and Trump could limit Starshield's usage and speed. Starshield is the military version of Starlink, supplied to Ukraine under a US Department of Defense contract. Ukrainian defence ministry sources told the BBC that on-the-move operation had been restored, and that officials were still trying to learn why it was restricted. The BBC has asked the White House and the Pentagon for comment.

The motive, Zelensky argues, is diesel. Last week the US and Russia agreed to release 300,000 tonnes of Russian diesel to the US and global market, according to the BBC. Al Jazeera put the figure at 1.8 million tonnes, and the sources do not reconcile the two numbers. Either way, the BBC describes the deal as Trump's latest effort to lower fuel prices before the midterms, after the Iran war pushed up petrol and especially diesel costs. Zelensky called it effectively investing in Russia's war effort, and predicted the next deal would be gas: "Definitely it will be gas."

Meanwhile the war did not wait for the diplomacy. Local officials reported at least 13 people killed across Ukraine over the past day, as Zelensky said more than 850 drones were launched at dozens of cities. Kirovohrad region suffered its largest attack since the full-scale invasion began in 2022, with nearly 360 drones over ten hours, and at least three people were killed there. Deaths were also reported in Donetsk, Kherson and Volyn.

Zelensky has put a price on a second pause: more Patriot air defence missiles. The BBC reports Ukraine has been short of them since the Iran war consumed stockpiles. It is a clean bargain in principle, since Ukraine stops hitting Russian refineries and gets the interceptors that make Russian strikes less costly. It is less clean in practice, because the party being asked to pay is also the one reportedly asking Ukraine to stop.

Politics around the bargain is not calm. Al Jazeera reported that Trump called on Saturday for Zelensky to be replaced, and that Musk wrote "Time for Zelensky to go." In London, Prime Minister Andy Burnham promised to speed up delivery of lightweight multirole missiles, and European leaders were expected to take up the matter at a Brussels summit on Thursday. The EU has pledged $50 billion for Ukraine next year, against the $175 billion Kyiv plans to spend on defence, according to Al Jazeera.

The ceasefire's practical legacy is a useful data point: a truce works best when both parties have agreed to it. What to watch now is whether Washington answers the Patriot request, and whether the Starlink and intelligence questions get a more detailed answer than a denial.
