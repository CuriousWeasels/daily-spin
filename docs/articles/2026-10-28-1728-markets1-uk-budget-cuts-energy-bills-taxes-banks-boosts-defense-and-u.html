Britain's New Chancellor Taxes Banks, Praises Banks, and Asks Banks to Understand

John Healey's first budget lifts the bank surcharge to 6% for 15 months, cuts household energy costs and pledges £10 billion to Ukraine, and bank shares rose anyway.

The wire said: The UK cuts energy bills, taxes banks and boosts defense in high-stakes budget · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 28, 2026 17:28 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Britain's finance minister John Healey delivered his first Autumn Budget on Wednesday under Prime Minister Andy Burnham, with inflation at 3.5% and borrowing costs elevated. He cited the impact of the U.S.-Iran war on fuel prices as a reason for the cost-of-living measures.

Why it matters

The budget shows how a government with high borrowing costs and rising defense commitments pays for relief: it taxes the one sector that can absorb it and hopes markets stay calm. Households get lower energy bills, while the rest of the bill is deferred to a spending review next year.

John Healey, Britain's finance minister, delivered his first budget on Wednesday, announcing help with energy bills, a higher tax on banks, and more money for defense and Ukraine. According to CNBC, markets responded broadly positively, which is the highest grade a chancellor can receive and one that is usually revoked within a few weeks.

The backdrop is unglamorous. Inflation rose to 3.5% in September, driven by fuel costs, and CNBC reported that markets expect the Bank of England to raise interest rates next month. Healey blamed part of the pressure on the U.S.-Iran war, a conflict he said Britain did not want. Translation: Britain imports energy, so it pays for other countries' disagreements at the gas meter.

The relief package is broad. Standing charges on household gas heating bills will be removed, a tax break on electricity is extended through July 2027, and £100 checks go to households in the four lowest local housing tax bands, at a cost of £2.6 billion. Tariffs come off more than 100 grocery products, the minimum wage rises 3.9%, and a planned cut to tax-free savings thresholds was canceled. Translation: standing charges are the fixed fee you pay for the privilege of being connected, before you have used anything.

To pay for it, Healey said he would raise money from those who can afford it, while promising no tax rises on wages or dividends. Banks, gambling businesses and vape shops will pay. The bank surcharge rises by 3 points to 6% from January and lasts 15 months. Healey had earlier this month called the banks a jewel in Britain's economic crown, and he described them on Wednesday as making a huge contribution. Translation: he complimented the patient before the invoice.

The sector appeared to have expected worse. CNBC reported that Barclays rose 3.3%, Lloyds more than 2.6% and NatWest 2.5%. JPMorgan Chase was less relaxed, saying that singling out the industry for additional taxes again lacks principle. CEO Jamie Dimon has previously suggested the bank might rethink its Canary Wharf headquarters buildout if the government is hostile, though the company said Wednesday it is committed to the U.K.

On defense, Healey set a 2030 target date for spending of 3% of GDP and reaffirmed the NATO pledge of 3.5% by 2035. He announced £10 billion in economic and military support for Ukraine, built on loans and export finance guarantees, plus £500 million in urgent military funding for this winter. He also announced £2.5 billion for a Rolls-Royce engine partnership. IG analyst Chris Beauchamp said the announcement lacked detail but that the spending bonanza for Rolls-Royce and others is still on the way, only slowly.

The numbers around it were tidy. The Office for Budget Responsibility forecast £15 billion of fiscal headroom by 2029/30, debt rising slightly to 83.8% of GDP next year, then falling to 81.7% by 2032, and growth of 1.2% this year. Translation: headroom is the cushion between what the government plans to borrow and what its own rules allow, and it is the figure markets watch to decide whether to panic. Ten-year gilt yields fell 2 basis points to 5.323%, which is still a rate that makes a finance minister choose his words carefully.

Healey also promised to cut the welfare bill, which hit 10.7% of GDP in 2024/25, while saying he would not write off a million young people. His predecessor watered down similar plans after a rebellion in her own party. The details of paying for defense are left to next year's spending review, which is where the actual choices tend to appear.
