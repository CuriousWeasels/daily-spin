Finland and Norway Phone Putin for 90 Minutes, Agree Mainly to Phone Again

The two Nordic leaders held a rare joint call with the Russian president, with allies notified in advance and Denmark still unimpressed.

The wire said: Norway and Finland leaders hold rare call with Putin · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 27, 2026 16:54 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Russia launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, and European leaders have had little direct contact with Putin since. Relations between the US, Ukraine and Europe have soured since President Trump agreed to lift some sanctions on Russia, according to the BBC.

Why it matters

Europe is trying to secure a seat near peace talks that have so far been run largely by Washington, Moscow and Kyiv. Whether the Nordic channel helps or merely fragments Europe's message will shape how any settlement treats European security.

The leaders of Finland and Norway held a joint 90-minute phone call with Vladimir Putin on Tuesday, the BBC reported, making them the first European leaders to speak to him since Emmanuel Macron did so in July 2025. The stated aim, according to the offices of President Alexander Stubb and Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre, was to look at "future negotiations to help to end the war" in Ukraine. It is the diplomatic equivalent of finally returning a call that several people had been saying someone should return.

The two countries, both of which border Russia, were careful to say they were not speaking for "Europe as a whole". Translation: they would like credit if it works and a technicality if it does not. Støre said all allies were informed ahead of time, which is how one describes notification as distinct from permission.

Not everyone felt sufficiently consulted. Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen told broadcaster DR she was "very sceptical" and that the dialogue had not been "co-ordinated with any of the rest of us". Poland's Donald Tusk, by contrast, said Stubb had briefed him and that Europe's position on upcoming talks in the United Arab Emirates was "moderately optimistic". That is a measured way of saying that optimism is permitted, but only in small quantities.

The content of the call was less a meeting of minds than a confirmation that minds exist in different places. Støre said it showed "big differences in views", while Stubb called it "frank and substantive". Translation: frank means nobody agreed, and substantive means they said so at length. Putin, per the BBC, said "confrontational" EU and Nato policies were prolonging the war. The Kremlin added that a "just and lasting peace" would require Russia's interests to be considered, a condition that has the advantage of being impossible to disprove.

The backdrop is a shift in Washington. The BBC reported that ties between the US, Ukraine and its European allies have been souring since Trump agreed to lift some sanctions on Russia, allowing it to export oil to the US. Last week Stubb, Støre and Volodymyr Zelensky met US envoy Steve Witkoff in Oslo before he travelled on to see Putin in St Petersburg. Afterward, Ukraine, Russia and the US agreed to resume three-way negotiations that Russia had stopped for months. Zelensky said he was grateful for the "active support" of both Nordic leaders and spoke to them after the call.

The call also fits a growing view among European leaders that Europe should establish its own contacts with the Kremlin instead of reading about the outcome. Moscow regularly mocks European leaders and accuses the EU of prolonging the war by arming Ukraine. Yet Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said last weekend that a European negotiator at the table would be "good". Both sides also agreed to keep their direct phone line open.

What to watch next is whether the Nordic channel is folded into a common European approach or becomes a competing one. For now, Europe has a phone line to Putin, a skeptical Denmark, and a negotiating seat that remains mostly hypothetical.
