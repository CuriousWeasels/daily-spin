OpenAI Claims It Disproved a Core Computer Science Idea, Asks Humans to Please Check

The company says its models produced proofs in math, computer science and physics, and has recruited outside experts to decide whether any of it is true.

The wire said: OpenAI announces three big science discoveries and a new way to work with academics · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 27, 2026 16:54 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Less than a month after dumping hundreds of manuscripts it said solved long-standing math questions, OpenAI has announced three more results, this time with outside experts it calls "shepherds" guiding review. The Verge and The Decoder both reported the announcement.

Why it matters

If the Exponential Time Hypothesis really is false, hundreds of computer science papers lose their proven foundation, though the near-term effect on everyday software is unclear. The episode is also an early test of how science should handle breakthroughs written by machines.

OpenAI said it has used internal AI models to produce proofs in three fields: a wave-equation conjecture in mathematics, a refutation of the Exponential Time Hypothesis in computer science, and an existence proof for certain quantum field theories in physics. According to The Decoder, the company calls the manuscripts a kind of raw research data that still needs to be understood. This is a modest way to describe a claim that, if correct, would rearrange several textbooks.

The computer science result is the one with the largest blast radius. The Exponential Time Hypothesis, or ETH, says that solving the Boolean satisfiability problem (SAT) in the worst case takes time that grows exponentially as variables are added. Translation: there is no clever shortcut, only faster ways of trying every combination. The Decoder notes that with 100 variables you are already looking at a 31-digit number of possibilities, which is why nobody has been tempted to just wait it out.

OpenAI says its proof is a deterministic algorithm that counts satisfying assignments in subexponential time, which would refute the ETH for 3-SAT. The Decoder reports that hundreds of papers in graph theory, computational biology, databases and computational geometry build on the hypothesis. If the proof holds, their results would not automatically be wrong, but they would no longer be proven. Researchers would be informed that a load-bearing wall was decorative.

Practical payoff is another matter. The Decoder says SAT solvers, which check chip designs and test software, already handle formulas with millions of variables, because real-world problems are usually easier than the worst case. MIT professor Ryan Williams, asked what developers would notice first, said nobody knows yet. He allowed that practical progress is plausible within 12 months, "Or we may see nothing of the sort. I really don't know," he admitted. Few press releases are improved by this much honesty.

The checking will be done by Williams together with Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi of UC San Diego. The Decoder reports that Impagliazzo and Paturi proposed the ETH in the first place, with Francis Zane, which makes them the rare reviewers who get to find out whether their own idea has been retired. Williams writes that the group has signed no nondisclosure agreements, works unpaid and will share interim findings. In an open letter they stressed that taking part is not an endorsement. At their request, OpenAI published the written proof and a Lean proof, and committed to funding a conference on what it means.

Translation on Lean: it is a programming language that lets a computer check every step of a proof. The Decoder points out this does not guarantee the result is error-free, because Lean only confirms that the proof establishes the statement as written in Lean. Humans must still decide whether that statement captures the intended problem, so the machines have not yet eliminated the committee meeting.

In mathematics, OpenAI says its models proved Sogge's local smoothing conjecture, proposed in 1991, in every dimension. That would also settle the Kakeya conjecture and Stein's restriction conjecture in all dimensions, according to the company. The Verge notes that NYU professor Hong Wang won a Fields Medal only months ago for resolving Kakeya in three dimensions. No dedicated group shepherds this one. On an outside expert's advice, OpenAI simply published the proof and Lean file and let researchers decide how to check it.

The physics claim is a mathematically rigorous existence proof of certain interacting quantum field theories in four dimensions, including Quantum Chromodynamics, according to OpenAI. The Verge says the full material has not been released. David Gross will lead a group including Michael Douglas, Arthur Jaffe, Igor Klebanov and Nikita Nekrasov to shepherd the work. The Decoder explains that physicists have long relied on approximations that work extremely well without an exact mathematical basis.

The release method is itself contested. The Verge reports that OpenAI's own AGMAI panel of math advisers said just a day earlier that "we recommend that AI companies do not adopt" the practice of giving chosen people early access. It cited unequal access, borrowed reputation and biased selection. The panel preferred releasing output on scholarly repositories with Lean proofs, details on how problems were chosen, and funding for those working to understand the results. OpenAI acknowledged its approach could have its own issues, and its open letter asks the community how credit, early access and conflicts of interest should work.

The next thing to watch is the Williams group's interim findings. Proofs of this size tend to be judged on a schedule set by mathematicians rather than by announcement dates, and the shepherds are, helpfully, unpaid and unhurried.
