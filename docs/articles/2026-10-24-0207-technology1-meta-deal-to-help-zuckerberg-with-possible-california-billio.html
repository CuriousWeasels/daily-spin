Meta Agrees to Reimburse Zuckerberg for Staying in California, Home of the Tax He Might Owe

A new eight-year deal gives the Meta chief more stock and easier sales to offset a possible $10bn state levy, while investors worry about AI spending.

The wire said: Zuckerberg wins Meta deal to help him foot bill for possible California billionaire tax · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 24, 2026 02:07 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

California voters may decide in November on a one-time 5% tax on residents worth more than $1bn. Mark Zuckerberg told Meta's board he was considering leaving the state, and Meta responded with a stock arrangement it calls the Founder Alignment Agreement.

Why it matters

The deal shows how a proposed tax can turn into a negotiation between a company and its largest shareholder, with other shareholders and the company's finances in the room. It also lands as Meta's AI spending is already making investors nervous.

Meta has agreed to a new deal with chief executive Mark Zuckerberg intended to keep him in California, where voters may approve a one-time 5% tax on residents worth more than $1bn, the BBC reported. Zuckerberg's wealth is estimated at more than $200bn, and the BBC says the tax would cost him around $10bn. Meta's solution was to make sure he receives more stock, which is one way of describing a tax rebate with a governance committee attached.

According to a Wednesday financial disclosure, the arrangement came from a special committee of Meta after negotiations that were "robust" and "at several points, contentious." Translation: a group of directors spent time debating how much a company should do to keep its founder from changing his mailing address. The disclosure does not say who raised their voice.

The mechanism is a stock split that created a new type of share, with one new share going to each existing shareholder. Zuckerberg will receive more stock, more often, and may sell it quarterly, according to the filing. The BBC says the shares do not change his control over Meta, which he holds through the design of his holdings, but they make it easier for him to cash in on the company's value.

Zuckerberg told the board last month that he was considering moving his primary residence out of California and possibly selling his property there, including homes in Palo Alto and Lake Tahoe. Such a move, he said, "would be public and inevitably noted and commented upon by company employees." He also said it would "raise questions for management of the company going forward." This is a polite way of saying that the boss's moving van is also a corporate announcement.

The deal runs for eight years as long as Zuckerberg remains a California resident, and the BBC says it appears to apply even if the tax fails. So the protection against a tax that may not exist is already in force. Meta called it "critically important" to keep him leading from California during what it described as a transformation driven by artificial intelligence.

The BBC also notes the backdrop. Americans for Tax Fairness says about 225 billionaires live in California, and the BBC puts Zuckerberg's estimated effective state tax rate at under 1% a year. Meta's headcount is down 15% from a year ago, meaning at least 10,000 workers have gone, so the employees who would comment on his move are now fewer in number.

Then there is the cost of the AI race. Meta said it plans to spend as much as $145bn this year on AI projects and posted negative cash flow for the first time since going public 14 years ago. Translation: it is spending more than it brings in. CFO Susan Li said the stock split would also give the company more ways to finance future AI spending.

Investors were not soothed. Meta's shares fell more than 7% on Thursday, erasing more than $100bn of market value, according to the BBC. That is roughly ten times the size of Zuckerberg's possible tax bill, a comparison the company has not yet been asked to explain.

What to watch next is the November vote. If the tax passes, Zuckerberg gets to see how the stock arrangement works in practice. If it fails, he will have an eight-year agreement protecting him from a tax that never arrived.
