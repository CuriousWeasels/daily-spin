SpaceX Borrows $40 Billion for Chips, Predicts Rivals Will Be Unable to Do the Same

Elon Musk says the company closed the debt deal for Nvidia-style GPUs and forecasts a wipeout among more than 200 AI cloud upstarts.

The wire said: SpaceX closes $40 billion debt deal for AI chips, Elon Musk says · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 26, 2026 01:55 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

SpaceX has built an AI business it calls "Super Intelligence" that rents out advanced chips, and it has been in talks with banks and investors to fund more of them. On Sunday, Elon Musk said the $40 billion financing had closed.

Why it matters

If debt markets really are closing to AI companies without other revenue, the chip buildout may consolidate around a few well-capitalized players. That affects who controls AI computing capacity, and who is left holding the loans if the boom wobbles.

Elon Musk said on X on Sunday that SpaceX has closed $40 billion in debt financing to buy graphics processing units, according to CNBC. GPUs are the chips that power AI systems. It is a large sum to borrow for hardware, though it is the sort of figure the AI industry now treats as a rounding error with ambitions.

Musk said the company is investment grade, meaning rating agencies consider it likely to repay, and that it earns revenue from Starlink satellite internet and rocket launches. In a follow-up post, he said the financing is mainly callable debt for GPUs, expected to arrive in the first and second quarters of 2027. Translation: callable debt is a loan the borrower can repay early. The money is announced now and delivered later, which is a fine way to get a head start on next year's shopping.

Bloomberg reported earlier, citing people familiar with the matter, that the plan used a special purpose vehicle to keep the debt off SpaceX's balance sheet and protect its investment-grade ratings. Translation: a special purpose vehicle is a separate legal entity that holds the borrowing, so the parent company's books look tidier. Bloomberg also said Goldman Sachs was leading talks with investors, including Apollo Global Management. Musk's posts did not describe the structure, so how the final deal compares with the reported one is unconfirmed.

The chips are for a business that is now SpaceX's largest. According to its latest earnings release, the AI segment's third-quarter revenue rose ninefold to $7.56 billion from $813 million a year earlier. A rocket company whose biggest division is no longer rockets is a useful reminder that in this market, the business you were founded for is often the collateral for the business you want.

Then came the forecast. Musk said debt markets have largely shut off financing to companies that lack substantial revenue outside AI data centers. He predicted that more than 200 so-called neocloud companies, which rent out computing infrastructure built for AI workloads, will face widespread failures and consolidation, with only a few surviving. Translation: a neocloud is a cloud provider that does only AI, so when lenders get nervous, it has no other business to point to.

There is a tidy logic to the argument, and also an obvious interest in it. SpaceX sells access to advanced chips, so fewer well-funded competitors is not a tragedy for SpaceX. The sources do not say how many neoclouds are actually struggling to borrow, and the claim rests on Musk's own assessment.

Worth watching is whether the money arrives on schedule in 2027, and whether lenders treat the next AI borrower as a rocket-and-satellite company or merely as a rented warehouse of chips. With the 10-year Treasury at 4.96%, the cost of that distinction is not small.
