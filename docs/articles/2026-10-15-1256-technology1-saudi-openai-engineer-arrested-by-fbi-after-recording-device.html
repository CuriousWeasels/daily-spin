OpenAI Finds Spy Device in Toilet Paper, Uses Own AI to Investigate

The FBI says an OpenAI engineer hid a recorder behind a conference room TV; he faces up to five years in prison if convicted.

By The Daily Spin Desk · Oct 15, 2026 12:56 UTC · 2 min read
Catch upThe FBI arrested Abdulrahman Alfozan, a 35-year-old Saudi national and US permanent resident who worked as an OpenAI engineer, over a recording device allegedly planted in a restricted executive meeting room. He has not been found guilty of anything, and his lawyer has asked the public to withhold judgment.
Why it mattersCompanies building the most sought-after technology now face insider threats, not just outside hackers, and the case shows how much a determined person can attempt with a USB device and some tissue. OpenAI says it has no indication that model weights, source code or customer data were accessed.

The FBI says an engineer at OpenAI hid an audio recorder behind a television in a restricted executive conference room, where leaders discuss technical progress, government engagements and commercial partnerships. Ars Technica reported that it took OpenAI two days to notice. The device was reportedly wrapped in toilet paper, which is the first known case of concealment technology being sourced from the same supply closet as the target.

Prosecutors name the suspect as Abdulrahman Alfozan, 35, who had worked at the company since July 2025, according to the BBC. He was charged with using or attempting to use a device "to intercept oral communications". Translation: he is accused of wiretapping a room, which is a crime even when the room belongs to a company that is itself fond of listening.

The complaint, as described by the BBC, leans heavily on chatbot logs. It says that between 17 August and 16 September he asked a productivity assistant about meeting rooms and their availability, including where external partners usually meet OpenAI. It also alleges that in September he used a chatbot associated with his spouse to ask how to build and install a recording device and access it remotely. The allegation, in short, is that a man who built AI tools asked AI tools for help, and the AI tools kept records.

The timeline is tidy. The FBI says the device was likely planted on 28 September and found on 30 September, when OpenAI determined that its contents were encrypted. CCTV had placed Alfozan near the room on eight occasions in September, though he allegedly had no authority to be in meetings there. On 5 October, after OpenAI installed a camera in the room, footage allegedly showed him looking behind the television. The same day, the BBC reported, an unattended bag in another room was opened by security and contained a second recording device, his wallet and his ID. Leaving your ID in a bag with the evidence is, in investigative terms, a generous gesture.

OpenAI did not fire him right away. Ars Technica reported that the company gradually cut off his access to systems while helping the FBI analyze the device, then timed his firing on Friday to coincide with the arrest. Translation: the company let him believe nothing had changed until the moment everything had. OpenAI also said on X that it used its own AI models to understand the device and where it came from, which doubles as a product demonstration.

It is not clear what he intended to do with any recordings, the BBC noted. The FBI alleged that he sought to spy on OpenAI and risked leaking trade secrets in a way that threatened national security. OpenAI said it has no indications of inappropriate access to model weights, source code or customer data.

Alfozan's lawyer, William Pernik, told the BBC the allegations "appear wholly out of character" and asked everyone to withhold judgment, noting that an accusation is not a finding of guilt. Alfozan made an initial appearance in federal court in California on Tuesday, and a detention hearing is set for 19 October. If convicted, he faces up to five years in prison and a $250,000 fine.

The FBI investigation is continuing. What to watch is whether anyone explains the motive, and whether OpenAI's executives start holding their most confidential meetings in rooms with the televisions unplugged.
