Apple Rewrites App Store Rules to Say Out Loud That Nudify Apps Are Not Allowed

The updated guidelines require developers to prevent and remove nonconsensual intimate imagery, following months of criticism over Grok and a city attorney's order to pull 13 apps.

The wire said: Apple’s updated App Store rules go after ‘nudify’ AI apps · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 17, 2026 01:44 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Apple on Friday updated its App Store rules to more explicitly ban AI apps that generate nonconsensual intimate images, after criticism over sexualized deepfakes made with Grok on X earlier this year. A San Francisco city attorney also ordered Apple in July to remove 13 such apps.

Why it matters

App Store rules decide what hundreds of millions of iPhone users can download, so clearer language gives regulators, developers and victims a firmer standard to enforce. Whether it changes anything depends on enforcement, which is the part of any rulebook that is hardest to write down.

Apple has updated the App Store guidelines to more explicitly ban so-called nudify apps, which use AI to generate intimate images of people without their consent, according to The Verge. Under the new language, developers must "proactively prevent and remove" nonconsensual sexual images as part of Apple's content moderation requirements. The company has, in effect, written down that this was not supposed to be happening.

The timing follows a run of pressure. The Verge reported that X users earlier this year used Grok to make nonconsensual, sexualized deepfakes, including images of minors. Apple did not remove X or Grok from the App Store, though it told senators that it had threatened to. Translation: the strongest penalty available was mentioned in a letter and then kept in a drawer.

Protesters at Apple's WWDC event in June called on the company to remove Grok, X and all nudify technology from the store. In July, a San Francisco city attorney ordered Apple to remove 13 nudify apps, which the city said Apple did. That is one of the few cases in this story in which a request, a deadline and an outcome all appear in the same sentence.

The new rules attach consequences. Developers who "fail to implement adequate safeguards" or "don't remedy violations after notice" may be removed. Note the word "may," which is doing the work that "will" would do in a firmer document, and the phrase "after notice," which gives offenders a chance to fix things before being punished for them.

Apple also clarified its ban on bait-and-switch apps, meaning apps that add or change features, including nudification, after they have been approved. Translation: an app can no longer pass review as a photo editor and then become something else once the reviewer has moved on. Apps that facilitate human trafficking or the exploitation of children will be pulled and "blocked from alternative distribution." That clause matters because Apple now allows some apps to be distributed outside its own store in certain markets, and a ban is only worth as much as its reach.

The stakes are not abstract. Ars Technica reported separately that Bucks County Detectives arrested New Jersey deputy attorney general Brian Richard Fitzgerald, 51, on felony charges related to AI-generated child sexual abuse material. Police allege he used AI to turn photos of real minors taken from social media into sexualized images and shared them. These are allegations; no conviction has been reported in the sources.

According to Ars Technica, the case began when the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children received a tip in July that a Discord user had shared an illicit image. Detectives used digital forensics, subscriber records and IP addresses to trace the account to a home address. They say a search recovered prompts and scripts used with an AI program, along with chat logs in which the account holder allegedly admitted making multiple images. The Justice Department has said since 2024 that "CSAM generated by AI is still CSAM," which is a legal principle that required no software update.

The case shows the other half of the problem. Apple can govern what appears in its store, but the tools and the platforms where images circulate are many. A rule on one storefront is a necessary step, though not the only one. Watch for whether Apple's first removals under the new language target obscure apps or prominent ones.
