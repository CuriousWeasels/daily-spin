Israel Votes at Record Turnout, Produces Record Uncertainty

With 85% of ballots counted, neither Netanyahu's bloc (53 seats) nor Eisenkot's (48) has the 61 needed, and Arab parties hold the arithmetic.

The wire said: Netanyahu's bloc leading Israel election but short of majority, partial results show · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 28, 2026 17:28 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Israel held its first general election since the 7 October 2023 Hamas-led attacks and the wars that followed in Gaza, Lebanon and Iran. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu faced former military chief Gadi Eisenkot, who built his campaign on removing him.

Why it matters

A stalemate could mean weeks of coalition haggling or a sixth election in roughly seven years, leaving policy on the wars, ultra-Orthodox conscription and judicial reform in limbo. Netanyahu would stay on as caretaker prime minister in the meantime.

Partial results from Israel's election show Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc leading but short of a majority, according to the BBC. With 85% of ballots counted, Likud and its far-right and ultra-Orthodox allies were on track for 53 seats in the 120-seat Knesset. Eisenkot's Yashar party and three other Jewish opposition parties were set for 48. A government needs 61, a number neither side is close to producing.

Translation: the voters have spoken, and what they said was a tie with a few footnotes. Exit polls on election night first gave Eisenkot's "Change Bloc" 52 to 53 seats against 50 for Netanyahu. Broadcasters later revised that to a tie at 51 each in two polls, and a narrow Netanyahu lead in a third. Both men then addressed supporters as if the matter were settled, which it was not.

Netanyahu told Likud headquarters his bloc had won a "great majority" and called for a strong right-wing government. Eisenkot said the public had "spoken in favour of change" and that the "coalition of failure" had lost the people's trust. The two statements cannot both be true, though both can be delivered with confidence, and were.

The real story is the Arab parties. The United Arab List and the Joint List are on course for an unprecedented 19 seats, helped by strong turnout among Arab Israelis, who make up about a fifth of the population. Many had previously boycotted elections, arguing that voting legitimised a system treating them as second-class citizens. This time, the BBC reported, the wish to oust the most right-wing coalition in Israel's history drew them to the polls.

That turnout creates a problem for the opposition. Eisenkot and every other Jewish party in his bloc except The Democrats have ruled out governing with Arab parties, yet BBC analysis says that is currently the opposition's only path to a majority. Yair Golan of The Democrats sees an opportunity. Joint List chairman Yousef Jabareen said his party is ready to help oust Netanyahu, while United Arab List leader Mansour Abbas hopes the numbers reach 61. Netanyahu, for his part, says it is "forbidden" to rely on "anti-Zionist Arab parties."

Translation: the opposition's math works only with partners its own leaders promised not to use. The BBC notes that only one independent Arab party has ever formally joined a coalition, in 2021, and it did not last long. Such a government would also be fragile on issues like Gaza and the West Bank.

Netanyahu's options are no tidier. He could woo Eisenkot into a unity government, but Eisenkot spent the campaign asking Netanyahu to retire. He could peel off Naftali Bennett or Avigdor Lieberman, but both campaigned to draft ultra-Orthodox men, which puts them at odds with the ultra-Orthodox parties already in his coalition. The BBC adds that replacing those parties with Bennett and Lieberman's may not reach a majority anyway.

That leaves the default setting: another election. The BBC notes Netanyahu would stay as caretaker prime minister meanwhile, and may judge a rerun favourable. Some analysts doubt the Arab surge would repeat, and small right-wing parties that missed the 3.25% threshold may drop out and send votes to Likud. Israel held five elections between 2019 and 2022, so the machinery is well rehearsed.

The process from here is procedural. Roughly 430,000 to 480,000 absentee ballots from soldiers and diplomats remain to be counted, and President Isaac Herzog expects official results next week. He will then consult every faction and ask the leader he thinks likeliest to succeed to try forming a government, which could take weeks. Herzog urged everyone to arrive with "an open mind," a request that, in coalition talks, is rarely honoured but frequently made.
