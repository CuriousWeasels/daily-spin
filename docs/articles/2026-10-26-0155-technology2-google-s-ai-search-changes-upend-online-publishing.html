Google Summarizes the Web So Thoroughly That Nobody Needs to Visit It

Publishers that spent years optimizing for Google clicks report steep traffic losses after AI Overviews, while Google maintains the web is thriving.

The wire said: Google’s AI search changes upended online publishing · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 26, 2026 01:55 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

In 2024 Google began placing AI-generated summaries at the top of search results, and in May 2025 it launched AI Mode, a chatbot-style replacement for standard search. Publishers say the features have cut the clicks that fund their work.

Why it matters

If fewer people click through to the sources behind an answer, the outlets producing the underlying reporting lose revenue, which could eventually shrink the supply of information the summaries depend on.

The Verge reported this week that Google's AI search features have upended online publishing, a business that spent two decades learning to please Google and is now learning what happens when Google no longer needs to send anyone anywhere. The arrangement was once simple: publishers filled Google's index with content, and Google returned traffic. One party has found a way to keep the content and skip the second part.

The mechanism is straightforward. In 2024, Google introduced AI Overviews, which put an AI-written summary of web pages at the top of results, with source links below. Translation: the page you worked on for three days is now a footnote to a paragraph that quotes it. According to the Press Gazette, as cited by The Verge, the summaries appeared on almost a quarter of news-related searches in May 2024.

The numbers since have been unkind. The Wall Street Journal reported that Similarweb data showed search traffic to The New York Times falling from 44 percent to 35.5 percent of its visits in three years. Digiday reported that 19 members of the trade group Digital Content Next lost between 1 and 25 percent of their Google traffic between May and June 2025. DMG Media, owner of the Daily Mail and Metro, told Britain's Competition and Markets Authority that click-through rates fell by as much as 89 percent between June and July 2025, citing AI Overviews.

Google sees things differently. Search head Liz Reid said click volume remained "relatively stable," and that AI search sends more clicks to sites with forums, videos, podcasts, and posts from other users. Nick Fox, a senior vice president, told the BBC: "From our point of view, the web is thriving." Both statements can be true if the web is defined as the parts of it still receiving visitors. Pew Research, for its part, found people are less likely to click links when they see an AI Overview.

Courts have not been much help. US District Judge Amit Mehta dismissed an antitrust case brought by Chegg and Penske Media, which owns The Verge, and also rejected a proposed class action over AI Overviews filed by Arkansas newspapers. Regulators have had more success: following an order from the UK, Google broadly rolled out a toggle letting publishers opt out of AI Overviews and AI Mode without disappearing from regular search. The Verge notes this does not fix the traffic problem, since opting out of the summary mostly means opting out of the thing people are reading.

Google has made concessions at the margins. Sources are now more prominent, with pop-ups on hover and in-line links placed higher in responses. The company says 400 publishers, including The Washington Post, CNN, Axios, and the Financial Times, are in its News AI pilot program, which licenses content for new AI features. Reports from The Information and Digiday say it is also testing payments for content used in AI Overviews and AI Mode.

Meanwhile, publishers are leaning on newsletters, social media, and subscriptions, which is to say they are building direct relationships with readers, a strategy that has the advantage of not requiring Google's permission. The Verge adds that the summaries have begun expanding automatically for some queries, and that The Wall Street Journal reports Reddit is weighing cutting off Google over falling traffic. Whether licensing fees can replace lost clicks remains unanswered, though Google has at least started asking the question.
