White House Swaps Canadian Tariffs Like Trading Cards, Four Days Before Election

Trump removes some 50% duties that Sen. Susan Collins wanted gone, adds others on valves, carpets and waste products, effective Oct. 30.

The wire said: Trump dropping some Canada tariffs but 'replacing' them with other products · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 24, 2026 02:07 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Trump imposed 50% tariffs on a range of Canadian goods over the summer using Section 338, a 96-year-old trade law. Maine, which imports more from Canada than from any other country, has felt the strain, and tariffs are a top issue in Collins' Senate race against Democrat Troy Jackson.

Why it matters

The tariff list is a moving target, so businesses that rely on Canadian inputs cannot assume that relief on one product means relief overall. Consumers in affected categories may see prices shift depending on which goods are on the list this week.

The White House said Friday that it would drop tariffs on some Canadian goods and replace them with tariffs on other Canadian goods, according to CNBC. Trump signed a proclamation removing certain products from the list of items hit with 50% duties this summer. A White House fact sheet says they are being replaced with "certain valves, carpets, waste products, and others." The net effect is that the tariff list has been edited, not shortened.

The sequence of events matters. Sen. Susan Collins, R-Maine, announced that she had secured exemptions for nine categories of products she called crucial to Maine's agricultural industries. According to CNBC, her announcement, first reported by The Wall Street Journal, did not mention the new tariffs. The White House announced those less than an hour later. Translation: the good news arrived first, and the rest of the news followed shortly after.

The exempted items, according to Collins, include corrugated paper, plastic boxes and packing bags, refrigeration equipment, several kinds of machinery, and seaweed and algae products. The changes take effect Oct. 30, four days before the Nov. 3 election. Voters in Maine have already started casting ballots.

The political setting is not subtle. Collins is fighting for reelection in a state that imports far more from Canada than from any other country, and the tariffs are unpopular there. Her spokeswoman, Phoebe Keller, told CNBC the senator requested exemptions for products important to Maine and will continue to do so. Asked what Collins had agreed to in her conversations with the administration, that was the answer.

Her opponent, Troy Jackson, a fifth-generation Maine logger, was less impressed. He said Collins enabled the tariff policies and now wants credit for "bringing a cup of water to put out the fire." At the Oct. 6 debate, when Collins took credit for removing road salt from the list, Jackson replied, "We can't eat road salt." The seaweed exemption, presumably, is edible.

The White House framed the proclamation differently. Spokesman Matthew Middleton told CNBC it addresses "Canada's discriminatory treatment of U.S. commerce" and responds to continued retaliation. The fact sheet describes the changes as refining the scope of the tariffs and does not mention Maine. Translation: the exemptions were a policy adjustment, the additions were a policy adjustment, and the timing was a coincidence of the calendar.

Collins says she will keep seeking additional relief and encourage a broader trade agreement with Canada, which she called the largest U.S. trading partner. CNBC noted that the Census Bureau lists Mexico in that position. Both facts can be true at once only if one of them is checked.

Watch whether the Oct. 30 changes bring any broader deal, or whether the list simply keeps rotating. For importers of Canadian valves and carpets, the next four days are for filing paperwork. For Maine voters, they are for deciding what a cup of water is worth.
