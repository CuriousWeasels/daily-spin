Power Flickers at One London Data Centre, Britain Briefly Cannot Pay for Parking

A power event in a single Google Cloud zone disrupted UK banks, couriers and court hearings, reminding everyone how many things sit in the same few buildings.

The wire said: Google resolves cloud outage affecting UK banks and websites · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 22, 2026 02:03 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Google Cloud reported an issue with multiple services in its London region, europe-west2, early on Tuesday. The company later traced it to a power event at a third-party facility and said services were restored by evening.

Why it matters

When banking apps, couriers and court video links share the same few cloud providers, one faulty power supply can stop payments and parcels at once. Small businesses with no backup plan feel it first and lose the most.

Google Cloud said on Tuesday it had resolved a disruption at its London cloud region, europe-west2, that knocked out or degraded services for UK banks, businesses and the courts, the BBC reported. The company attributed the problem to a "power event" at a third-party facility. Translation: somewhere, electricity did something it was not supposed to do, and a large part of the British economy noticed.

Among the affected were Revolut, Lloyds Bank and HSBC, which confirmed disruption after thousands of user reports. Royal Mail, DPD and e-bike service Forest also drew complaints. HM Courts and Tribunals Service said it was aware of an issue with the video platform used for remote hearings, which is a rare case of a technical fault delaying justice for reasons other than the usual ones.

A "region", in cloud terms, is a cluster of data centres that a provider like Google, Amazon or Microsoft runs in a given area. Within each region are smaller "zones", so that a failure in one should not take down the rest. Google said the trouble began in one zone, europe-west2-b. That is the design working as intended in theory, and customers experiencing it as a general outage in practice.

The human cost was mundane and real. Rachael Ellen, a Revolut customer in Glasgow, told the BBC she could not pay for parking at a shopping centre and could not see a payment from a relative, while the app's help bot assured her nothing was pending. Revolut said its customers' money and data "remained safe throughout", which is reassuring, if slightly beside the point when the car park barrier is down.

Small businesses took a harder hit. Guy Baybutt of The Veg Box Company in Preston told the BBC his firm could not download orders, could not book parcels onto DPD's system, and lost sales when customers could not reach its website. Fresh produce, he noted, does not wait for a status dashboard to turn green.

Lloyds Banking Group, whose brands include Halifax and Bank of Scotland, did not confirm the cause of its problems, but said it understood the root issues had been resolved. Revolut, by contrast, said plainly that the Google Cloud outage was responsible. Both approaches are standard: one bank names the culprit, the other lets you infer it.

The incident came exactly a year after an outage at Amazon Web Services' largest cloud site knocked more than 1,000 sites offline, the BBC noted. Prof Feng Li of Bayes Business School said it showed how deeply economies depend on a handful of providers, and that what matters is how far failures spread and whether customers have workable alternatives. Google promised a full analysis on its service health dashboard.

Until then, the lesson is the familiar one. The cloud is, as ever, someone else's computer, and on Tuesday it was also someone else's power supply. The question for banks and couriers is whether the backup plan lives anywhere other than the same building.
