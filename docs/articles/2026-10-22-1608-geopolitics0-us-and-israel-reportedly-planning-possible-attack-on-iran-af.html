U.S. and Israel Reportedly Schedule Iran Strike for Day After Voters Finish Having Opinions

Officials are said to be planning a short, intensive operation after the November 3 midterms, weeks after the president said no attack would come before them.

The wire said: U.S. and Israel are planning for a possible attack on Iran, source tells NPR · NPR World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 22, 2026 16:08 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The U.S. and Israel launched a war against Iran in late February, and the U.S. last struck Iran in early September. Talks to end the conflict have stalled, and the U.S. maintains a blockade of Iranian oil exports.

Why it matters

High energy prices from the war are already hurting the president's party in the polls, and renewed fighting could affect oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz and the price of fuel. For Iranians, it would mean more economic hardship and likely more internet restrictions.

The U.S. and Israel are preparing plans for a possible attack on Iran in early November, after the U.S. midterm elections, a person briefed on war planning told NPR. Iran said it is prepared. The timing is a coincidence in the way that a dentist appointment scheduled after your wedding photos is a coincidence.

The New York Times reported on Wednesday, citing senior U.S. officials, that the two countries have developed specific plans for a short but intensive operation. Targets would reportedly include nuclear facilities, energy infrastructure, and Iran's stockpile of missiles and drones. One official told the paper the aim would be to extract diplomatic concessions by further weakening Iran's economy. Translation: bombing as a negotiating technique, used when the negotiating has not produced any negotiating.

The politics are not subtle. DW notes that polls show high energy prices from the war are hurting Donald Trump's Republican Party. On October 8, the president posted that the U.S. would not attack Iran before the midterms, citing productive discussions with Tehran. Israel holds its own election on October 27, and Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has campaigned on regime change in Iran. After November 3, DW says, Trump may feel less politically constrained, which is a diplomatic way of saying that voters are the last item on the pre-war checklist.

The preparations are visible. Axios reported in early October that the Pentagon told Central Command to finish preparations for resuming major combat operations. Two U.S. carrier strike groups are in the Arabian Sea, with a third expected by late November. Trump told Newsmax he was considering a strike on Pickaxe Mountain near Natanz, where U.S. officials say sensitive nuclear material may have been moved underground. CNN reported he is weighing a MOAB, a bomb whose blast wave is mostly above ground, which DW says limits its usefulness against underground sites. Translation: a very large bomb for a problem that is under a mountain.

Iran says it has been busy too. An adviser to President Masoud Pezeshkian told DW that Tehran's strategy is about preserving the regime rather than matching U.S. strength, and pointed to the rapid replacement of killed senior officials. Reuters, citing Iranian officials, reported that Iran is preparing for renewed attacks rather than a deal, and is dragging out talks to weaken Trump before the midterms. So both sides appear to be using the negotiations mainly as a calendar.

Iran has also warned Gulf neighbors that U.S. bases and critical infrastructure could be targets if they help any new operation. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said Iran would give diplomacy another chance given evidence of sincerity, but that choosing war would bring an even greater strategic failure for the U.S. Iran's latest offer, passed through Qatari mediators, would reopen Hormuz under Iranian control in exchange for lifting the blockade, releasing frozen assets and ending military action. Washington says the nuclear program must top the agenda, so the two sides are negotiating different meetings.

One wrinkle is Iran's leverage. The Wall Street Journal, citing Kpler ship-tracking data, reported that Hormuz oil flows are approaching prewar levels as the U.S. escorts tankers and shippers accept more risk for bigger payoffs. That raises questions about whether Iran's grip on the strait is slipping, though a new round of strikes could change that quickly.

For Iranians, the stakes are less strategic. A Tehran doctor told DW, anonymously because speaking to media is dangerous, that the economic situation has become extremely bad. Asked about underground nuclear sites, another woman said: "They are inside mountains." It is the one point on which she and the bomb planners seem to agree. Watch November 4.
