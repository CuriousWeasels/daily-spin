Senators Ask Who Slowed Ukraine's Drones, as Musk Says Not Him, Exactly

A bipartisan group wants the Pentagon's inspector general to explain speed curbs on Starlink in Ukraine, while SpaceX's owner points at the US military.

The wire said: US senators call for investigation into Ukraine's Starlink restrictions · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 16, 2026 15:38 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Starlink, SpaceX's satellite internet service, is widely used by Ukraine to guide drones and keep its forces connected. Between 12 and 14 October, Ukraine says speed limits cut off drones moving faster than 100mph.

Why it matters

A single privately owned network now sits underneath a country's defense, and nobody in public can say with certainty who is allowed to turn the dial. If the US government or its contractors can throttle it, Ukraine's allies and adversaries will both notice.

A bipartisan group of US senators has signed a letter asking the Pentagon's inspector general to investigate disruptions to Starlink in Ukraine, the BBC reported. The letter, led by Democrat Jeanne Shaheen, expresses "grave concern" about speed curbs reported between 12 and 14 October. It is the rare document that Republicans Thom Tillis, Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski and John Cornyn have signed alongside Democrats Richard Durbin, Jack Reed, Richard Blumenthal and Christopher Coons.

The mechanism is simple. According to the BBC, Starlink limited speeds on Tuesday night to 100mph (160km/h), so any weapon travelling faster than that lost its connection to the network. Translation: the internet stayed on, but anything built to move quickly stopped being able to use it. A drone that cannot talk to its network is, for operational purposes, a very expensive glider.

The senators want the inspector general to determine whether the restrictions came from the US defense department or its contractors. They say the curbs reportedly degraded Ukraine's ability to defend against Russian drone and missile attacks on civilian infrastructure. They also cite reports of glitches affecting intelligence-sharing and threat warnings, including incidents in which US diplomatic, military and security personnel were unable to reach designated bunkers during Russian air raids.

Responsibility is where the story gets tangled. Elon Musk wrote on X that Starshield systems are "owned and controlled by the US government," and that any limits on speed or usage are decided by the US military and ultimately the president. Starshield, per the BBC, is the government and military version built on the Starlink network, and both belong to Musk's SpaceX. So the company that owns the satellites says the customer decides how they are used, which is a tidy description of a vendor and an impressive way of saying it was not him.

A US official told the BBC that "no such request was ever made by the president." That statement covers the president, not the Pentagon or its contractors, which happens to be the exact question the senators are asking. NPR reported that Ukraine now says its Starshield access has been restored, and President Volodymyr Zelensky said on Friday that speeds were still being brought back. "Has the speed been fully restored to what it was before? No," he said. "Is the speed being restored now? Yes."

The backdrop is not subtle. Zelensky has accused the US of withdrawing intelligence-sharing to pressure Kyiv into ending attacks on Russian energy sites. That follows an "energy ceasefire" announced by Donald Trump on Sunday, which Zelensky says Russia ignored within hours; the Kremlin says it never agreed to it. Zelensky has also criticized a US-Russia deal to release 300,000 tonnes of diesel to the global market, which the BBC describes as part of Trump's effort to lower fuel prices before the midterms after the Iran war pushed costs up.

So the sequence is a ceasefire one side says was never agreed, a fuel deal aimed at American gas pumps, and a speed limit on drones that nobody has yet admitted to setting. The inspector general now gets to find out whether the last item was a technical glitch or a policy. Either answer is awkward for someone, and the awkwardness is what senators are counting on to produce an answer.
