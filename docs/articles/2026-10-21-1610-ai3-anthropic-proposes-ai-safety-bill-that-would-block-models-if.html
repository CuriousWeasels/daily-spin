Anthropic Drafts AI Safety Bill, Sets Danger Threshold at 50 Deaths

The draft, reported by Politico, would let a judge halt a risky model's release, but only after registered evaluators, possibly paid by the labs, sound the alarm.

The wire said: Anthropic pitches AI safety bill that may block a model if 50+ people could be killed · TechCrunch AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 21, 2026 16:10 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Politico reported that Anthropic has been circulating a draft federal AI safety bill, and TechCrunch summarized its terms. A spokesperson confirmed the draft, saying it reflects the company's Advanced AI Framework, which it has pushed with lawmakers since June.

Why it matters

If Congress ever acts on AI, drafts like this one will shape the starting point, including who checks the models and who is allowed to stop a release. Whether the industry's preferred referee is independent in practice could matter more than the headline numbers.

Anthropic has written a bill that would let the government stop an AI model from being released, according to Politico, as summarized by TechCrunch. The trigger is a "foreseeable, imminent risk" that more than 50 people could die or more than a billion dollars in property could be destroyed. The company is asking Congress to regulate the industry, which is the kind of request that tends to arrive with a detailed set of preferences attached.

The risk must come from one of three sources, per Politico: a biological weapon, a model evading its developer's controls, or a cyberattack. The 50-person and billion-dollar thresholds resemble those in California's recently passed Senate Bill 53, which uses them to define "catastrophic risk." Translation: the law has a minimum body count, and the industry has now agreed on roughly where it sits.

The bill relies on "independent evaluators" registered with the U.S. Commerce Department. These evaluators could be paid by the AI labs themselves, provided the "payment is not contingent on the evaluations' results," Politico writes. TechCrunch notes it is unclear how the system would stop payment from influencing the findings. Translation: the inspector is hired by the restaurant, but promises to be paid whether or not the kitchen passes.

If an evaluator finds an imminent catastrophic risk, it has 72 hours to tell Commerce. Commerce then has to ask a Washington, D.C., judge to halt the release. The draft expressly bars any federal agency from stopping a model on its own, per Politico. So the government gets a brake pedal, but must first obtain a court's permission to touch it.

The scenarios are also a bit removed from what is worrying the industry right now, TechCrunch says. The recent concern is agentic hacking, a rash of incidents in which AI programs apparently disobeyed their operators and hacked websites on the open internet. That is a smaller disaster than 50 deaths, but it has the advantage of having actually happened.

TechCrunch observes that labs warn their technology could end human civilization while mostly resisting mandatory oversight. Their usual alternative is a voluntary system of embedded evaluators, supposedly neutral observers placed inside AI organizations. Against that, Anthropic's draft goes somewhat further than other industry proposals. Nvidia's CEO, Jensen Huang, recently argued that the industry needs no new laws at all.

Anthropic did not immediately respond to TechCrunch's request for comment. What to watch is whether any lawmaker picks up the draft, and whether anyone asks who signs the evaluators' checks.
