Machado Returns to Venezuela After Washington Decides the Time, Finally, Is Right

The Nobel laureate came home to cheering crowds on Sunday, in a country where the US removed Maduro in January and now seems content with his successor.

The wire said: Opposition leader María Machado returns from exile to a Venezuela reshaped by Trump · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 27, 2026 02:33 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Maria Corina Machado left Venezuela in December 2025 to collect the Nobel Peace Prize and could not return for about ten months. In January, US troops removed President Nicolás Maduro, and his former vice-president, Delcy Rodríguez, took over.

Why it matters

Venezuela holds some of the world's largest oil reserves, and who governs it, and on whose terms, shapes energy markets and the prospects for a negotiated vote. Machado's presence on the ground gives the opposition a visible leader at the moment Washington-brokered talks are under way.

Venezuelan opposition leader Maria Corina Machado returned home on Sunday and was greeted by thousands of supporters along a route from the western state of Lara to Caracas, according to the BBC and DW. She announced her arrival on social media with the words "I am here. With you," NPR reported. It is a warm homecoming for a woman who, by the BBC's account, had made at least eight attempts to get back by air and by sea.

The first thing to understand is who had been stopping her. The Venezuelan government was one obstacle. The other, according to the BBC, was the Trump administration, which insisted the "time wasn't right." DW reported that Machado has blamed travel restrictions and flight cancellations partly on Washington's worry that her presence could inflame tensions during a transition, and partly on interference by Rodríguez. Translation: she was the opposition leader the US favoured, provided she stayed somewhere else.

Her exit in December was a nighttime affair. DW reported she sheltered in the Argentine embassy in Caracas and was smuggled out with US help to receive the Nobel Peace Prize in Oslo. On the way back she wore her trademark white T-shirt and blue jeans, leaned out of a sunroof, and shouted that "this country is giving lessons to everyone," according to DW. In Guacara, AFP reported, she sang the national anthem in a packed plaza.

The politics have been rearranged since she left. The BBC reports that US troops removed Maduro on 3 January and flew him to New York, where he faces drugs, weapons and torture charges and has pleaded not guilty. Rodríguez took over and has, in the BBC's words, turned the page with Washington. President Trump has called her "wonderful" and "a terrific person," which is notable given that he previously described her administration as made up of "narco-communists." Translation: the label depends on whether the oil flows.

The oil is not a metaphor. The BBC reports that the US recently signed a deal giving it a 100-year concession over 65bn barrels of Venezuelan crude, reached with a private company, North American Blue Energy Partners, run by businessman Alejandro Betancourt. Machado has criticised the deal, but, the BBC notes, she aims her objections at Caracas and Betancourt rather than at Trump, to whom she gave her Nobel medal and whose support she wants. This is a careful way to complain about a contract without complaining to the counterparty.

Rodríguez responded on state television, saying "the show could have been avoided" because "the airports are right here." The BBC said state television otherwise ignored the event, which supporters livestreamed. Machado's spokesperson said she was willing to sit down with the government. The BBC adds that Rodríguez indicated she might be prepared to talk, though she needs Washington's goodwill to avoid her predecessor's fate. It also notes reports that Secretary of State Marco Rubio largely dictates decisions by regular WhatsApp calls. Those are reports, not confirmed facts, but they suggest the group chat is carrying real weight.

Machado still faces charges ranging from treason to conspiracy of insurrection, which she and her supporters have long called politically motivated and baseless. Rubio said her "safe return" was "the direct result of the US-supported reconciliation talks." Analyst Beningno Alarcon told the BBC her ability to mobilise remains intact. A study by the think tanks Polis and Nexo found three times as many street protests in the first nine months of this year as in all of 2025, with repression of protests down by more than half.

So Venezuela now has an interim president Washington likes, an opposition leader Washington has finally allowed home, and a negotiation both are expected to attend. Whether the crowds along the highway translate into an election, and whether anyone has set a date, is what to watch next.
