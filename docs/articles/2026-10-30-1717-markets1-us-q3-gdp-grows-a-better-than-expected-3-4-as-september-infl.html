Economy Grows 3.4% While Prices Climb 3.6%, Giving Fed Two Reasons to Keep Watching

Third-quarter GDP beat forecasts as September inflation accelerated, a day after the Fed held rates at 3.75%-4% and promised to evaluate incoming data.

The wire said: Third-quarter U.S. GDP increases a better-than-expected 3.4% as September inflation accelerates · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 30, 2026 17:17 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The Federal Reserve voted Wednesday to hold its benchmark interest rate steady, saying it could afford to wait for more information. On Thursday, the Commerce Department delivered a large amount of it.

Why it matters

Strong growth and sticky inflation make rate cuts harder to justify, which keeps borrowing costs for mortgages, car loans and business debt elevated. The data also suggests energy prices, not just AI spending, are shaping what households pay.

The U.S. economy grew at a seasonally adjusted annualized rate of 3.4% in the third quarter, beating the 3.1% economists expected and the 2.2% recorded in the second quarter, the Commerce Department reported Thursday, according to CNBC. In the same release cycle, the department said inflation picked up in September. The economy, in other words, is doing more of everything, including getting more expensive.

Translation: an annualized rate means the quarter's growth is projected as if it lasted a full year. It is a way of making a three-month period sound more decisive than it was.

The Fed's preferred inflation gauge, the personal consumption expenditures price index, rose 0.5% in September, the largest monthly gain since March. That put the annual rate at 3.6%, a tenth of a point below forecasts. Excluding food and energy, the core reading was 0.3% for the month and 3.1% for the year, matching expectations. Officials generally treat core as the better guide to longer-term trends, since they cannot do much about the price of gasoline, a fact they tend to mention only when asked.

The Fed's target is 2%. The 3.6% headline figure is therefore a target in the way a restaurant's posted closing time is a target.

The September jump was driven by energy. Gasoline and other energy goods rose 9.2% for the month, and energy goods and services broadly rose 5.4%. Economists fear this could spread elsewhere, CNBC reported. Services prices excluding energy and housing rose 0.4% and housing costs rose 0.3%, which suggests the spreading may already have begun in a modest, unhurried way. Brent crude stood at 123.1 in the latest official data, which gives the worry some context.

There was a better number buried in the quarterly view. PCE prices rose 2.1% on the headline measure and 2.5% on core, down from 5% and 3.3% in the second quarter. So inflation over the quarter looked calm, while the final month looked less so. Both statements are true, which is the central difficulty of reading economic data.

Growth came from the usual suspects. Consumer spending rose 3.8%, the same as the prior quarter. Private domestic investment soared 19.4%, with equipment spending up 20.9% and intellectual property products up 8.4%, reflecting the artificial intelligence build-out. Real sales to private domestic purchasers, which Fed officials use as a proxy for underlying demand, rose 4.9%.

In September itself, consumer spending rose 0.5% against a forecast of 0.6%, and personal income rose 0.2% against a forecast of 0.3%. Households are spending slightly faster than they are earning, a pattern that can continue until it cannot. Meanwhile, initial jobless claims fell to 194,000, below the 200,000 forecast and near levels last seen in the late 1960s.

The Fed, led by Chairman Kevin Warsh, held its rate at 3.75%-4% on Wednesday. Warsh said the period ahead is "information rich." Thursday's data added to the pile and suggested, in CNBC's words, that the Fed could have more work to do. Chris Rupkey of Fwdbonds said growth is powering ahead despite geopolitical risks and the damage higher energy costs inflict on many sectors.

Markets took it with restraint. Stock futures slipped and Treasury yields fell. The latest official data put the 10-year yield at 5.05% and the 2-year at 4.67%, so the bond market continues to charge a premium for patience. The Fed has said it will keep reading. There is now a great deal to read.
