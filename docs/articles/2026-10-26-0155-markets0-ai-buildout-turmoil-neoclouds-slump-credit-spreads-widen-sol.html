AI Buildout Discovers Credit Markets Also Have Opinions About Spending

Neocloud stocks sank, default insurance on CoreWeave and Meta hit records, and Solidigm pulled its IPO, while Nvidia's CEO said demand is off the charts.

The wire said: Turmoil hits AI buildout as neoclouds face pressure, credit risks mount and Solidigm pulls IPO · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 26, 2026 01:55 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

For two years, the largest tech companies have spent record sums on data centers and chips to serve AI demand, with growing doubts about whether the returns will justify it. This week, stress showed up in the debt markets and in canceled stock offerings.

Why it matters

The AI buildout now depends on borrowed money and guarantees, so trouble at smaller players can spread to the large companies that dominate stock indexes and many retirement accounts. If funding tightens, the spending that has driven markets for two years could slow.

CNBC reported that the AI infrastructure trade had a rough week. The Nasdaq fell again on Friday and ended the week down 0.7%, with Alphabet shares sinking 6% ahead of its earnings report. Microsoft and Amazon each slipped less than 1%. This is not a crash, but it is a noticeable cough from the sector holding up the economy's optimism.

The sharpest pain was among neoclouds, which are companies that rent out AI computing power from rented or borrowed chips and data centers. CoreWeave, Nebius and IREN each lost well over 30% in October, according to CNBC. CoreWeave's credit default swaps, which are insurance contracts against a company failing to repay its debt, widened past 11%. Translation: the market is charging a lot for a policy on the assumption that the house may have a problem.

CoreWeave's bonds due in 2031 and 2032 now yield over 15%. The company carries roughly $51.6 billion in total debt, about $35 billion of it in notes, bonds and loans. A 15% yield means lenders want a fifteen-percent annual reward for believing it will all work out, which is a polite way of expressing doubt.

The mood spread abroad. Elice Group, a South Korean cloud provider, fell 30% in its market debut, and Australia's NextDC dropped 39.5% after cutting its annual earnings forecast. SK Hynix, one of the biggest winners of the boom, scrapped an IPO for its Solidigm storage unit, saying in a release that the review was discontinued given market conditions and share price trends. Translation: the offering was withdrawn because buyers did not seem to be there.

Even the giants showed strain. Meta fell 7% on Thursday after reporting negative free cash flow of over $2 billion in the third quarter and raising the floor of its capital spending forecast to $135 billion from $130 billion. It also disclosed about $825 billion in planned lease and contract spending, over $450 billion of it in non-cancellable take-or-pay agreements. Translation: Meta has agreed to pay whether or not it ends up needing the space.

Meta's five-year credit default swaps hit a record above 124 basis points, while Nvidia's reached 126 and Oracle's 343. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so these are small numbers that mean the cost of insuring against trouble has never been higher. CNBC noted Oracle has lost over a third of its value this year and faces possible downgrade risk.

Nvidia sits at the center of this. A second CNBC report described how it has become the financier of its own customers, using its investment-grade credit rating to back neocloud agreements and OpenAI projects. As of its July filing, it had $36 billion in off-balance-sheet backstops for neoclouds and a guarantee of up to $105 billion tied to a proposed OpenAI data center in Ohio. Translation: Nvidia sells the shovels, then co-signs the mortgage on the mine.

Nvidia says it pays only if participants default, which is a reassuring sentence until the word default appears in the same article as 15% yields. David Trainer of New Constructs told CNBC that if compute demand does not meet lofty expectations, there will be a problem. Nvidia's stock is down 7.6% in October, on pace for its worst month since last November, and it declined to comment.

Jensen Huang remains unbothered. He told CNBC that demand for neoclouds is off the charts and that the real shortage is capital, and he said CoreWeave does not need Nvidia's backstop. Gartner, meanwhile, predicts that 80% of neoclouds will shut down or be absorbed by 2030. Both can be true, since a business can have enormous demand and still be unable to pay its lenders.

What to watch: Alphabet, Microsoft and Amazon report next week, and the question will be whether they keep spending or start explaining why. Until then, the AI trade has a new metric to track alongside revenue, which is the price of insuring against it going wrong.
