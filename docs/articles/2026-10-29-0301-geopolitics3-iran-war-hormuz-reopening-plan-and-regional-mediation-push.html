Iran Offers Seven-Day Plan to Reopen Hormuz; Washington Denies Talks, Cites Medical Supplies

Three regional powers visit Tehran, Iran says it is in close contact with mediators, and President Trump says any contact with Iran was about medical supplies.

The wire said: Iran war live: Tehran presses 7-day Hormuz plan; Trump denies peace talks · Al Jazeera

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 29, 2026 03:01 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The US and Iran remain in a state Iranian leaders call 'neither war nor peace,' with the Strait of Hormuz disrupted and a US naval blockade in place. Pakistan has spent months mediating, and fears are growing of new US and Israeli strikes after the November 3 midterm election.

Why it matters

The Strait of Hormuz is a critical shipping route, so any reopening or renewed fighting affects energy markets and prices far beyond the region. Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey are also testing whether a new defense pact can restrain a wider war.

Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said on October 29 that Tehran remains in 'close and ongoing contact' with mediators on ways to carry out its seven-day plan to reopen the Strait of Hormuz, according to Al Jazeera. President Donald Trump, for his part, denied media reports that he had told Vice President JD Vance to begin peace talks with Iran. He said the contact concerned Tehran's need for 'help with medical supplies.' Translation: there are no talks, only a conversation that closely resembles one.

The diplomacy has been busy. According to DW, Pakistan's army chief Asim Munir, Saudi Defense Minister Khalid bin Salman and Turkish intelligence chief Ibrahim Kalin all traveled to Tehran on Monday to meet President Masoud Pezeshkian and other senior officials. They represent the Mecca defense pact, an alliance of Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Turkey formally set up this summer. Under its terms, an attack on one member counts as an attack on all.

Saudi Arabia said the trip aimed to open negotiating channels and 'prevent the return of military escalation.' Details of the agenda were scarce, but a senior Pakistani official told Reuters the talks covered a regional non-aggression network, US-Iran talks, Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia, Hormuz and the US naval blockade. Another official said Munir offered a proposal giving something to both Washington and Tehran, with the three Mecca states and Qatar as mediators, and that Trump had earlier let Munir try diplomacy. Translation: everyone is mediating, and the one person who must agree says he is not negotiating.

Iran described the atmosphere as 'very positive,' though no breakthroughs were announced. Mohsen Rezaei, head of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, told state television that 'a new atmosphere is emerging in the region.' State media said Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei then called for protecting Iran's interests and keeping Tehran's conditions for ending both the war and the current limbo. Pezeshkian also went to Moscow, where he met Vladimir Putin and Qatar's emir, which Tehran framed as proof that the US cannot isolate it.

The visiting trio is open to two readings. Analyst Babak Dorbeiki told DW the simultaneous presence of three senior figures signaled the countries act as a bloc, but he also argued the pact is built for deterrence, not offensive war, and that having an alliance does not mean wanting to fight Iran. Mediation could continue, he said, though more escalation could change that. An Iranian presidential adviser, Ali Asghar Shafieian, rejected the idea that the delegation brought an ultimatum, adding that Iran, like the US and Israel, can prepare for fighting while negotiating.

Riyadh has the most pressing dilemma. Houthi attacks, which are Iran-allied according to DW, have damaged Saudi infrastructure and the economy, yet a wider war could threaten the investment projects at the heart of its economic strategy. Pakistan would rather not be distracted from its concerns with India, and Turkey sent its intelligence chief rather than a foreign or defense minister, which Dorbeiki read as caution. Dorbeiki also argued that Iran is extending talks while avoiding real commitments, and has not offered the guarantees on Houthi attacks that Riyadh wants.

The one apparent outcome, per DW, is that everyone agreed to keep talking. With the election on November 3 and a seven-day plan on the table, the question is whether talking is a step toward reopening Hormuz or just the most economical way to wait.
