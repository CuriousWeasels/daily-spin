Moderna Cancer Vaccine Works; Moderna Stock Falls 25% Anyway, Having Already Celebrated

Leaked partial data sent shares down as much as 25% even though full results showed a 42% cut in recurrence or death risk in melanoma patients.

The wire said: Moderna shares plunge after cancer vaccine data leaks — despite positive results · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 23, 2026 02:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Moderna and Merck are developing a personalized cancer vaccine, intismeran autogene, designed to be paired with Merck's immunotherapy Keytruda. Partial phase 3 data leaked on Thursday before the companies released the full results.

Why it matters

The episode shows how a stock priced for perfection can fall on good news, while patients and doctors judge the same data by a different standard. Investors in richly valued biotech names should note how little room expectations leave for anything short of a miracle.

Moderna shares fell as much as 25% on Thursday after partial data from its melanoma vaccine trial leaked, CNBC reported. The full data, released later that day, showed the shot paired with Keytruda cut the risk of recurrence or death by 42% compared with Keytruda alone. The market, having reviewed a result that doctors called a huge development, sold the stock.

Some numbers for those who prefer them to adjectives. About 82% of patients on the combination were alive without their melanoma returning at two years, versus 70% on Keytruda alone, according to the companies. The risk of the cancer spreading to distant parts of the body or death fell 38%. After the full six-month vaccine regimen, a separate analysis showed a 58% reduction in risk of recurrence or death.

Dr. Hussein Tawbi, a trial investigator at MD Anderson, called the results "a huge development for our patients," and noted the added benefit came with much milder side effects, such as injection-site pain. Translation: the treatment worked, it was tolerable, and the people most qualified to judge it were pleased. The people qualified to price it were not.

The explanation, offered by several analysts, is expectations. BMO Capital Markets analyst Evan Seigerman wrote that the reaction likely reflected "inflated expectations into the full data release," not a true indictment of the therapy. UBS likewise called it a strong data set and blamed the drop on massive expectations. Translation: a sell-the-news reaction, in which investors buy a stock for months in anticipation of good news and sell it the moment the news arrives, because nobody is left to buy.

The expectations had a visible address. Moderna shares had risen more than 530% this year as of Thursday afternoon, so even after a drop of up to 25%, the stock was presumably still living well. Merck, a much larger company, fell about 1% and is up nearly 41% this year. Size, it turns out, is a sedative.

There is also genuine caution in the science, and the executives said so. The companies say it is too early to know whether the treatment helps patients live longer overall. Merck research chief Dr. Dean Li said one result was like knocking down one tree, which "does not give you a cabin or a village." The vaccine is being tested in lung, bladder and kidney cancers, and UBS said these data increase confidence in the kidney and bladder studies.

Manufacturing is the other practical hurdle. Because each shot is built around the mutations in an individual patient's tumor, production can take six weeks. Merck CEO Robert Davis told CNBC the company believes it can shorten that timeline. Patients with melanoma are not in a position to be impressed by a leak; they would like the six weeks to be fewer.

The detailed results are due at the European Society for Medical Oncology conference this weekend. Watch whether the full presentation changes any minds on Monday, or whether the market simply finds a new figure to be disappointed by.
