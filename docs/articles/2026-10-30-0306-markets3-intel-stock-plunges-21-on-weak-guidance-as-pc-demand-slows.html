Intel Beats Estimates, Then Falls 21% After Telling Investors What Comes Next

The chipmaker posted a strong third quarter, but a weak forecast tied to soaring memory prices and a fatal factory explosion sent shares down more than 20% after hours.

The wire said: Intel stock plunges 21% as chipmaker blames PC slowdown for weak guidance · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 30, 2026 03:06 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Intel has been one of the year's big stock winners, up about 150% before Thursday's report, as investors bet on AI demand for its server chips and its revival as a U.S. manufacturer. The company reported third-quarter results on Thursday alongside guidance for the current quarter.

Why it matters

Memory shortages created by AI data centers are now raising costs for laptops and slowing PC sales, which means the AI buildout is showing up in ordinary consumer electronics. Investors holding Intel after a 150% run learned how quickly a forecast can undo a good quarter.

Intel shares fell more than 20% in extended trading on Thursday after the company gave a fourth-quarter forecast well below what analysts expected, CNBC reported. This came after a quarter that beat expectations. The market, as usual, paid for the future rather than the past, and the future was marked down.

The quarter itself was solid. Revenue rose 21% to $16.51 billion, in line with the $16.5 billion analysts expected, and adjusted earnings were 52 cents per share against 40 cents expected, according to LSEG figures cited by CNBC. Data center and AI revenue jumped 78% to $7.3 billion. Translation: the part of Intel that sells to AI builders is doing wonderfully, and the part that sells to people who own laptops is not.

The guidance is where it went wrong. Intel expects fourth-quarter revenue of $15 billion to $16 billion and adjusted earnings of 25 cents per share. Analysts had pencilled in $17.1 billion and 43 cents. Translation: guidance is the company's own prediction of its next three months, and investors treat it as the only part of the earnings report that matters.

The cause is memory. Revenue in Intel's client computing group, which covers PCs, fell 5% to $8.1 billion and missed estimates. Counterpoint Research said global PC shipments fell 4% in the second quarter on soaring memory and component costs. CNBC's Investing Club commentary explained the mechanism: data center builders can raise money by issuing debt or selling equity and will pay whatever memory costs, while consumers and corporate IT departments cannot. Both groups draw on the same limited factory capacity, and the people with the bond market behind them are winning.

CEO Lip-Bu Tan said customers began signaling a PC slowdown in recent weeks, based on memory prices and availability. He also described one of the most significant supply shortages in history. Finance chief David Zinsner said the PC slowdown should continue at least through the first half of next year. Nobody on the call suggested the shortage was caused by anything other than the industry's own enthusiasm.

There was also a human cost that the earnings call had to account for. An explosion at an Intel fab in Israel earlier this month killed two workers and seriously injured a third. Tan called it an unfortunate and tragic accident. It has caused an extended shutdown at the plant, affecting shipments of 10-nanometer chips. Zinsner put the cost at about $600 million in lost revenue over two quarters, while the Investing Club piece cited roughly $300 million in the fourth quarter and a similar amount in the first.

The long-term case remains intact, at least in management's telling. Intel estimates agentic AI, where software agents run tasks in the cloud and lean on CPUs, could expand the server CPU market from $40 billion to as much as $200 billion in five years. Its 18A chip is in high-volume production in Arizona, but it still has no major outside customers for it. Tan said a couple of customers could be announced over the next two quarters. The company's foundry external revenue was $411 million, which CNBC's Investing Club noted was boosted by a non-recurring program.

The stock had already fallen about 23% in October on worries about the size of the AI buildout, so Thursday's drop landed on a lot of nervous holders. Nvidia reports in mid-November and AMD reports next week, which will test whether Intel's problem is its own or the whole sector's. For now, the AI boom has successfully made chips more valuable and laptops more expensive, and Intel sells both.
