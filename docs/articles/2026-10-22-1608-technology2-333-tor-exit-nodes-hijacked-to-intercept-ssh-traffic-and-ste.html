Hackers Commandeer 333 Tor Exit Nodes, Discover Reused Password Remains Internet's Most Reliable Exploit

Attackers redirected SSH traffic and tried to coax email into plaintext across hundreds of relays, according to a Tor volunteer's report, beginning with a password someone used twice.

The wire said: 333 Tor exit nodes hijacked to intercept SSH traffic and retrieve plaintext email · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 22, 2026 16:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Tor routes traffic through volunteer-run relays, and exit nodes are the last hop before the open internet. Ars Technica reported that unknown attackers took over 333 of them in a campaign to intercept SSH logins and unencrypted email.

Why it matters

Tor is meant to protect people who cannot afford to be watched, and an exit node sees whatever traffic is not otherwise encrypted. The episode shows that a privacy network is only as strong as the weakest password on its management servers, and that users who click through security warnings are doing the attacker's work for him.

Unknown attackers hijacked 333 Tor exit nodes and reconfigured them to redirect SSH connections to servers they controlled and to favor unencrypted email, according to a report published Tuesday by a Tor volunteer who goes by Kaba and relayed by Ars Technica. Translation: exit nodes are the last stop before Tor traffic reaches the ordinary internet, which makes them an excellent place to stand if you wish to listen to people who believed they were anonymous.

The origin story is modest. According to Tor volunteers, the attacker got into a Tor management server in late August using a valid password that had been reused elsewhere. Translation: the most sophisticated privacy network in common use was briefly defended by the same security policy as a gym locker room.

From there the attacker installed Ebury, a Linux backdoor first discovered 12 years ago, which suggests the toolkit did not need to be new, merely patient. The attacker then connected to a second Tor server, called Switzerland2, over SSH using an RSA key that investigators could not find among its authorized keys, for reasons nobody has explained. Ars Technica notes that investigators have also not established how the attackers gained control of all the underlying servers behind the 333 relays.

The reconfiguration came in two parts. First, SSH traffic passing through the affected exit nodes was redirected to one of 12 attacker-controlled interception servers. Users who saw the warning that the SSH host key had changed, and proceeded anyway, would then type their passwords into a machine belonging to someone else. Translation: a host key is the server's identity card, and the warning means the card does not match the face.

Second, the exit nodes blocked ports 25, 465, 587, 993 and 995, which handle encrypted email, while leaving the unencrypted ones open. The idea, per the report, was that email services might downgrade to plaintext when the secure route failed, at which point the attackers could read the messages. It is unclear whether any such downgrade succeeded. This is a plan that depends on software being too polite to refuse.

The affected window for the email interception ran from October 2 to October 5. Tor volunteers had already outlined the initial compromise and the email attempt, but Tuesday's report added the count of compromised nodes and the SSH redirection scheme. Kaba wrote that ordinary web traffic passed through as usual, so a user routed through an infected node would notice nothing until trying SSH on port 22. Kaba said that is exactly what happened on Friday morning, and a warning followed.

The practical lesson is unglamorous. Do not dismiss changed-host-key warnings, do not let email fall back to plaintext, and do not reuse passwords, including on the servers that run the anonymity network. The attackers, for their part, remain unknown, and the mysteries in the timeline suggest the investigation has some distance left to travel.
