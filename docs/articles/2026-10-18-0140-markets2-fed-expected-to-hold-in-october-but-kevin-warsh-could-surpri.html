Markets Bet Fed Will Hold in October, Unaware Chairman Has Declined to Say

Futures price just a 15% chance of a hike on Oct. 28, but Kevin Warsh has rejected the habit of telling investors his plans in advance, CNBC reported.

The wire said: Analysis: Everyone expects the Fed to hold rates in October. Kevin Warsh could surprise us all · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 18, 2026 01:40 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The Federal Reserve raised interest rates unanimously in September under new chairman Kevin Warsh, who has argued inflation is a bigger worry than the job market. The committee next meets on Oct. 28.

Why it matters

If the Fed hikes when markets expect a pause, borrowing costs and asset prices could move sharply and quickly. Mortgage, loan and savings rates all follow the Fed, so a surprise reaches households as well as traders.

Traders are betting heavily that the Federal Reserve will leave interest rates alone when it meets later this month. According to CNBC analysis, futures markets gave a 15% probability to an October hike as of Friday morning. That leaves an 85% chance of nothing happening, which is the kind of confidence markets usually reserve for things the Fed has explicitly promised.

The Fed has not promised it. Chairman Kevin Warsh said at an International Monetary Fund event in Bangkok that he was not signaling a decision either way. Translation: the person who decides has declined to say what he will decide, and the market has filled in the blank with a guess it feels good about.

The guess rests on habit. Fed Vice Chairman Philip Jefferson and New York Fed President John Williams recently said they saw little urgency to raise rates, and markets quickly repriced toward a hold. Warsh, Jefferson and Williams form an informal leadership group known as the troika. Historically, when the troika's other two members speak, they speak for the chair. CNBC notes that outsiders cannot be sure that convention survived the change in leadership.

Warsh has also been tidy about distancing himself from the committee's paperwork. The Fed's September Summary of Economic Projections showed the median official expecting one more hike in 2026, which left room to pause in October and move in December. Asked about it at his September press conference, Warsh said, "Those aren't my forecasts." Translation: the document investors use to guess the Fed's path is, by the chairman's own account, not necessarily his path.

Then there is the inflation data. Consumer prices rose 3.7% in the year through September, and producer prices rose 6.3%. CNBC says that implies core personal consumption expenditures, the Fed's preferred gauge, will come in around 3.1%. The Fed's goal is 2%, which Warsh called in Jackson Hole "a firm, fixed target." A firm, fixed target is hard to describe as met when you are at 3.1%.

Warsh also said in Bangkok that the world has "broadly conformed to our expectations" since September, which could mean the hot readings do not worry him. It could also mean something else. CNBC's analysis is that we can infer his thinking, but will not really know until the press conference after the meeting.

Current conditions do not make the choice easier. The federal funds rate sits at 3.88%, the 10-year Treasury yields 5.1%, and Brent crude is at 126.55. Unemployment is 4.2%. Markets, meanwhile, are calm: the VIX, Wall Street's fear gauge, stood at 16.46. Translation: investors are relaxed, which is the condition in which surprises do the most damage.

CNBC points out that a hike against strong market expectations is a recipe for volatility, and could dent the credibility of Jefferson and Williams if they were seen to have guided investors wrongly. Warsh could instead drift toward the committee's middle once he gauges its views. Watch Oct. 28, when the chairman will at last communicate something, in the traditional manner, after the decision.
