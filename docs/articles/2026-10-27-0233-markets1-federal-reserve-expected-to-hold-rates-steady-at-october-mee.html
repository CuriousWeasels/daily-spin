Fed Expected to Do Nothing Days Before Election, Which It Insists Is Unrelated

With inflation at 3.7% and oil surging, the central bank is widely expected to hold rates at 3.75% to 4% this week, just ahead of the midterms.

The wire said: Federal Reserve is likely to hold interest rates steady in October. Here's what that means for consumers · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 27, 2026 02:33 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The Fed raised rates by a quarter point last month, its first hike since 2023, as inflation climbed and oil prices rose amid the U.S. war with Iran. Its next meeting falls days before the midterm elections.

Why it matters

A pause keeps credit card and mortgage costs near current high levels rather than pushing them higher, but it does not make borrowing cheaper. Savers keep decent yields, and anyone hoping for relief should plan on waiting.

The Federal Reserve is expected to leave interest rates unchanged at its meeting this week, CNBC reported, even though energy costs are making the inflation picture worse. Consumer prices rose again in September, taking annual inflation to 3.7%. Fed funds futures, which are bets on where rates are headed, put the chance of another quarter-point hike at under 10%.

The Fed raised rates by 25 basis points last month to a range of 3.75% to 4%. Translation: a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so the Fed moved the price of money by a quarter of one percent and the country has talked about little else since. It was the first hike since 2023.

The timing is awkward. The meeting lands just days before the midterm elections, and the Fed is officially an independent body that does not consider such things. Heather Long, chief economist at Navy Federal Credit Union, was less formal. "Given that it's right before an election," she said, "they won't hike in October."

History suggests the Fed is not entirely shy about this. According to CME Group economist Mark Shore, the Fed has raised rates near a midterm four times: 1978, 1982, 2018 and 2022. All four were tied to rising inflation. Translation: the Fed avoids moving near elections unless prices are doing something it cannot ignore, which is a fair description of the current situation.

Not everyone sees a pause as free. Bank of America economist Aditya Bhave wrote on Oct. 23 that if the Fed holds, markets could question its inflation-fighting credibility. Chairman Kevin Warsh has said he is committed to the 2% target, a number inflation is currently exceeding by a little under two percentage points. Futures traders, for their part, see nearly a 75% chance of at least one hike by year-end, so the pause may be less a decision than an intermission.

For households, the practical effects are mostly about what does not get cheaper. Most credit cards carry variable rates that follow the Fed's benchmark, so they should stay near the current average of 23.96% on new offers, according to LendingTree. Translation: a balance on a card costs roughly a quarter of its value per year, which is a rate once associated with a different category of lender.

Mortgages are a separate story. They track Treasury yields more than the Fed, and the 10-year yield sits near 5%, keeping the average 30-year fixed rate close to 7.4%, per Mortgage News Daily. Moody's chief economist Mark Zandi said higher long-term rates are doing more damage to wallets than short-term ones, weighing on refinancing, homebuying and car sales. Edmunds says car buyers are coping by taking larger loans over longer terms, which is a way of paying more to borrow for longer.

Savers get the one upside. Because rates remain high, high-yield savings accounts and other deposit products still pay relatively attractive yields. Brent crude, meanwhile, hit a five-month high on Friday before slipping, so the inflation story has not finished. Watch whether the Fed's statement hints at December, because voters will have made their choice by then and the Fed will be free to make its own.
