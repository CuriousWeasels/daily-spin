Poland Sends Riot Police to Protect Rule of Law From Its Own Judges

Prime Minister Tusk's government moves to strip the Constitutional Court's two top judges of immunity, and President Nawrocki turns his plane around to join the standoff.

The wire said: Police deployed during stand-off over control of top Poland court · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 31, 2026 02:52 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Poland's Constitutional Court has been contested since the Law and Justice (PiS) party took power in 2015 and installed judges to gain a majority there. Donald Tusk's government, in office since the PiS era ended, says it is restoring the rule of law, but it has been blocked by presidential vetoes.

Why it matters

A court that cannot agree on who runs it cannot reliably settle what the law is, and that uncertainty reaches every Polish business and citizen. The dispute also tests how far a government may go to fix a broken institution before the fix becomes part of the problem.

Riot police were deployed at Poland's Constitutional Court in Warsaw, the BBC reported, amid an escalating standoff between Prime Minister Donald Tusk's government and the court's senior judges. Justice Minister Waldemar Żurek said he had asked to lift the immunity from prosecution of court President Bogdan Święczkowski and Vice-President Bartłomiej Sochański so they can face criminal charges. The police were there to keep demonstrators out of the building, which is a notable way to defend an institution whose purpose is to check the state.

Translation: lifting immunity means removing the legal shield that normally stops prosecutors from charging a senior official. Żurek's stated reason is that the two judges have refused to let certain colleagues hear cases. Those colleagues are five judges elected by parliament since March, whom President Karol Nawrocki has declined to swear in, citing alleged flaws in how they were selected.

Nawrocki, who is backed by PiS, cancelled a trip mid-air to the Summer Youth Olympic Games in Dakar, Al Jazeera and the BBC reported, and has since entered the court, according to his chief of staff. PiS supporters gathered outside in protest. The court, for its part, posted online that the two judges remain its legally appointed senior justices, and that everyone taking part in these actions is committing "the crime of a coup d'etat." Tusk posted that restoring the rule of law is a duty and "a difficult, sometimes painful process."

The background is a genuine mess, and both sides can cite it. After PiS took office in 2015 it moved quickly to appoint judges and win a majority on the court, which can rule on whether legislation is legal. The European Court of Human Rights and the Court of Justice of the European Union later found that three of those judges were improperly elected because they occupied seats already validly filled by the previous parliament. The European courts concluded the tribunal was no longer an independent and impartial one, and Tusk's government used that as grounds to stop publishing its rulings.

Tusk has not been able to undo the changes by ordinary legislation, partly because Nawrocki and his predecessor Andrzej Duda used presidential vetoes to block him. Anna Korwin-Piotrowska, one of the judges Nawrocki has declined to swear in, has now been placed in temporary charge of the court. Earlier this month the Venice Commission, the Council of Europe's advisory body on constitutional matters, said Nawrocki should swear in the judges so they can sit on the bench.

Even people who dislike the court's leadership are uneasy. Lawyer Marcin Matczak, a critic of the PiS-era judicial changes who calls Święczkowski the court's worst president, said the suspension has no legal basis "for a million reasons." He warned that images of armed police and a justice minister removing a court president by decree will cause a backlash against the rule of law "for years to come." Some of Tusk's own supporters, meanwhile, have accused him of not acting decisively enough, and with parliamentary elections due next year, some see the move as a bid for popularity. The BBC also noted criticism of Tusk's September pick of Maciej Berek, a lawyer who served in his cabinet, as a court candidate.

The structure of the dispute is tidy in its own way. One side says the court was captured, so it must be removed by force of law. The other says the removal is the capture. Both say they are defending the constitution, and both are standing in front of the same building, one of them with riot shields.

What to watch next is whether parliament and prosecutors actually lift the judges' immunity, and who ends up in the courtroom first.
