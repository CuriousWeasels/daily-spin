Zelenskyy Asks Europe for $80 Billion, Receives Applause and a Request for a Proposal

At an EU summit in Brussels, Ukraine's president sought money and military backing as Washington slowed satellite service and bought Russian diesel.

The wire said: Ukrainian President Zelenskyy speaks at EU summit amid spat with Donald Trump · DW

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 17, 2026 01:44 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Ukraine's relationship with the Trump administration has deteriorated, with disputes over Russian diesel purchases, strikes on Russian refineries and disruptions to Starlink and Starshield. Zelenskyy went to Brussels seeking European support for what analysts expect to be a hard winter.

Why it matters

Ukraine's battlefield communications depend partly on a US-linked system that can reportedly be restricted without warning, while its funding depends on a European Union that has not yet worked out how to pay. Higher fuel prices are also testing European public support for aid.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy was applauded by EU leaders at a summit in Brussels this week, according to DW. He asked for nearly $80 billion (about 70 billion euros) more next year and left without a figure, a mechanism or a promise. The applause, at least, was delivered in full.

The money is the simplest part of the story. The EU lent Kyiv roughly $100 billion last year, which makes it Ukraine's biggest international backer. Analyst Olena Prokopenko told DW the war is getting more expensive while Russian strikes cut into Ukrainian tax and customs revenue. Translation: costs go up, income goes down, and the gap is the thing Zelenskyy flew to Brussels to discuss.

European Council President Antonio Costa said Ukraine "remains a priority" and asked the European Commission to draft a proposal for more funds in 2027. Translation: the summit agreed that a plan should exist. Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said the EU will "continue to lead" and urged other partners to step up, which is how leaders say that someone else should also pay.

The second problem is Starlink. Ukraine uses the commercial service and its military version, Starshield, for battlefield communications, interceptor drones and mid-range strikes. Zelenskyy said he was surprised to learn of disruptions, and that they hobbled Ukraine's defences. Elon Musk and the White House both say they are not responsible, while the BBC reported that Musk and media accounts suggest the order may have come from the White House. A slowdown with no one responsible is an impressive feat of administration.

The EU's foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas was blunt about the alternatives: Europe cannot replace the capability in the short term. Prokopenko said that is why Ukraine's most credible medium-term option is to keep relying on the US, with the EU lobbying Washington on its behalf. Zelenskyy said speeds had improved but were not fully restored, and that Ukraine is looking at partial fixes with countries including France.

The diplomatic backdrop is poor. Trump told Newsmax he finds Zelenskyy harder to deal with than Putin, and struck a deal on October 12 to buy Russian diesel, which DW describes as a step back from sanctions. Translation: sanctions are penalties meant to make Russia's economy hurt, and buying its fuel is a way of making them hurt less. Trump blames Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries for higher fuel prices, while Ukraine blames the US and Israeli war against Iran, the BBC reported.

Italy shows how the politics cut inside Europe. Defence Minister Guido Crosetto told the BBC that limiting Ukraine's access to satellite systems and intelligence would be a "complete mistake", and Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni called the diesel deal a "very risky choice". Yet Crosetto said parliamentary approval for further deliveries would get "more difficult" as elections approach, and a September poll put support for supplying arms at 18%. Fuel prices have prompted truckers' blockades in the south and student protests in Rome.

On the front, the BBC reported that Ukraine says Operation Vivaldi retook 277 square kilometres in Donbas and freed 16 settlements over the year. Those figures are Ukrainian claims, and no Russian official has acknowledged the gains. Commanders say the offensive relied on drones, ground robots and Starshield connectivity, which makes the reported restriction harder to treat as a minor technical matter.

Zelenskyy closed in Brussels with the words "We will keep living and fighting with dignity." Next up is a Commission proposal, an American decision on satellite speeds, and a winter that will not wait for either.
