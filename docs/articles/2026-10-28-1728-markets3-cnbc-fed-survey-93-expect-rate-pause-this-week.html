Fed Expected to Do Nothing This Week, Then Something Later, Say People Paid to Guess

A CNBC survey finds 93% of 30 forecasters expect a pause now and 76% expect a hike in December, with 10-year yields sitting around 5%.

The wire said: Fed rate hikes are coming, but not this week: CNBC survey · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 28, 2026 17:28 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The Federal Reserve meets this week, with inflation at 3.7% and oil prices elevated by the war in Iran. CNBC surveys fund managers, strategists and economists before every Fed meeting, and the latest edition polled 30 of them.

Why it matters

Higher rates mean costlier mortgages, car loans and credit cards, while a Fed that waits too long risks letting inflation settle in. The forecasters mostly expect the pain to be postponed, not cancelled.

The Federal Reserve is expected to leave interest rates unchanged at its meeting this week, according to CNBC's latest Fed Survey. Of the 30 fund managers, strategists and economists polled, 93% expect a pause. That is about as close to consensus as economics gets, a field where people usually disagree over the definition of the word 'recession.'

The consensus ends there. Seventy-six percent expect a rate hike at the December meeting, and more than half expect the Fed to raise rates next year by an average of about two hikes. Translation: the Fed will not raise rates today, but respondents think it will be raising them soon, which is the central banking equivalent of saying you will start the diet on Monday.

Some respondents think Monday has already come and gone. Richard Bernstein of Janus Henderson called the Fed's hesitancy 'unfathomable,' pointing to nominal GDP running 6-8% and uniformly warm-to-hot inflation indicators. Translation: nominal GDP is growth before subtracting inflation, so a big number there can mean the economy is expanding or merely that everything costs more. John Ryding of Brean Capital wrote that demand and inflation data point to a strong need to hike. Thirty percent said the Fed should hike at this meeting, and survey respondents expect at least two Fed voters to dissent from holding steady.

The other camp is led by Mark Zandi of Moody's Analytics, who cited the intensifying Iran war, falling real wages, rising interest rates and a shaky AI-fueled stock market. 'Inflation is high, but there's little sign it is becoming entrenched,' he wrote. Translation: entrenched inflation is when businesses and workers start assuming prices will keep rising and act accordingly, which makes it self-fulfilling and very annoying to remove.

The survey's broader numbers describe an economy that is being told to brace while continuing to behave. Respondents see GDP growth of 2.4% this year, above the 2.2% they see as potential for 2027, and put recession odds at 26%, down from 29%. Unemployment is expected to stay at 4.2% before rising to 4.4% in 2027. The Consumer Price Index is forecast at 3.6%, falling to 2.6% next year. Forty percent say the Fed must slow growth below potential to fix inflation, which is a polite way of saying the cure involves making the economy somewhat worse on purpose.

Oil complicates the story. Respondents forecast the U.S. crude benchmark at $88 a barrel in six months, below the current $101 cited by CNBC. Seventy percent say inflation is broader than energy prices. Official data show Brent crude at 123.1 as of Oct. 27, so anyone relying on a falling-oil forecast is making a confident bet against the thing currently happening.

Then there are bond yields. Respondents split almost evenly on the cause: inflation, growth, heavy debt issuance and expectations of higher Fed rates. Asked about supply, 50% blamed government debt, 13% blamed borrowing to fund the AI buildout, and a third said both contributed equally. The 10-year Treasury yield stood at 5.0% in the latest official data, and respondents see it staying above 5% this year. Subodh Kumar called yields above 5% 'a new band, not an anomaly.' Translation: the government's borrowing costs have moved up a floor, and nobody is bringing the old furniture back.

Politics gets a turn as well. Sixty-three percent see some market risk from legal challenges that could drag out the question of who controls Congress after next week's election. More than half now expect Democratic control of both houses, up from 29% last time, and 40% expect Democrats to take the House but not the Senate. Yet respondents see little change in taxes and little difference in growth forecasts by party, which suggests the markets fear the uncertainty far more than the outcome.

The biggest risks named were the continuation of the Iran war and a burst AI bubble. Rob Morgan of MOSAIC suggested the Fed chair tell the president that the quickest way to lower inflation is ending tariffs and the war. It is a tidy policy proposal that sits outside the Fed's authority, which may be why it was offered by a strategist and not a voter. For now, the Fed holds, the forecasters wait for December, and the 10-year yield stays where it is.
