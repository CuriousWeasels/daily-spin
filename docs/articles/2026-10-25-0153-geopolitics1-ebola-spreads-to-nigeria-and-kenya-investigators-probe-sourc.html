Ebola Investigators Trace Two Patients to One Airport, and One Country's Denial

Nigeria's first Ebola case in over a decade has no confirmed source, and Uganda and Switzerland disagree on where the patient went.

The wire said: Mystery over source of Ebola infection in Nigeria fuels concern about spread of virus · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 25, 2026 01:53 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

An outbreak of the rare Bundibugyo strain of Ebola, announced on 15 May in Democratic Republic of Congo, has spread to Uganda, Kenya and now Nigeria. It has infected more than 9,500 people and killed nearly half of them, and it has no approved vaccine or treatment.

Why it matters

If health officials cannot establish where infections happen, they cannot decide whom to trace, test or isolate. An outbreak that already covers more countries than any before it is harder to contain when governments cannot agree on a patient's itinerary.

Health officials across Africa are trying to work out how Nigeria's first Ebola patient in more than a decade became infected, according to The Guardian. The patient, a woman based in Abuja, is the fourth country's case in an outbreak the Guardian describes as the most geographically widespread in history. The one thing everyone agrees on is that she has Ebola. Where she got it is the subject of a dispute.

Uganda's Health Ministry said preliminary findings showed she travelled to eastern DR Congo for business, then passed through Uganda without symptoms. Translation: if she caught it in Congo, it is not Uganda's problem. Switzerland's foreign ministry then told Reuters that she and her husband, a Swiss diplomat in Abuja, did not travel to Congo at all, and that she was believed to have been infected while travelling in Uganda. DW noted that this Swiss assessment has not been independently confirmed.

The WHO and Africa CDC said they had no confirmed information that she went to Congo. WHO's Catherine Smallwood said staff in Congo had nothing showing the patient had been there. None of this proves she was infected in Uganda. It does mean the first account of her movements has not held up well.

Uganda's government told Reuters it has no Ebola cases and would make them public if it did. The WHO declared the country Ebola-free in August, after cases in May and June. Uganda, in other words, is holding to a position that is technically consistent with every fact currently available, which is how most official positions survive.

Then there is the airport. Africa CDC's Wessam Mankoula said the Nigerian patient and Kenya's first confirmed patient both passed through Entebbe airport on 3 October. The Kenyan patient had travelled from Congo through Uganda and died in Nairobi on 5 October. Investigators are checking whether their movements overlapped.

Experts are cautious. Smallwood said a link was possible but that officials must compare the timing with when the Nigerian woman's symptoms began, which DW reports was 7 October. Dutch virologist Marion Koopmans, a member of the WHO's Ebola emergency committee, called the theory "very speculative" without evidence of shared space. Being in the same large airport, she said, is not a supported epidemiological link. Translation: two people in one terminal is a coincidence until someone shows they stood near each other.

The timing matters because, according to the WHO as cited by DW, people with Ebola are not considered infectious until symptoms appear, and symptoms take two to 21 days to develop. A traveller can therefore pass every checkpoint looking entirely healthy. DW notes that even well-run border surveillance cannot catch every infected traveller, which makes the checkpoints more a gesture of diligence than a guarantee.

Kenya has its own gap. Patrick Amoth, the country's director-general for health, told the Daily Nation that officials know a Ugandan contact of the dead patient by name, passport number and phone, but cannot reach him. He was reportedly using a South Sudanese number. DW stresses that an untraced contact is not necessarily infected; he is simply unlocated, which in contact tracing is a problem of its own.

Nigerian Health Minister Muhammad Ali Pate said the case remains contained, with 44 contacts identified, including health workers and caregivers, and tracing continuing. The woman is stable and isolated. In Abuja, residents told DW of anxiety, with one saying people are now worried about surviving more than about sickness.

What to watch next is simple and unglamorous: the date of the woman's first symptoms against the Entebbe timeline, and whether anyone can find the missing contact. Until then, three countries have three itineraries and one virus has none.
