West Bank Olive Harvest Becomes Race Against Sunrise, Settlers, and Eventual Tear Gas

Masked settlers attacked Palestinian farmers and a Washington Post photojournalist near Abu Falah, as the UN says this harvest is already more violent than all of last year's.

The wire said: Palestinians and US journalist injured by Israeli settlers in West Bank harvest attack · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 16, 2026 15:38 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Every autumn Palestinians in the occupied West Bank pick olives, a crop central to rural livelihoods. This year the UN says settler attacks during the first weeks have already exceeded those of the whole 2024 season.

Why it matters

For farming families, the harvest is a year's income, and losing trees or access to land is hard to reverse. The attacks also test whether Israel and its main ally will turn stated condemnation into arrests and convictions.

Dozens of masked Israeli settlers attacked Palestinian olive pickers near the village of Abu Falah in the occupied West Bank on Friday, burning cars and olive trees, the BBC reported. A local official told the BBC that 12 Palestinians were injured, including four dual Palestinian-American citizens. Among the wounded was Heidi Levine, a photojournalist working for The Washington Post. The harvest, in other words, now comes with a threat assessment.

A BBC team witnessed one of the attacks. Reporters saw cars carrying wounded farmers speeding out along a stony track, loaded with picking equipment, as smoke rose behind them. Majdi Fheed, bleeding from the head, said about 15 men had attacked with guns and rocks. A council member from Turmus Aya said Fheed, an American citizen, was struck with nail-studded sticks, and that his elderly mother was also hurt.

Levine, 64, told reporters she was jumped and kicked while trying to protect her camera. The BBC quoted her saying the beating went on for at least 10 minutes, and that her helmet and flak jacket protected her. Al Jazeera reported she wore a vest marked "PRESS" and that her cameras and phone were taken. Washington Post executive editor Matt Murray said the paper was outraged and urged a thorough investigation.

The farmers had planned around the danger. Landowner Amin Khalil told the BBC that three villages pooled their labor because lone pickers at the edge of a village get attacked. Translation: the safety strategy was to bring more people. It did not hold. Dozens of settlers dressed in black came down the hill and set fire to groves, while the farmers stripped trees with power tools as fast as they could, one eye on a ridge where a new outpost sat.

Outposts matter to this story. All Israeli settlements are illegal under international law, according to the BBC. Outposts, the small hilltop encampments, are also illegal under Israeli law, yet many still receive state support. Translation: a structure can be unlawful and subsidized at once, a combination that is rare outside government.

When Israeli forces arrived, accounts of what followed differ in emphasis. The BBC said the soldiers fired tear gas into Palestinian fields to end the confrontation, calling it a well-worn pattern, and noted it was unclear who fired live ammunition. Al Jazeera, citing an independent journalist at the scene and Wafa, said soldiers shot a Palestinian man in the leg and did nothing to stop the settlers. The Israel Defense Forces said it sent personnel after a report of a violent confrontation, noted a female journalist was injured, and said it would examine all circumstances.

The US State Department said it was aware of the reports. It condemned criminal violence by any party and called for investigations, arrests and convictions. Translation: the list of things requested is clear, and the list of things confirmed as having happened is not yet written.

The wider numbers are the larger story. The UN says this year's harvest attacks have killed one Palestinian and injured more than 120 across the West Bank. Al Jazeera, citing Reuters, said the rights group Yesh Din recorded at least 170 attacks since September 27. Al Jazeera, which uses the word pogroms, also reported attacks in Burqa and Turmus Aya, plus the October 7 killing of Ali Hassan Yasser al-Bazzari, 44.

Al Jazeera's correspondent argued the intimidation is meant to push residents off their land so it can later become settlements. That is his interpretation, not an established finding. Imm Khaldoun, 73, gave her own forecast to the BBC: she would always return to her land. The olives, at least, are on a deadline, and so is the investigation.
