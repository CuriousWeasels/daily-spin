China Expels Record 33 Party Members, Sets Another Record for Empty Chairs

Only 279 of 376 Central Committee members attended this week's plenum, as Xi Jinping's discipline drive left a quarter of the room vacant.

The wire said: China expels a record number from Communist party amid Xi’s intensified purges of top officials · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 30, 2026 17:17 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Since 2012, Xi Jinping has run an anti-corruption campaign inside the Chinese Communist Party that has lately reached the top ranks of the party and military. The party's Central Committee met this week for its fifth plenum, a roughly annual gathering of senior officials.

Why it matters

The party that runs the world's second-largest economy is shrinking its own leadership bench ahead of a 2027 congress where Xi is expected to secure a fourth term. Fewer experienced officials and a culture of fear could make policymaking more cautious, and succession harder to plan.

China expelled a record 33 members from the Chinese Communist Party at this week's fifth plenum, the Guardian reported. Just 279 of the Central Committee's full and alternate members showed up, compared with the 376 named in 2022. That is the most empty chairs at a plenum in modern Chinese history, which is one way of measuring a campaign for discipline: count who is no longer available to be disciplined.

The confirmed expulsions included Zhang Youxia, formerly the second-ranking figure in the military hierarchy under Xi, and Ma Xingrui, the former party secretary of Xinjiang. Other absences were unexplained. Translation: a plenum is a closed-door meeting of top officials, and when someone does not turn up, the party often sees no need to say why.

The military has taken the worst of it. A series of purges has reduced the seven-man Central Military Commission, the ruling body of the People's Liberation Army, to two members: Xi himself and Zhang Shengmin, the military's anti-graft chief. Zhang was also promoted to the politburo this week and is now its only military member. The man in charge of catching corruption has, by a process of elimination, become the army's leadership.

Xi's stated goal is cleaner government. He told officials earlier this year to uproot "sick trees" and called the anti-corruption fight a "never-ending journey." The Guardian noted that the campaign has also raised questions about whether it doubles as a way to remove rivals or punish disloyalty. A never-ending journey is, among other things, a very reliable job-creation program for investigators.

DW reported that the party is asking for two things at once: more loyalty and more initiative. Xi recently denounced "two-faced functionaries" who profess loyalty while pursuing their own goals, and officials who play "nice guys" and avoid problems. Kerry Brown of King's College London sees a dilemma: loosen control to encourage initiative and corruption may grow, tighten it and initiative may wither. The party, he said, is "very, very risk averse."

Chong Ja Ian of the National University of Singapore offered a way out of the paradox. In his reading, the proactivity Xi wants is limited to implementation, not decision-making. Officials are invited to show initiative in carrying out the agenda, but not in questioning it. Translation: be bold, within the lines already drawn for you.

Brown argued the approach "kind of works" for now, since the economy is "not great, but it's not disastrous," and the outside world is "a mess." Unity, he said, keeps you alive another day; disunity can kill you the same day.

The plenum also set up the 21st Party Congress, expected in the second half of 2027, where Xi, now 73, is expected to win a fourth term. Removal of term limits in 2018 means he could stay until 2032. The Guardian noted that many analysts think the purges have been so broad that few suitable candidates remain for promotion when the Central Committee and politburo are refreshed.

That leaves the succession problem. Chong said naming a successor early could undermine Xi's authority, while failing to prepare could create a power vacuum. Solving it takes self-confidence and a readiness to step back, he said, and not everyone has that. Meanwhile, the party has begun solving the seating problem by having fewer people to seat.
