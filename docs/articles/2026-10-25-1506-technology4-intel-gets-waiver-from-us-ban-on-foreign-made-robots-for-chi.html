Intel Asks Government If Its Robots Are Illegal, Gets One-Year Answer of 'Maybe'

The FCC granted Intel a one-year waiver for foreign-made robotic vehicles in its US chip fabs, after the company said it could not tell whether the new ban applied to them.

The wire said: Intel gets waiver after worries fuzzy US ban on foreign-made robots may hit fabs · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 25, 2026 15:06 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The Federal Communications Commission has restricted certain foreign-made robots, mainly new models such as humanoid robots and robot vacuums. Intel, which runs chip factories in the US, was unsure whether the rule reached the automated vehicles that haul materials around them.

Why it matters

If a company as large as Intel cannot tell whether a rule covers its factory equipment, smaller manufacturers face the same guesswork without the lawyers or the waiver. It also shows how a trade restriction aimed at one kind of robot can spill into the supply chain for domestic chipmaking, a stated US priority.

Intel asked the Federal Communications Commission for permission to keep using foreign-made robotic vehicles in its US chip plants, and received it. According to Ars Technica, the FCC published its order on October 22, allowing the use for one year. Intel said it applied "out of an abundance of caution," which is corporate for "we read the rule several times and it did not help."

The vehicles in question are automated guided vehicles. Translation: they are carts that drive themselves along tracks or predefined paths, often with an operator watching, and move materials around warehouses and factory floors. Intel's own website describes them this way. Bloomberg News reported that such machines are typically made by Japanese firms like Murata Machinery and Daifuku Co.

Which specific machines Intel uses is not public. They were listed in a "confidential appendix" attached to the request, so the public record contains a waiver for robots whose identity is a secret. The arrangement has a certain elegance: the government has approved something that only the government and Intel have been told about.

The ban itself is aimed mainly at new models of mobile robots, such as humanoid robots and robot vacuums. Robots already approved for sale in the United States can still be imported. The rules, however, appear to restrict major modifications to previously authorized devices, which matters because factory equipment is not a thing people buy once and leave alone.

That is where Intel's uncertainty came in. Its request said it was "unclear" whether its vehicles qualify as robots covered by the ban at all, but that it wanted relief just in case. This is the regulatory equivalent of asking a police officer whether a thing you are doing is illegal, and receiving a signed note permitting you to keep doing it for twelve months.

The waiver also lets Intel make certain hardware, software, and firmware changes. Translation: it can keep the machines updated and repaired, which is the minimum required of any machine that is expected to work. Without that, the vehicles would have been allowed to exist but not to be maintained, a status usually reserved for historic monuments.

The practical question is what happens in a year. Either the FCC clarifies whether factory vehicles count as robots, or Intel returns with another appendix. Companies without Intel's resources, and with equally fuzzy robots, may simply have to guess.
