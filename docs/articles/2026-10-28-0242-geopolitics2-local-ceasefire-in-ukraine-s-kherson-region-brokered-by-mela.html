Ceasefire Brokered by First Lady and Pope Holds Across Kherson, Mostly Because Everyone Is Watching

A truce meant to feed about 2,000 people trapped in Oleshky has widened to the whole Kherson region, with evacuations planned for Friday and no agreement on where evacuees will end up.

The wire said: Local ceasefire brokered by Melania Trump starts in southern Ukraine · NPR World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 28, 2026 02:42 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Russia seized Oleshky in the first days of its 2022 invasion, and the city has had no electricity, gas or water since the Kakhovka Dam was blown up in 2023. Pope Leo XIV appealed to Vladimir Putin this month for aid access, and the White House then said Melania Trump had secured a ceasefire.

Why it matters

Roughly 1,700 to 2,000 civilians in Oleshky have little food and no safe exit, so the truce is their best chance of aid and evacuation. How it ends may also show whether small humanitarian deals can lead to broader peace talks.

A localized ceasefire between Ukraine and Russia took effect early Tuesday in the Russian-occupied city of Oleshky and was later extended across the wider Kherson region, according to the BBC and NPR. By the BBC's account, no breaches have been reported. In a war that has featured very few quiet days, a quiet day counts as a diplomatic achievement.

The mediators are an unusual pair: US First Lady Melania Trump and Pope Leo XIV. Translation: when the formal channels of diplomacy cannot deliver a bag of flour to a besieged town, the job passes to people with no formal channels at all. Mrs. Trump said on X that the ceasefire zone had expanded to provide "safe passage" for humanitarian convoys. Officials in Kherson, including deputy head Oleksandr Tolokonnikov, said Russian shelling had stopped in both the city and the wider region.

Both governments say the truce is being observed, which is its own small marvel. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said everything was going "according to plan" and thanked US mediation. Ukrainian Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha said success could "pave the way for other steps in the peace process." David Arakhamia, a senior figure in President Zelensky's parliamentary party, called it the "largest de-escalation since 2022" and said it runs until 22:59 Ukrainian time on Sunday 1 November.

The need is plain. Oleshky had 24,000 residents before the war. An estimated 1,700 to 2,000 remain, with a few thousand more in the surrounding area, and the BBC reports that many are cooking garden weeds to survive. The roads out are mined and under drone threat. One woman told the BBC she stepped on a landmine and is in hospital after two operations, worried she will miss the evacuation.

The plan runs in stages. Roads are cleared of mines, aid may arrive as early as Thursday, and evacuations are planned for Friday. Russia's defence ministry says its Dnepr group is already clearing access roads of explosive and booby-trap devices. Translation: the side that controls the town is removing the hazards, which helps the aid get in and also means it controls who leaves and by which route.

That route is the central dispute. Bridges over the Dnipro are destroyed and the riverbank is mined, so the only practical way out leads into Russian-held territory. President Zelensky warned that routes demined by Russia will send civilians "towards Russia and stay there." Russian-installed official Vladimir Saldo said accommodation is ready in Kherson, Crimea and three Russian regions, and claimed most residents want to stay in Russia. The BBC notes his account has not been corroborated on the ground.

Residents have their own doubts. Relatives told the BBC they fear Russia may not let people return to Ukraine, or may search phones for signs of Ukrainian sympathies. Many are wary of giving officials their names and addresses, though a local volunteer said about 500 people had expressed interest by Tuesday afternoon. Ukraine's general staff asked people not to spread unverified rumours about routes and timings. Communication is patchy, so rumours are the only thing moving freely.

Across the river in Kherson city, there have been no explosions or drone injuries since 03:00 on Tuesday, and cars and pedestrians are moving around. "It feels a bit calmer," resident Galina Kyrychenko told public broadcaster Suspilne. Meanwhile NPR reported that missile attacks resumed in Kyiv, a reminder that the truce is local and the war is not. Friday's evacuation will show whether this is the start of something larger or just a very well-observed pause.
