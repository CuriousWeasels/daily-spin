Musk Says Ukraine Broke Starlink Rules; Ukraine Says Physics Already Did the Job

After a throttling that disrupted drone operations, SpaceX's owner alleged offensive use of the service, while Ukrainian officials say the commercial version cannot technically do what he describes.

The wire said: Elon Musk says Ukraine’s military violated Starlink’s terms of service · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 28, 2026 02:42 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Nearly two weeks ago, Ukrainian officials accused SpaceX of limiting the military's satellite internet service, disrupting drone missions and operations. Elon Musk has now said Ukraine violated Starlink's terms of service, and Ukraine denies it.

Why it matters

A single private company's settings can affect how a national military communicates in wartime, and the public currently has only competing statements to explain why. Governments relying on commercial infrastructure are learning that the terms of service are also a foreign policy document.

Elon Musk said this week that Ukraine's military broke Starlink's terms of service by using the network for offensive drone strikes, and that the loophole was closed last week, according to The Verge. Ukrainian officials say that did not happen and that it is not possible. It is a rare dispute in which one side cites a user agreement and the other cites speed limits.

The statement arrived nearly two weeks after Ukrainian officials accused SpaceX of placing limits on the military's service that disrupted drone missions. Axios reported that Ukraine uses Starlink for communications, logistics, and defense against incoming drones. For offensive missions, officials say, Ukraine uses Starshield. Translation: Starshield is the version of Starlink controlled by the US military, which is a different product with a different landlord.

Musk pointed to a post from President Volodymyr Zelenskyy that referred to using Starlink in mid-range strikes against enemy military targets. Serhii Beskrestnov, a defense technology advisor to Zelenskyy, answered on Telegram that Starlink's systems connect only to devices moving between 70 and 90 kilometers per hour, about 43 to 55 miles per hour, and do not work in Russia. In his words, Ukraine therefore "physically cannot use commercial Starlink for deep strikes or medium-range strikes." A drone that is slower than a highway commute is apparently the only kind commercial Starlink was built to tolerate.

Ars Technica pointed to a further explanation from Serhii Sternenko, a former Ukrainian advisor. He said stationary terminals could be registered by military personnel or civilians, but any terminal working on the move had to be added to a special list and connected to Starshield. His conclusion was that civilian Starlink for drone missions was technically impossible. If true, the alleged violation would require a feature the product does not have.

Zelenskyy said on Saturday that Starlink and Starshield services had been fully restored. The Kyiv Independent reported that with the restriction lifted, the military can again connect to devices traveling faster than 400 km/h, about 248 mph. That is the difference between a throttle and no throttle, measured in drones.

The question of who pulled the lever remains untidy. Musk initially shifted blame for the slowdown to the US government, per The Verge. President Donald Trump responded that he was not involved and did not know who throttled SpaceX's services. The Wall Street Journal later reported that Trump asked Musk to lift the restrictions, which suggests that someone knew where the dial was.

So the current account is that a restriction was imposed, blamed on Washington, disowned by Washington, justified by a terms-of-service violation Kyiv says is technically impossible, and then lifted. Watch for whether anyone explains the dial in writing, or whether it stays an informal arrangement between a CEO and a war.
