Meta's Muse AI Makes Private Chats Easy to Find, Pauses Sharing

Researchers enumerated 6,800 shared Muse links with a single query, turning loan applications and revenue reviews into public reading, and Meta has now suspended the feature.

The wire said: Meta Muse exposes users' personal and business secrets when they share conversations · Ars Technica AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 20, 2026 15:53 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Muse is Meta's new general-purpose AI agent, which Mark Zuckerberg said last month was built for privacy and security. It lets users share conversations through web links, which security firm Claroc found could be discovered in bulk.

Why it matters

Anything typed into an AI assistant and shared by link may be less private than the 'unguessable URL' convention implies. Users and companies should treat share buttons as publishing tools, not as private envelopes.

Meta has paused the sharing feature in Muse, its new AI agent, after researchers reported that links users created to share conversations could be found by anyone who knew where to look. According to Ars Technica, security firm Claroc enumerated 6,800 such links. Meta said it is reviewing the report and considering changes to how the links can be discovered, which is a gentle way of describing a lock that came with a directory of doors.

Sharing links usually work like this: you click a button, the service creates a web address with a long random string, and only people you give the address to can open the page. Translation: the secrecy rests on nobody being able to guess the address. That arrangement holds up fine, provided the addresses are not also written down somewhere searchable.

According to the researchers, that is roughly what happened. The easiest route to the links was a passive DNS search. Translation: passive DNS is a commercial database that logs which domain names resolved to which servers over time, collected by sensors watching internet traffic. Per the report, once an intended recipient clicks a share link, the DNS information is recorded in a searchable database. Ars described a central design decision in how Muse builds its URLs as what made the links discoverable, though the excerpt provided does not spell out the exact mechanism.

After opening a few hundred of the 6,800 links, the researchers found material plainly not meant for the public. It included a job candidate's notes for a confidential executive-search call with a frontier AI lab, naming the lab's internal contact and meeting time. It also included a national marketing company's internal revenue review marked for its CEO, and a small-business owner's loan application showing the amount requested.

The list went on. One to-do list contained a bank fraud alert with a partial account number and a transaction amount and time. A real-estate investor's internal report listed two property addresses with exact return, income, and mortgage figures. A workplace's full-year vacation calendar showed dozens of employees against the week each would be away, which is useful information chiefly to people planning something unfriendly.

The researchers were frank about the effort involved. 'It took one query,' they wrote, adding that they had expected gathering the links to take real effort. In security, this is known as a finding. In customer service, it is known as a problem with the word 'privacy' in the marketing.

Meta's statement noted that it had opened a bug bounty program so researchers could raise scenarios like this one. Translation: a bug bounty pays outside researchers to find flaws before someone less friendly does. The company also pointed out that users can choose to share Muse artifacts, creating a page reachable by anyone with the link. That is true, and it is also the sort of sentence that works better before the 6,800 links.

What to watch next is what Meta changes. Fixing discoverability may be simple. Persuading users that a chat window is not a diary is considerably harder, particularly for the person whose bank fraud alert is now a case study.
