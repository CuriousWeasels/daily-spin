Musk Considers Merging His Two Trillion-Dollar Companies, Asks Himself for Opinion

Special committees at Tesla and SpaceX are studying a combination that would be one of the largest mergers ever, though Musk says he is not on either one.

The wire said: Elon Musk confirms he is considering merging Tesla and SpaceX · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 23, 2026 02:08 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Elon Musk said on SpaceX's earnings call that he is actively considering merging Tesla, valued at about $1.5 trillion, with SpaceX, valued at $2 trillion. Both companies have formed special committees to examine the idea, and Tesla shareholders would have to approve it.

Why it matters

Tesla shareholders could end up owning a smaller slice of a far more complex AI-and-rockets business, and Musk would gain more voting control. Tesla drivers are unlikely to notice much, since analysts say cars are increasingly an afterthought.

Elon Musk confirmed this week that he is considering merging Tesla and SpaceX, the two largest companies he runs. "Do I think it would be a good idea? Yes," he told analysts on SpaceX's earnings call, according to the BBC. He then added that nothing is agreed, which is the standard way to announce something that is mostly agreed in spirit.

The process is more formal than the enthusiasm suggests. Musk said special committees have been formed at both companies to examine the deal. Translation: a special committee is a group of directors who are supposed to negotiate on behalf of shareholders when the boss sits on both sides of the table. Musk said he is not a member of either, describing himself as a "conflicted party" as chief executive of both. He compared commenting at all to "tap dancing through a minefield", a phrase he repeated on Tesla's call a day later, when he had nothing further to add.

The scale is difficult to overstate, though the BBC and analysts tried. Tesla is valued at roughly $1.5 trillion and SpaceX at $2 trillion, so a combination would rank among the largest mergers in history. At least 50% of Tesla's shareholders would need to approve it, a threshold Musk called "quite a high hurdle". Shareholders did approve a pay package for him worth around $1tn last year, so the hurdle has been cleared by people in similar positions before.

The case for the deal depends on whom you ask. Musk says there is merit in combining work on chips, artificial intelligence and software. The Verge notes that the story runs through self-driving cars and humanoid robots at Tesla, and AI models and data centers at SpaceX. Sceptics might note a simpler benefit: AI infrastructure attracts enormous amounts of money, and a single company can pitch all of it at once. The Verge also points out that Musk's companies compete for the same scarce Nvidia chips, so merging them settles an argument he is currently having with himself.

The numbers show where the business is heading. SpaceX's AI and compute arm generated about $7.5bn of revenue in the quarter, up from $813m a year earlier, according to the BBC. That outgrew Starlink, whose revenue rose to $5.2bn from $3bn. The Verge says much of the AI revenue likely comes from renting computing capacity to Anthropic and Google, which makes it look more like CoreWeave, a company that rents out computing power, than like an AI lab. SpaceX lost $1.1bn over nine months on $27bn in revenue, though it reported a $4.4bn profit for the latest quarter.

Tesla, meanwhile, reported third-quarter sales of $28.05 billion against $28.1 billion a year earlier, and the Verge says profits fell for a second straight quarter. The company is shifting resources toward robots, including Optimus humanoids built at a California plant, and it is competing with BYD, which overtook it as the world's biggest EV maker in January. Analyst David Nicholson told the BBC the odds of a merger are "100%" and the only question is when. Musk said calling it certain would be "overstating" things.

Investors reacted promptly. SpaceX shares rose 8% in after-hours trading and Tesla shares rose 2%. Analyst Dan Sheehan of Telos Wealth Advisors told the BBC the deal would leave Tesla shareholders with a "smaller slice of a much larger and more complex business", and that there is "no real precedent" for pricing such a company. He also said the deal would not add to Musk's wealth but would concentrate it in one stock, and the BBC reports it would give him more voting control than he has at Tesla today.

There are loose ends. The Verge notes that Terafab, the planned Tesla-SpaceX chip venture, was described in SpaceX's S-1 filing as being in very early stages, with financial terms and intellectual property rights unfinalized. Musk said it would reach volume production in the second half of 2028, and when an analyst asked whether he had ordered the needed equipment, he did not answer. Regulators and a possible change in the US Congress after next month's midterms could also complicate matters, Nicholson told the BBC.

The committees now have to decide what a fair price looks like for a deal the chief executive of both companies has already called a good idea. Watch for terms, and for whether Tesla shareholders are asked to clear that high hurdle.
