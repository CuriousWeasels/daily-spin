Australia Proposes Giving Users an Off Switch for the Thing Designed to Never Turn Off

A new bill would force social media firms to let Australians disable recommendation algorithms, though not by default, and not without objections from Washington and Instagram.

The wire said: Toxic algorithms wouldn't let them go - now Australia wants an off switch · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 18, 2026 14:56 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Australia introduced a Digital Duty of Care bill this week, whose centrepiece is an algorithm switch letting users see only accounts they follow. It follows a campaign called Fix Our Feeds and Australia's earlier social media minimum age law.

Why it matters

If it passes, Australians could opt out of the engagement machinery that decides what they see, and platforms could face fines of up to A$100m for breaching a broader safety duty. Other governments watching will learn whether an off switch works when the default stays on.

The Australian government this week introduced legislation in parliament that would require social media platforms to let users turn off recommended content, so that they see only posts from accounts they follow, the BBC reported. The bill also creates a legal duty of care for online services. It is, in essence, a proposal to make companies offer a feature that their business model has spent two decades arguing nobody wants.

The switch is part of the Digital Duty of Care bill, which covers social media, gaming sites, chatbots and more. Translation: a duty of care means a company must take reasonable steps to prevent harm, rather than apologise afterwards with a blog post. Services would have to ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, a safe online environment. Children would be protected from harmful material such as content encouraging eating disorders, and adults from a narrower category of seriously harmful material. Breaches could attract fines of up to A$100m (£52m, $70m).

The human case for the bill is told by people who found the algorithm hard to leave. Varsha Yajman, now 24 and in recovery from anorexia, joined social media at 13 and says she was fed calorie challenges and weight-loss content. She says she still receives such content despite having every safety setting on and a long list of banned words. Flynn Martin, a Melbourne man, says manosphere figures flooded his feeds when he was 20 and struggling with his mental health. He was pulled out only after a period of psychosis and a hospital stay. Both say social media was not the sole cause of their problems, but was a key factor.

The policy began as a petition. Activist Chanel Contos launched Fix Our Feeds, which demanded that algorithms be off by default and that users opt in. More than 15,000 people signed, and two former prime ministers backed it. The government adopted a version in September but stopped short of making the switch off by default. Prime Minister Anthony Albanese said the aim was control for people, not government: if they want to doom scroll, that is up to them.

That compromise matters, because the history of such tools is that they are transient or come with trade-offs in functionality, according to the BBC. A switch that is available but buried, or that resets itself, is a switch in the way a fire exit behind a vending machine is an exit. Australia says it has drafted the law more tightly to prevent that, though the sources do not detail how.

The platforms have been quiet. Meta, Snap, TikTok, X and Google all declined BBC requests for comment. Instagram boss Adam Mosseri did argue that non-algorithmic feeds would show users fewer posts from friends, and said he did not believe his app was addictive, distinguishing colloquial addiction, like binge-watching a show, from clinical addiction. The BBC notes that a US jury recently ruled otherwise.

Opposition comes from the other direction too. Right-wing parties in Australia and the Trump administration say the reforms risk over-moderation and censorship. Defenders reply that users can still follow and post whatever they like, including manosphere podcasts and claims the moon landing was fake. Critics also worry the government has bitten off more than it can chew. Communications Minister Anika Wells says the bill shifts responsibility from victims to the companies that profit from Australian users.

Watch next for whether parliament keeps the switch intact, and whether the off position is as easy to find as the scroll button.
