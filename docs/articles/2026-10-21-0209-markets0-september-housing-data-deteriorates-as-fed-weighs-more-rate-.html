Housing Market Enters Deep Freeze as Fed Considers Making It Colder

Pending home sales hit a record low as 7.6% mortgage rates meet a central bank that is worried about inflation, not houses.

The wire said: Housing is flashing warning signs just as the Fed weighs more interest rate hikes · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 21, 2026 02:09 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Mortgage rates climbed sharply in September, from about 6.8% to 7.6% according to Mortgage News Daily, while inflation readings stayed hot. The Federal Reserve is now weighing rate hikes that could push borrowing costs higher still.

Why it matters

Anyone hoping to buy a home faces borrowing costs more than double those of five years ago, and anyone who already has a cheap mortgage has a strong reason never to move. The Fed's inflation fight is unlikely to ease either problem soon.

The National Association of Realtors reported Tuesday that pending home sales, which count contracts signed but not yet closed, fell 7.3% in September and 11.3% from a year earlier. That is the lowest reading in records going back to 2001, according to CNBC. Wall Street had expected a decline of 0.4%, so the forecasters were off by roughly a factor of eighteen, which in economics qualifies as a near miss.

The cause is not mysterious. The 30-year mortgage rate began September near 6.8% and ended around 7.6%, Mortgage News Daily said. Translation: a loan to buy the same house got meaningfully more expensive in four weeks, and buyers responded by declining to buy the house. NAR chief economist Lawrence Yun said housing affordability is more affected by mortgage rates than by job growth or income gains, which is a polite way of saying a raise cannot outrun a rate spike.

Construction is not faring better. CNBC reported privately owned housing starts fell 5.2% against an expected 0.4% decline, and building permits were off 0.2% where analysts had predicted a 2% gain. Single-family permits totaled 845,000, the lowest since 2023, according to the Commerce Department data cited. A separate CNBC report put single-family starts at 859,000 annualized, the lowest since September 2025. The sources use slightly different figures and categories, and both agree that the direction is down. Homebuilder sentiment, meanwhile, sits at a 14-year low, per the National Association of Home Builders.

Ordinarily, a slumping housing market invites the Fed to cut rates, since cheaper borrowing revives demand. This is not an ordinary moment. Wholesale final demand goods prices rose 2.6%, the fastest pace in a data series dating to December 2009, and the latest official CPI reading is 3.74% year over year. Fed officials have also said they are watching the inflationary effects of heavy AI investment, which is raising the cost of chips and electronics. Translation: the central bank has noticed the house is cold, but it is busy putting out a fire in the kitchen.

Markets expect the Fed to hold steady at next week's meeting, with the fed funds rate at 3.88%. Futures pricing, via CME's FedWatch tool, implies a strong chance of quarter-point hikes in December and March 2027. The 10-year Treasury yield, which mortgage rates loosely follow, stands at 5.15%, so the mortgage market is not waiting for the Fed's permission to be expensive.

There is also the lock-in effect. Goldman Sachs economist Ronnie Walker noted that more than 95% of mortgage borrowers have rates below the prevailing level, and 65% are at least 2 percentage points below it. Translation: owners with cheap mortgages will not sell, because moving means trading a 3% loan for a 7.6% one. Goldman expects existing home sales of 3.9 million in 2027, about 26% below 2019 levels. The result is a market in which almost nobody can afford to buy and almost nobody can afford to sell.

Yun offered the lone note of optimism. Mortgage rates have already risen much more than the Fed's own rate hikes, he argued, so they could be topping out. Buyers and sellers, he said, may be waiting for rates to move favorably. That is a reasonable hope, and also a description of the entire housing market at the moment: everyone is waiting, and the Fed's next move will decide for how long.
