US Bans DeepSeek From Civilian Agencies, Which Presumably Were Not Using It Wrong Enough Already

Homeland Security ordered the Chinese AI removed from most federal systems, though the Pentagon is exempt and the notice skips the exact security concerns.

The wire said: US bans DeepSeek AI from government use as tech tension with China continues · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 20, 2026 02:16 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

DeepSeek, a Chinese AI company, rattled markets after its first public release in 2025 by claiming it trained a competitive model for far less than US rivals spent. Washington has since grown increasingly critical of Chinese AI firms.

Why it matters

The ban shows that cheap, open-source AI is now a matter of national security policy, and it could complicate life for US companies that quietly build on DeepSeek's models.

The Department of Homeland Security has banned all use of DeepSeek within civilian government agencies, along with any technology built with the aid of its AI models, the BBC reported. The notice was made public on Monday, but the ban took effect at the end of July. Translation: the rule has been in force for a while, and the announcement is the part that arrived late.

The order covers civilian departments including Treasury, State and Justice. Agencies within the Department of Defense are excluded. This means the ban applies to the parts of government that file paperwork and not to the part with the aircraft carriers, a distinction the notice, according to the BBC, does not explain.

Nor does the notice detail what exactly the security concerns are. The BBC noted only that the White House has criticised Chinese AI firms this year. White House tech lead Michael Kratsios wrote in an internal memo that 'malicious exploitation' of American AI firms had been occurring, mainly by rivals in China.

American companies have added their own complaint. Anthropic and OpenAI, both described as poised to list on the public stock market in the coming months, have claimed that some Chinese firms, including DeepSeek, essentially copied their models. Translation: two companies about to ask investors for large sums would like it known that their expensive product was apparently easy to imitate, which is a delicate thing to advertise before an IPO.

The underlying appeal of DeepSeek is arithmetic. It is open source, meaning free to access and cheaper to use, and the BBC reported that using it costs a developer a fraction of what closed models such as Gemini, Claude or OpenAI's Codex do. Last year DeepSeek said it took about $6m to train its debut model, against roughly $100m for OpenAI's GPT-4. Such revelations sent tech stocks tumbling, which is the market's way of saying it had not budgeted for a bargain.

That appeal has not been confined to China. DeepSeek's free chatbot app ranks among the most downloaded in Apple's App Store, and its models are built into offerings from US companies including Snowflake, Vercel and OpenRouter. The ban reaches technology built with the aid of DeepSeek, so federal contractors and agencies may now need to check what is inside the software they buy. That is a task most procurement offices will approach with the enthusiasm of a dental appointment.

The timing is also notable. The newest DeepSeek version was released last week, and users online have praised it as beating even the most advanced OpenAI tools on common tasks such as software coding. The BBC said DHS, DeepSeek and the Chinese embassy in Washington had been contacted for comment.

What to watch next: whether the government eventually explains the security concern in writing, and whether the companies built on DeepSeek decide the savings are worth the paperwork.
