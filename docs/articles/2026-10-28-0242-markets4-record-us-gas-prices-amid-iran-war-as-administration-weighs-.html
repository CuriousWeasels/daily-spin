Administration Eases Russian Diesel Sanctions to Lower Pump Prices Before Tuesday's Midterms

A deal with Moscow will add roughly 75,000 to 124,000 barrels a day to a market that burns 28 million, which analysts say will not materially lower prices.

The wire said: As Trump touts energy companies’ fortunes, U.S. consumers face the most expensive year for gas prices ever · MarketWatch

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 28, 2026 02:42 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Fuel prices have spiked since the U.S. and Israel attacked Iran on Feb. 28, and the administration now faces voters in next Tuesday's midterm elections. President Trump struck a deal with Vladimir Putin letting Russia export diesel under a U.S. sanctions waiver.

Why it matters

Drivers and businesses that depend on diesel should not expect relief at the pump from this deal, according to analysts cited by CNBC. The episode also shows how election-year price pressure can shape sanctions policy toward a country the U.S. is trying to pressure over Ukraine.

The Trump administration has suspended U.S. sanctions on Russian diesel exports, and Energy Secretary Chris Wright told CNBC on Tuesday that more Russian fuel could be allowed onto the market depending on Moscow's behavior. He described the suspension as "an incentive" for Russia to end the war in Ukraine. Fuel is, in this arrangement, both the reward and the thing voters are angry about.

According to CNBC, the deal calls for Russia to export 800,000 metric tons of diesel in October and November, followed by another million tons. Translation: a metric ton is a unit of weight, and diesel is normally traded by volume, so analysts converted. The first shipment of 300,000 tons works out to about 75,000 barrels per day if spread over a month. The second, 500,000 tons, equals about 124,000 barrels per day.

Global demand is around 28 million barrels per day, so the headline volumes amount to a rounding error with a flag on it. Analysts told CNBC the deal will not materially lower prices. Wright himself said it would help with supplies only "on the margin," which is the economist's way of saying a little, eventually, maybe.

The logistics are already underway. MarineTraffic shows the Russian-flagged tanker Talisman expected on the U.S. East Coast on Nov. 9, five days after the election. Another tanker, the Leruo, has left Russia bound for the "Atlantic Coast" without saying whether that means America. Wright said more tankers could "quite possibly" follow, and described the policy as "trying to put a carrot in front of Russia."

The carrot is awkward for several reasons. Ukraine has been striking Russian refineries this year to reduce the revenue the Kremlin earns from energy exports, and those strikes forced Moscow to impose a diesel export ban in July. The Kremlin has partially lifted that ban to honor the deal with Trump. Ukraine's campaign to reduce Russian diesel sales and Washington's campaign to buy some of it are therefore running at the same time.

President Zelenskyy has sharply criticized the arrangement. Several Republican senators joined Democratic colleagues in a letter calling it "a gift to our adversary and a betrayal of our Allies." It is rare for the Senate to agree on anything, and the subject it found is a diesel waiver.

Meanwhile, MarketWatch reports that Trump has continued to tout the fortunes U.S. energy companies have made during the Iran war while campaigning for Republican candidates. The same report says consumers face the most expensive year for gas prices ever. Brent crude was last recorded at $115.22, and CPI inflation stood at 3.74% year over year in September. Producers are celebrating the price level and motorists are paying it, which is the standard division of labor.

The kicker arrives next week. The election is Tuesday, the first tanker docks Nov. 9, and the oil market will go on needing 28 million barrels a day regardless of who wins.
