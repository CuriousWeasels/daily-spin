Saudi Arabia Postpones 'Davos in the Desert' as Bankers Decline to Be Intercepted

With Houthi strikes hitting Riyadh's airport 20 times this month, Britain is sending Typhoon jets and the kingdom's flagship investor conference is moving to 2027.

The wire said: Saudi Arabia cancels 'Davos in the Desert' due to Yemen's Houthi strikes · NPR World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 21, 2026 16:10 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Yemen's civil war has escalated since a years-long truce collapsed in September, with Houthi rebels striking Saudi infrastructure and attacking tankers. Britain announced military support for Riyadh in September and expanded it this week.

Why it matters

The conference is central to Saudi plans to attract foreign money and reduce reliance on oil, so a postponement shows how security risk can undercut an economic strategy. Houthi attacks on tankers are also pushing up energy prices, which reach ordinary consumers.

Saudi Arabia has postponed its Future Investment Initiative conference, informally known as 'Davos in the Desert,' until 2027, DW reported. The event was scheduled for October 26-29 and was expected to draw leading international financiers. According to media reports cited by DW, major banks and private equity firms had raised security concerns about attending. Translation: the people who manage other people's money prefer not to do it near incoming missiles.

On Tuesday, the British government announced it will deploy Royal Air Force Typhoon fighter jets to Saudi Arabia, following a request from Riyadh. The Ministry of Defense said the aircraft will protect Saudi cities and critical infrastructure from drones and missiles. They will not carry out offensive strikes on Houthi targets in Yemen. Translation: the jets are there to play defense, which is the military term for being very expensive and hoping to be bored.

Britain is also sending an Atlas A400M transport aircraft and military advisers, and has extended for another 90 days its offer of air-to-air refueling for Saudi aircraft operating against the Houthis. British Defense Secretary Wes Streeting called the attacks 'absolutely despicable,' according to DW. He singled out strikes on Riyadh's international airport, which has been targeted 20 times since the start of October. Reports say those strikes have killed at least 18 people and wounded hundreds.

The violence is not confined to Saudi territory. Yemeni authorities said a Houthi strike on Monday killed 58 people at a training camp for Saudi-backed government security forces. The Houthis have also imposed a naval blockade and attacked tankers, disrupting oil exports and pushing up energy prices. Saudi Arabia and the United States accuse Iran of supporting the Houthis, an allegation Tehran denies.

The economics here are straightforward and slightly awkward. The conference is a key part of the kingdom's effort to attract foreign investment and diversify away from oil. But the same conflict is hitting oil facilities and shipping, which means Saudi Arabia is trying to sell a calm, diversified future while its present is being shot at through the airport.

For Britain, the arrangement offers a way to show support for an ally without joining the offensive war. Whether that line holds depends on whether the attacks continue, and the sources give no sign that they are slowing. For now the investors will wait, and the Typhoons will do the attending.
