French Supermarket Fridges Hacked Into Ovens, Proving Password Was Basically the Calendar

An estimated 700 French supermarkets saw refrigeration systems heated to as high as 50°C after attackers reached internet-connected controllers, with some fires reported.

The wire said: Fridge hack costing millions and sparking fires stemmed from poor security · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 15, 2026 14:38 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Earlier this week, attackers compromised refrigeration controllers at an estimated 700 supermarkets across France, triggering defrosters until food spoiled and, in some cases, electrical systems caught fire. According to Ars Technica, the affected devices had known, publicly documented security flaws.

Why it matters

The incident shows that ordinary infrastructure, from store freezers to building controls, is now online and often poorly secured, and the costs of that land on grocers, insurers and eventually shoppers. It also shows that fixes existing is not the same as fixes being installed.

According to Ars Technica, a mass hack of refrigeration systems at an estimated 700 supermarkets in France caused them to malfunction this week, spoiling what the outlet called untold tons of perishables and, in some cases, causing fires. French authorities say the compromised equipment was a set of internet-connected controllers that technicians use to manage whole-store fleets of fridges and freezers. It is a rare cyberattack in which the stolen item is cold.

The attackers, who remain unknown, activated defrosters for long enough that the units reached temperatures as high as 50° Celsius, or 122° Fahrenheit. Translation: a freezer was persuaded to do the job of a mildly ambitious oven. By the time anyone noticed, the food was ruined and some electrical systems had caught fire.

Ars Technica reports that this was not an exotic break-in. Three controller models reportedly involved, sold by Danfoss of Denmark and by Dixell and its parent company Copeland in the US, all had critical vulnerabilities documented in security advisories and public research. Those flaws let an attacker log in remotely with the same rights as an administrator. Translation: the person fixing the fridge and the person cooking it had the same keys.

The Danfoss case is the most instructive. Until recently, the controllers used a credential that changes each day, which sounds rigorous until you learn how it works. According to Ars Technica, the password was generated by publicly available firmware based on the date. Anyone willing to read the code could work out the password for any device that had not received a recent update. The same daily password also worked across models in hundreds or thousands of stores. It is security in the sense that a calendar is a secret.

Copeland and Dixell controllers had their own problem. Ars Technica says a raft of critical vulnerabilities fixed in the past few months gave similar remote access. The word doing the work there is fixed. A patch is a promise the manufacturer makes, and an update is a chore the customer must perform, preferably before the fridge is on fire.

The incentives are tidy. Manufacturers sell convenience, namely remote administration without a truck roll. Stores buy it because it saves labor. Nobody is rewarded for the unglamorous work of changing defaults and installing updates, until 700 supermarkets discover the cost of that omission in spoiled stock. Ars Technica's verdict was blunt: as is now the norm, basic cybersecurity would have prevented it.

What to watch next is who pays and who patches. The sources do not say how large the losses were beyond the word millions in the headline, or who was behind the attack. The one detail that is clear is that the next advisory will arrive before the next update does.
