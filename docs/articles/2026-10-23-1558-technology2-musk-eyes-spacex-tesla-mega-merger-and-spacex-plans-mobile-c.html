Musk Says Merging SpaceX and Tesla Is a Good Idea, Adds Nothing Is Decided

Special committees are weighing a $3.5 trillion combination that would be the largest merger ever, and the man proposing it cannot vote on it.

The wire said: Why is Elon Musk plotting a mega-merger of SpaceX and Tesla? · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 23, 2026 15:58 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Elon Musk confirmed that special committees are assessing a possible merger of Tesla, valued at about $1.5 trillion, and SpaceX, valued at $2 trillion. He stressed that nothing has been agreed and that no outcome is guaranteed.

Why it matters

A merger would put Musk's cars, robots, rockets, satellites and AI into one stock, changing what Tesla investors actually own. Tesla drivers are unlikely to notice much soon, but shareholders could end up with a smaller slice of a far stranger company.

Elon Musk, chief executive of both Tesla and SpaceX, said on Tuesday that merging the two companies would be a good idea. According to the BBC, he added that special committees have been formed to assess a deal, and that nothing has been agreed. On Tesla's earnings call the next day he said he felt like he was "tap-dancing through a minefield", which is a reasonable description of announcing the largest merger in history while insisting it is hypothetical.

The arithmetic is simple. Tesla is valued at roughly $1.5 trillion and SpaceX at $2 trillion, so a combined company would be worth about $3.5 trillion and would dwarf any previous deal. Shares of SpaceX rose 8% in after-hours trading and Tesla's rose 2%. Translation: investors heard "maybe" and priced it as "probably."

The approval process has a built-in awkwardness. Half of Tesla's shareholders would need to back the deal, a threshold Musk himself called "quite a high hurdle". As boss of both companies he cannot take part in shareholder discussions. He is, however, a key Tesla shareholder, and analyst Dan Sheehan of Telos Wealth Advisors told the BBC he expects Musk will likely get his way within a few years. Shareholders did approve a pay package valued around $1 trillion last year, so there is precedent for agreeing with him.

David Nicholson of The Futurum Group put the odds of a merger at 100 percent, with timing the only open question. Musk said calling it a certainty would be overstating things. Nicholson also read the recent Tesla Roadster, an electric car powered by SpaceX rocket thrusters, as a symbolic hint. It is rare for a product launch to double as a pre-announcement.

The business logic is that Tesla brings cars, robots and self-driving software, while SpaceX brings rockets, data centres, satellites and telecoms. Musk cited overlapping work on chips and artificial intelligence. SpaceX has already absorbed xAI and X before going public this year, so this would be consolidation round three. Sheehan noted the deal would not add to Musk's wealth but would concentrate it in one stock, and the BBC reported it would give him more voting control than he has at Tesla today.

For Tesla customers, experts expect little near-term change. Nicholson described vehicles as almost "an afterthought" next to the combined technology. Sheehan asked how much priority cars get inside a company increasingly driven by AI infrastructure, a question Tesla owners may want answered before their next service appointment. Tesla's third-quarter sales were $28.05 billion against $28.1 billion a year earlier, and it faces competition from BYD, which overtook it as the world's biggest EV maker in January.

SpaceX, meanwhile, has other projects. Bloomberg reported, via The Verge, that it is in talks with Crown Castle about access to at least 80,000 cell sites, roughly half of the 158,500 towers operating in the US, as it tries to compete with Verizon, T-Mobile and AT&T. Crown Castle's CEO said "you can assume that we're talking to all parties," which confirms nothing and denies nothing. SpaceX recently bought low-band spectrum licences and won FCC approval for 15,000 upgraded Starlink Mobile satellites, after paying EchoStar $17 billion for 2GHz spectrum last year. Translation: spectrum is the radio airwaves phones need, and SpaceX is buying a lot.

Regulators may take an interest, particularly if Democrats, many critical of Musk, gain seats in next month's US midterms. Sheehan said there is no real precedent for how a public company like Tesla gets priced in such a deal. Watch the special committees, who must weigh a merger that would hand the proposer more control.
