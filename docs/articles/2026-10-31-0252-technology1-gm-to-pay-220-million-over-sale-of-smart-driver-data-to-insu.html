GM Pays $220 Million to Settle Claims It Sold Drivers' Habits to Insurers

General Motors reached a settlement with 35 states and the District of Columbia over Smart Driver data that ended up with insurance companies, and a third party will now check its homework.

The wire said: GM to pay $220 million after selling customer Smart Driver data · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 31, 2026 02:52 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

GM marketed Smart Driver as a tool for monitoring teen drivers or one's own habits, but in 2024 it emerged that the data was being shared with brokers who sold it to insurers. GM and OnStar settled with the FTC last year, and the deal was finalized in January.

Why it matters

Data collected by your car can end up in the hands of the company that sets your insurance premium, often without you knowing you agreed to it. The settlement suggests regulators are willing to treat that as a legal problem rather than a feature.

General Motors will pay $220 million to settle a lawsuit brought by the District of Columbia and 35 states, whose attorneys general joined forces over the company's handling of data from its Smart Driver system, according to Ars Technica. The feature was sold as a way for parents to watch teen drivers or for adults to examine their own habits. It turned out that a third audience had been watching the whole time.

According to Ars Technica, GM shared that data with brokers, who then sold it to insurance companies. Some drivers found their insurance became more expensive as a result. Many of them said they had no idea they had agreed to any of this, which is a reasonable thing to say about a permission buried somewhere in a vehicle's enrollment process.

Translation: a data broker is a middleman that collects information from one company and resells it to another. The driver is not a party to the transaction, though the driver is the product, and also, eventually, the person billed for it.

This is not GM's first reckoning. Last year GM and its OnStar subsidiary reached a settlement with the FTC, finalized in January, that bars them from sharing geolocation or driver behavior data for five years. At that time GM also discontinued Smart Driver across its entire range, which is one way of resolving a product problem: stop selling the product.

GM said in a statement that "safer, more helpful and personalized experiences are enabled by connected vehicle data." It added that it is committed to being clear about what information is used, how it is protected, and what choices customers have. The statement does not say whether the clarity was scheduled to begin before or after the lawsuits.

One detail stands out. According to TechCrunch, as cited by Ars Technica, the state settlement is notable because it requires a third party to assess GM's compliance with the new privacy program. Translation: GM will no longer be the only one grading its own privacy practices, which is the arrangement that produced the lawsuits.

The sources do not say how the $220 million will be divided among the states or whether affected drivers will receive anything. Readers hoping for a refund on a premium that rose because of their own commute should watch for those details.
