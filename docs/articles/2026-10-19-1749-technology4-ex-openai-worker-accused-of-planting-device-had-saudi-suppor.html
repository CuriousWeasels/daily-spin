Prosecutors Say OpenAI Bug Suspect Has Friends in High Places, Flights Included

A court filing says the former OpenAI engineer accused of hiding a recorder behind a conference-room TV had Saudi royal contacts and a family one-way ticket out.

The wire said: Ex-OpenAI worker accused of planting device had Saudi support says court filing · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 19, 2026 17:49 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Abdulrahman Alfozan, a 35-year-old Saudi national and former OpenAI engineer, is charged with secretly planting an audio recording device in an OpenAI meeting room. Prosecutors have now filed to keep him in custody before trial.

Why it matters

The case shows that the most valuable assets at AI companies are conversations, and that the people with access to the building are part of the security perimeter. It also tests how courts weigh alleged foreign government ties when deciding who gets released before trial.

A federal court filing in California says a former OpenAI engineer accused of planting a recording device in a company meeting room had "considerable support" from the Saudi government, the BBC reported. Prosecutors say Abdulrahman Alfozan, who worked at the company for about a year, was in "frequent communication" with a member of the Saudi royal family. The Saudi government did not immediately respond to the BBC's request for comment, which is the standard diplomatic way of saying nothing yet.

According to the filing, OpenAI found the device on 30 September. It was plugged into the back of a TV in a conference room and covered with toilet paper. Translation: the alleged spy tool was a recorder, and the alleged camouflage was a bathroom supply. It is a reminder that corporate espionage does not always require a cinematic gadget budget.

The company then installed a camera of its own. On 5 October, prosecutors say, it filmed Alfozan entering the room and appearing to look behind the television where the recorder had been inserted. The FBI arrested him on 9 October, and OpenAI terminated his contract.

The government's argument for detention rests on two things: national security and travel plans. The filing says executives met government officials in that room during the days the device was believed to be in place, so the alleged conduct could pose "a danger to the national security of the United States." Translation: the concern is not just what a company said to itself, but who else was in the room.

On flight risk, prosecutors say Alfozan was due to fly from San Francisco to Riyadh on a one-way itinerary three days after his arrest, with his wife and son. They say his wife told FBI agents that her US visa was expiring and the family was "permanently moving back to Saudi Arabia," which her husband intended to do too. The day after the arrest, the filing says, she and her son flew to Doha, Qatar, on a one-way business class ticket booked and paid for by a Saudi military official.

"Ultimately, the defendant has little incentive to remain in the United States to see this case through," prosecutors argued. In pretrial detention hearings, a one-way business class ticket is not a neutral piece of evidence. The filing does not say what the recorder captured, if anything, and the FBI investigation is ongoing.

Alfozan was scheduled to appear in federal court in San Francisco on Monday. If convicted, he faces up to five years in prison and a $250,000 fine. For an alleged intelligence operation involving a company valued as a strategic national asset, the statutory maximum is a notably modest number.

The next thing to watch is whether the judge accepts the flight-risk argument. The filing's case that he will leave the country already has the receipts: the airline booked it.
