Fed Holds Rates, Cuts Number of Times It Will Be Asked to Change Them

Chairman Kevin Warsh kept the benchmark rate at 3.75% to 4% on a 10-2 vote, then announced that future meetings will include fewer opportunities to be wrong about it.

The wire said: Here are five key takeaways from the Fed rate decision and Warsh news conference · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 29, 2026 17:39 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The Federal Reserve raised rates in September under new Chairman Kevin Warsh, after holding them in June and July, while inflation stayed well above its 2% target. Markets spent October guessing whether a second hike would follow this week.

Why it matters

With inflation at 3.74% and the 10-year Treasury near 5%, borrowing costs for mortgages, car loans and businesses depend on whether the Fed hikes in December. Fewer rate votes also means fewer scheduled moments when anyone outside the Fed learns what it is thinking.

The Federal Reserve voted 10-2 on Wednesday to keep its benchmark rate in a range of 3-3/4 to 4 percent, according to the central bank's statement. Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack dissented, both preferring a quarter-point increase. Translation: two officials wanted to raise rates now, which is the Fed's version of shouting across the table.

CNBC reported that Cook's dissent was the real jolt. Governors rarely vote against the majority on rates, and she had not done so since joining the board in 2022. Markets had expected a hold, so the surprise was not the decision but who objected to it.

The statement described the economy as expanding at a strong pace, with resilient spending, strong productivity and robust investment. It also said inflation remains elevated and promised the Committee will deliver price stability. Translation: growth is good, prices are not, and the Fed would like credit for the part it has not yet done. Official data put annual CPI inflation at 3.74%, against a 2% target.

At his news conference, Warsh pushed back on the idea that policy is on autopilot. "If we had a firm conclusion about the conduct of monetary policy, we'd announce it to much fanfare today," he said. He also said that while financial conditions are tighter, he is "unwilling and unable" to call them tight. CNBC's analysis read this as groundwork for a possible December hike, which is a lot of work for one adjective.

The bigger structural news was the calendar. Starting in 2027, the Fed will still meet eight times a year, but one meeting will carry no rate vote and no statement. In 2028 that rises to two, leaving six chances to change rates outside emergencies. Translation: the Fed will keep holding meetings, but some will be for thinking rather than deciding, a distinction most workplaces have never managed.

CNBC's analysis said the change devalues a good chunk of Fedspeak, since a regional president's remarks matter less when a vote is months away. Regional presidents also lose one scheduled vote in their rotation. Advocates for transparency argue that sunlight adds rigor, and critics such as PGIM's Robert Sockin said Warsh did not sufficiently explain why conditions did not justify a hike. Sockin called holding a policy mistake.

Warsh also declined to confirm the market assumption that officials who talked down hike expectations before the meeting did so at his direction. "To suggest somehow that I am some kind of all-knowing person sending messages out, that's not the committee of the FOMC that I know," he said. CNBC noted this gives him plausible deniability for guidance the Fed formally prefers not to give, and that the market received the message anyway.

Even the press room is being redesigned. Reporters are limited to one question, the conference runs about half an hour, and seating moved from larger outlets up front to alphabetical order last month and reverse alphabetical this month. Analysts disagree on what it all signals: JPMorgan Wealth Management sees patience, while Principal Asset Management expects hikes in December and early 2027.

The next test is December, with a stack of new data due first. If inflation stays stubborn, the Fed has a chairman who says he is flexible, a committee with two members already voting to hike, and a shrinking number of meetings in which to explain it.
