DHS Staff Directory Published Online, Though Not by DHS and Not as Intended

Details on more than 41,000 Homeland Security personnel were leaked in a database called Icepick, the third government data incident in recent weeks, according to the BBC.

The wire said: Details of thousands of US immigration agents leaked in major hack · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 30, 2026 17:17 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

A group called the Department of Peace says it accessed Department of Homeland Security records, and the transparency site Distributed Denial of Secrets, with journalist maia crimew, published them. The BBC reports the leak follows recent hacks or suspected hacks at the FBI and the Federal Election Commission.

Why it matters

The leak puts names, work addresses and phone numbers of federal employees into public view, raising security concerns for staff and accountability questions for an agency whose agents often go unidentified. It also adds to a run of government data incidents that tests how well agencies protect what they hold.

Details of more than 41,000 personnel at the US Department of Homeland Security were published online on Wednesday, the BBC reported. The data includes names, work addresses, phone numbers and descriptions of the work each person does, covering Immigration and Customs Enforcement, Customs and Border Protection and other units. It is, in effect, an employee directory, compiled by people who were not asked to compile it.

According to the data, more than 21,000 of those listed work for CBP and nearly 6,600 for ICE. Other records cover Homeland Security Investigations, the Federal Protective Service and even the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. Translation: the agency responsible in part for defending American systems from hackers appears in the list of people whose information was obtained by hackers.

The material was put in a database called Icepick. The Department of Peace says it obtained the records through Okta, a single sign-on service. Translation: Okta is the digital front desk that checks whether you are allowed into the building, so that employees need only one password rather than forty. Okta told the BBC that it is aware of the claims but that "Okta is secure," and that it has no evidence its data or systems were compromised. The BBC does not say how the group actually got in, so the two accounts are currently both on the table.

The leak also reportedly includes dozens of previously unknown operation names, the agents who led and worked on them, and some of the technology they used, including products from Cellebrite and Palantir, as the BBC spelled the first. DHS said it is "aware of a potential breach" and is working to address it. It added that it takes threats against its employees seriously, which is the sort of sentence that arrives pre-written.

The publishers say the point is not a full directory. Emma Best of DDoSecrets said the aim was to replace "anonymity with accountability." She described the data as identifying officials overseeing covert operations, the hunt for leakers and the failed BlueLeaks investigation. BlueLeaks was a 2020 leak of US police data, which DDoSecrets also hosted. The Department of Peace tied its action to Markwayne Mullin, who became Homeland Security Secretary in March and said on July 17 he was "turning up the heat." The group's reply, in effect, was that heat travels in both directions.

The context is a long-running dispute over anonymity. The BBC notes that ICE agents often conduct operations with faces covered by masks and sunglasses and rarely identify themselves, despite a legal requirement to do so, and that politicians have demanded DHS enforce it. This year, the BBC says, ICE agents have shot at more than two dozen people and killed at least nine, including Alex Pretti and Renee Good in Minnesota, neither of whom was the target of an arrest. Deportations since Trump's return in 2025 have reached nearly 1 million, per the Migration Policy Institute, and more than 70% involved people with no criminal record.

The leak is the third recent government data incident. Last month the FBI was hacked by a group called ShinyHunters, which took personal information on thousands of agents, and the Federal Election Commission was recently taken offline over a suspected hacking attempt. Taken together, the pattern suggests that the federal government has become very good at collecting information and somewhat less practiced at keeping it.

What to watch: whether DHS confirms the scope of the breach, whether Okta's denial survives an investigation, and whether the agency responds by enforcing its identification rules or by tightening its passwords. Only one of those requires a new software license.
