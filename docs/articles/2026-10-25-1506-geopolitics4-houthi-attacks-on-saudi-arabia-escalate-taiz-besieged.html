Saudi Arabia Imports an AI Targeting Tool to Fight an Enemy That Thrives on Being Targeted

As Houthi missiles reach cities near Mecca, Riyadh gets US intelligence and software, though not the American military action it reportedly requested.

The wire said: Saudi air defences intercept Houthi barrage near Mecca · Al Jazeera

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 25, 2026 15:06 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Yemen's Houthi rebels have escalated attacks on Saudi Arabia since September, striking an airport and oil facilities, while fighting inside Yemen has intensified. Saudi Arabia has sought help from allies, including the United States.

Why it matters

The escalation threatens air travel, energy infrastructure and civilians in Saudi Arabia and Yemen, and tests whether AI-assisted targeting can achieve what years of conventional bombing did not.

Saudi air defences intercepted a barrage of missiles and drones fired from Yemen that began Saturday evening, according to Al Jazeera. Saudi Civil Defence said shrapnel from two intercepted ballistic missiles fell in Jeddah, Taif, Rabigh and part of Mecca province, and a drone and a ballistic missile were stopped over Riyadh. In total, 10 ballistic missiles aimed at the southern region were intercepted. Interception is the system working as designed, though it does require the debris to land somewhere.

The Gulf Cooperation Council's secretary-general, Jasem Mohamed AlBudaiwi, called the strikes a grave violation of the sanctity of the Holy Sites. Translation: a regional bloc of Gulf states is expressing condemnation, which is the one weapon it can deploy at no cost. The Houthis, a Zaydi Shia group, have denied targeting Muslim holy sites.

Riyadh's airport is part of the story. International flights from King Khalid International Airport were set to resume from Terminal 2, with Terminal 1 reserved for domestic flights, after suspensions following Houthi strikes. One strike on October 10 killed 13 people and wounded hundreds. Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree responded by declaring the airport a military target and warning airlines and travelers away. Airport reopening plans now include reading enemy press statements.

Meanwhile, DW reported that Saudi Arabia is looking to Washington for help. Media reports say President Donald Trump declined Mohammed bin Salman's request for direct US military action against the Houthis. The US is instead providing intelligence, including an AI targeting tool. CNN, citing unnamed American officials, reported in September that 100 to 200 US soldiers were in Saudi Arabia as part of a new joint force command to help. This is what diplomats call support short of intervention, and what a restaurant calls delivery.

The tool resembles the Maven Smart System, which grew out of a 2017 Pentagon AI program. Translation: it is software that pulls in satellite and drone feeds, labels likely rocket launchers, tanks and convoys, and suggests how to hit them. Analysts at the Center for Strategic and International Studies said it replaces humans squinting at hours of footage. DW reported that in the February attack on Iran it helped US forces hit more than 1,000 targets in the first 24 hours, versus roughly 100 a day before.

Speed is the selling point and also the problem. A human is supposed to verify each target, but DW noted that among the sites hit in Iran on day one was a primary school, where an American missile killed almost 200 people, most of them children. Analysts writing in War on the Rocks said loosened safeguards in Israel's use of AI targeting overshadowed its benefits in Gaza. The kill chain, the time from spotting a target to striking it, shortens. Review time shortens with it.

There are also practical limits. The Houthis use truck-mounted missiles and small drone swarms from rugged hills and canyons, so fighters may be gone before any strike arrives. Dust storms, wind, terrain changes and decoys can all reduce the software's success rate. Chatham House's Farea al-Muslimi said the group's ideology of martyrdom and sacrifice means bombing can serve it well. Journalist Antony Loewenstein told DW he doubts AI will change the outcome, except by causing civilian casualties.

On the ground, the cost is already visible. Yemen's forces said they carried out 736 strikes on Houthi positions in 24 hours, claiming 307 people neutralised. The Rasd Organization for Rights and Freedoms warned that Taiz, besieged by the Houthis since October 4, faces shortages of dialysis supplies for about 1,110 kidney patients, along with 192 intensive care patients and 154 babies in incubators. NPR separately reported a severe drinking water crisis in the city.

What to watch: whether faster targeting produces any change beyond a faster pace of targeting. Past campaigns by Saudi Arabia, the US and others have failed to significantly weaken the Houthis, and software has so far not been shown to supply the missing ingredient.
