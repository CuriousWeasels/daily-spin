Lula Wins Fourth Term in Brazil After Losing the First Round, Which Had Never Worked Before

Brazil's 80-year-old president took 51.79% in the runoff, while his opponent asked for the result to be annulled and markets sold off.

Catch up

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, a left-wing former metal worker, faced Flávio Bolsonaro, son of the imprisoned former president Jair Bolsonaro, in a runoff after Bolsonaro finished first in round one on 4 October. Lula has already served three non-consecutive terms as president.

Why it matters

Brazil is Latin America's largest country, and a contested result, a hostile Congress and a jittery market will shape its economy and its politics through 2030. The outcome also tests whether a regional swing to the right has a limit.

Brazil's Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva won a fourth term as president on Sunday, taking 51.79% of the vote to Flávio Bolsonaro's 48.21%, according to the BBC and The Guardian. It is a historic result in two ways: no Brazilian has won four terms before, and no one since the return of civilian rule in 1985 had won after losing the first round. Lula turns 81 on Tuesday, so the record comes with a birthday attached.

The comeback had a mechanism, and it was not mysterious. In round one, Bolsonaro led 47% to 45%, but 33 million people did not vote. The BBC reported that Lula's camp spent the weeks afterward trying to get those people to show up, and abstention fell by the largest margin between rounds of a presidential election since 1989. Translation: the winning strategy was persuading people who already existed to leave the house.

Bolsonaro has not conceded. He was reportedly poised to give a victory speech, then called the result "inexplicable" and asked for it to be annulled. Observers told the BBC this was no surprise, because his father did the same after losing to Lula in 2022. Jair Bolsonaro is now serving a 27-year sentence for a coup attempt, which suggests the family tradition has costs.

The challenge appears weak, and the reasons are mostly arithmetic. Lula's margin is 4.4 million votes. Bolsonaro's allies point to Supreme Court Justice Cristiano Zanin's decision to extend voting nationwide by two hours, noting that he once represented Lula as a lawyer. But the BBC reported that both campaigns had asked for the extension: Lula's cited a lack of buses in São Paulo and Bahia, and Bolsonaro's cited heavy rain. Both sides wanted more time because both sides wanted every vote. Electoral court president Kássio Nunes Marques, who was appointed by Jair Bolsonaro, confirmed the result and said the extension did not change it.

Bolsonaro later said in an Instagram video that police told him to hand over his passports and that he had been banned from leaving the country. The sources do not say more about why.

Abroad, congratulations arrived from across the spectrum, including Mexico's Claudia Sheinbaum, Peru's Keiko Fujimori, Emmanuel Macron, Ursula von der Leyen and Canada's Mark Carney. US President Donald Trump was the exception. Without evidence, he said it "looked like another rigged election" while also saying he had not taken sides. Flávio Bolsonaro visited Trump at the White House earlier this year seeking his support, so the neutrality was of a particular kind.

Lula answered the Americans in his victory speech. Referring to Trump's so-called "Donroe Doctrine", which seeks US dominance in the Western Hemisphere, he said Brazil "will not accept being anyone's backyard". He also promised his fourth term would be the best of any president in Brazil's history, a modest goal.

Investors were less festive. The BBC reported that the São Paulo stock exchange opened 15% down, triggering a brief suspension before a slight recovery, while interest rate futures rose. Translation: traders are betting that borrowing costs will climb because they doubt Lula's spending plans, and they would like to be paid for the doubt. He also faces a divided country and a Congress packed with Bolsonaro allies, so those plans will need negotiating.

The new term starts on 5 January and runs to 2030. Lula has beaten a regional trend that has put right-wing candidates in power in Argentina, Chile and Colombia. What he does with the victory depends on a Congress that did not vote for him and a market that has already expressed its view.
