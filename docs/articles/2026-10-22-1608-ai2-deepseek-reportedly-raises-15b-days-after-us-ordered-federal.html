US Bans DeepSeek From Federal Computers; Investors Respond With $15 Billion

Two days after Washington ordered civilian agencies to remove the Chinese AI lab's technology, Bloomberg reports DeepSeek raised about 100 billion yuan.

The wire said: DeepSeek reportedly raises $15B days after US restrictions · TechCrunch AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 22, 2026 16:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

DeepSeek, a Hangzhou-based AI lab, rattled US tech in early 2025 with a free, open-weight reasoning model. The U.S. has now ordered federal civilian agencies to remove its technology over security concerns.

Why it matters

The funding suggests US restrictions are not cutting off Chinese AI labs from capital, and that rivalry in AI is increasingly funded domestically on both sides. It also hints at how crowded and expensive the global AI race has become.

Two days after the U.S. ordered federal civilian agencies to remove DeepSeek's technology from their systems over security concerns, the Chinese AI lab raised roughly $15 billion, or about 100 billion yuan, Bloomberg reported, as relayed by TechCrunch. It is one of the largest funding rounds in China. As penalties go, this one has a distinctive feature: the penalized party got richer.

Potential investors include battery maker CATL and Tencent, the company behind WeChat, among others, according to the report. Translation: a firm that makes batteries for cars and a firm that makes a messaging app both concluded that the most sensible place for large sums of money is a language model lab. This is the logic of the current AI market, in which everybody is adjacent to AI and nobody wants to be left out of the round.

The new money follows a June round in which DeepSeek raised more than $7 billion at a $50 billion valuation. The company was also reportedly seeking another $1.5 billion at a valuation near $71 billion in July, with a possible IPO in China as early as next year. Translation: an IPO is when a private company lets the public buy in, usually just after private investors have decided the price is high enough to be worth selling into.

According to the report, the startup is ramping up spending on AI infrastructure and on open models for both domestic and international markets, even as it faces restrictions in the U.S. The expansion could intensify competition among Chinese rivals. Those rivals are not short of funding either: Moonshot AI, developer of Kimi, secured $2 billion at a $20 billion valuation in May, and Manus AI raised more than $500 million earlier this month.

The public markets are in on it as well. Zhipu AI and MiniMax, which both listed in Hong Kong in January, have market capitalizations of roughly $29 billion and $7.2 billion respectively, TechCrunch said. That means the sector now has a price tag visible to anyone with a brokerage account, which is generally when sober assessments of value begin.

DeepSeek was founded in 2023 by Liang Wenfeng, who previously co-founded the quantitative hedge fund High-Flyer, which helped finance the lab's early development. Translation: a firm that trades on math paid for a firm that sells math. Liang still holds an estimated 81.6% of the Chinese operating entity, according to ownership data analyzed by WireScreen, so he is raising billions without having sold much of the company.

DeepSeek did not immediately respond to TechCrunch's request for comment. Its position may be summed up by the figures themselves. Washington's message was that the technology is a security risk. Investors' message was a 100 billion yuan cheque. Which signal prevails will depend on whether the next restriction costs more than the next round raises.
