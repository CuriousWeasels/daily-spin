Fed Chair Declines to Say What Fed Will Do, Calls This Progress

Kevin Warsh says he no longer previews rate moves, even as inflation hits 3.7% and consumers start treating high prices as permanent.

The wire said: Fed's Warsh withholds rate signal, praises recent global tightening · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 16, 2026 15:38 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The Fed raised rates to 3.75%-4% last month, its first hike since 2023, as energy costs tied to the Iran war and AI spending push prices up. U.S. CPI has now run above 2% for roughly 65 straight months.

Why it matters

If the Fed pauses in October and hikes in December, as Goldman and futures traders expect, borrowing costs stay high while wages struggle to catch up with prices. The bigger risk is that people stop expecting inflation to end, which makes it harder to end.

Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh declined on Friday to say whether the central bank will raise interest rates again this month. Speaking in Bangkok at the IMF-World Bank meetings, he said he does not prejudge meetings and does not believe in saying now what he might do later. Markets, which had hoped for a hint, received a thorough explanation of why they would not get one.

Warsh was blunt about the change. "It's a new chapter at the Fed [and] we're not in that business anymore," he said, referring to guiding markets in advance. Translation: forward guidance is when a central bank tells investors what it plans to do so nobody is surprised. Warsh thinks investors should read the data themselves, which is a bit like a teacher announcing that the exam date is no longer discussed in class.

Investors read the silence anyway, because that is what investors do. Goldman Sachs economists led by Jan Hatzius wrote that Warsh's remarks and other recent Fed comments suggest a pause in October, followed by a second and final hike in December. CME FedWatch put the odds of no change at the Oct. 27-28 meeting at 72.3%. So the Fed declined to guide the market, and the market guided itself to the same place.

Warsh also praised what he called a "synchronous tightening" by central banks worldwide, meaning most of them are raising rates at once. Translation: nobody gets to keep borrowing costs low while everyone else fights inflation, hence his remark that there are "no free riders." The backdrop is surging energy costs from the Iran war and the AI investment boom, with global public debt on pace to top 100% of world GDP, a level last seen after World War II. Brent crude was at $126.55 on Oct. 14, and the 10-year Treasury yield was 5.19%.

The U.S. government, CNBC reported, has insisted that growth is the best way out of its debt pile, and dismissed the IMF's warning that relying on growth alone will not work as something to take "with a grain of salt." Warsh was upbeat on AI, saying it should make economies that use it well richer, though the road may be "very bumpy" and its effect on jobs is uncertain. He offered "optimism leavened by the seriousness of purpose," a phrase that is hard to put in a retirement account.

Meanwhile, the people paying the prices are not in a fireside chat. September CPI rose 3.7% from a year earlier, and CNBC calculated that annual readings have stayed above 2% for about 65 months, the longest streak of the century. The New York Fed's survey shows one-year inflation expectations rose to 3.9%, the highest in more than three years. Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack warned that consumers are beginning to accept high inflation as "a fact of life."

That matters because of how inflation works. If businesses expect higher costs, they raise prices early, and if workers expect it, they demand bigger raises, which pushes costs up further. Rate hikes are the Fed's tool for breaking that loop, and they work less well once everyone has priced in the loop. Economists told CNBC the realistic goal is not lower prices, since deflation brings its own trouble, but wage growth that finally outruns inflation.

Until then, shoppers are adjusting. One Long Island lawmaker told CNBC she has cut restaurant meals and her friends have swapped bowling for nights at home. With midterms next month, the Fed's refusal to forecast itself may be the only prediction in Washington not on the ballot. Watch December, which everyone already seems to know about.
