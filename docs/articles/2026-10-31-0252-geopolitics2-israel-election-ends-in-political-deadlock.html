Israel Votes, Counts, and Learns Nobody Has 61 Seats

With Netanyahu's bloc ahead but short of a majority, Israel faces long coalition talks while Germany, among others, waits and says as little as possible.

The wire said: Israel is in a political deadlock. What comes next? · NPR World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 31, 2026 02:52 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Israel held a parliamentary election this week, and with roughly 90 percent of votes counted, neither Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's right-wing bloc nor the opposition bloc led by former military chief Gadi Eisenkot has a clear path to a majority in the Knesset. Netanyahu's government stays in office on an interim basis while the bargaining begins.

Why it matters

Until a government forms, decisions on Gaza aid and the West Bank may stall, and foreign capitals are holding back on policy. The Arab-majority parties' strong showing could make them decisive, which is a problem for a bloc that has ruled out working with them.

Israel's election has ended in deadlock, according to NPR, with neither side able to claim a majority. Even with the right-wing bloc led by Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu ahead of the opposition bloc led by Gadi Eisenkot, DW reported that neither has a clear route to a governing majority in the Knesset. Translation: being ahead is not the same as being able to govern, a distinction that matters a great deal in parliamentary systems and not at all on election night television.

The practical result is coalition building, a process in which the winner is whoever can persuade enough smaller parties to agree on everything at once. DW describes this as likely to be extremely difficult. In the meantime, Netanyahu's government remains in office on an interim basis, which means the old government keeps its job precisely because the voters did not clearly hire a new one.

The arithmetic is complicated by the Arab-majority parties, led by Palestinian citizens of Israel, which made large gains, DW reported. They are usually counted in the opposition camp, and the results could make them kingmakers. Translation: kingmaker means the party whose votes the others need but would prefer not to be seen needing. Netanyahu's bloc has ruled out working with them.

Eisenkot's bloc has not said whether it would do so. One of the parties, United Arab List/Ra'am, which featured a Jewish candidate, has said it is willing to work with Eisenkot's bloc to unseat Netanyahu. Jürgen Hardt, a foreign policy expert for Germany's CDU, told DW that a minority government led by Eisenkot and backed by parties representing Arab Israelis is conceivable. A minority government is one that governs without a majority and relies on others not to vote it out, which is a stable arrangement until it is not.

Hardt also offered an assessment of how the situation arose: Netanyahu's campaign, aimed primarily against Arab Israelis, greatly mobilized the opposition, to the point where his camp no longer has a majority. In other words, the strategy worked on voters, just not the ones intended.

Berlin, meanwhile, has chosen the diplomatic art of saying something without saying it. A spokesperson for Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul declined to comment on the results, though Wadephul said Germany would do everything it can to work well with any future government. Germany has also been distancing itself from Netanyahu's policies in its public rhetoric, DW reported, and does not intend to recognize a Palestinian state for now. The CDU's hope for a more centrist government, without figures such as Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich, is expressed politely and as a hope, not a request. Green politician Omid Nouripour called the outcome good news for Israeli democracy.

The deadline that coalition math does not respect is humanitarian. Development Minister Reem Alabali Radovan told Politico that winter in Gaza will be difficult and that aid cannot wait for coalition negotiations. She also urged an end to radical settler violence, noting that the interim Netanyahu government is still in office. Settlement activity in the occupied Palestinian territories is considered illegal under international law.

Next comes the traditional phase of Israeli politics in which everyone negotiates, nobody concedes, and the calendar keeps moving. Watch whether the Arab-majority parties are courted, ignored, or both.
