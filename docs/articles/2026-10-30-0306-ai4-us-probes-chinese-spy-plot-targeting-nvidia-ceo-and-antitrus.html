Nvidia Chief Dined With Suspected Spy as Washington Watched, Said Nothing, Kept Selling Chips

The New York Times reports US agencies believe a Megaspeed executive close to Jensen Huang informed for Beijing, while antitrust regulators separately examine Nvidia's $12.93 billion Hugging Face deal.

The wire said: Chinese spies targeted Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, according to The New York Times · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 30, 2026 03:06 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The New York Times reported that US intelligence agencies suspect Alice Huang (Huang Le), an executive at Singapore data center operator Megaspeed, of passing information to China while close to Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang. Megaspeed was already under Commerce Department scrutiny over claims it helped Chinese firms get restricted AI chips.

Why it matters

The story shows how export controls on AI chips depend on resellers and relationships, not just rules on paper. It also lands as Nvidia faces antitrust questions in the US and France, which affect who controls the supply and software that AI runs on.

US intelligence agencies believe an executive in Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's circle is an informant for China, according to The New York Times. The executive is Alice Huang, also known as Huang Le, of Megaspeed, a Singapore-based data center operator. The report says US authorities, while monitoring her, concluded she passes information to Beijing and is close to Huang. It is rare for a dinner invitation to become an intelligence matter, but then it is rare for the dinner guest to sell data center capacity built on restricted chips.

The Times reported that President Trump was briefed about a year ago and did nothing, likely to avoid disrupting Nvidia's chip sales or further straining trade relations with China, according to The Decoder's summary. Jensen Huang himself was not warned, because officials believed at the time that he had no idea. Translation: the government watched a possible spy sit near the head of the world's most important chip company, and judged that the quarterly sales figures were more fragile than the secret.

TechCrunch, also citing the Times, adds that Alice Huang was photographed among several people at various business dinners with Huang in the Singapore region. The worry, according to sources, was that she could be gathering information on Nvidia's plans and on the CEO's efforts to persuade the Trump administration to let it ship more advanced technology to China. That is a fairly specific thing for a rival government to want to know, and a fairly ordinary thing for a salesperson to discuss over dinner.

Nvidia rejects the framing. A spokesperson told TechCrunch that Huang's interactions with her are 'not unusual or unexpected,' that meetings with customers are part of a CEO's job, and that he talks with thousands of customer executives every year. The company also said regional cloud customers receive ordinary business information, not trade secrets, and 'zero information relevant to national security.' The Chinese embassy called the allegations 'groundless smears and slanders,' and Megaspeed has announced an internal investigation, which is the corporate equivalent of asking the suspect to check the locks.

The money matters here. The US government suspects Megaspeed of helping China obtain Nvidia chips, and according to The Decoder, a Malaysian sister company bought at least $4.8 billion of AI hardware containing Nvidia chips between June 2024 and February 2026. TechCrunch notes the Commerce Department investigated Megaspeed in 2025 over concerns it helped Chinese companies sidestep export rules by reselling restricted chips. Export controls, in practice, are only as strong as the least curious distributor.

Nvidia's week gets worse on a second front. Reuters, as summarized by The Decoder, reports that the Justice Department is looking at Nvidia's planned $12.93 billion acquisition of open-source platform Hugging Face, asking whether it could give Nvidia control over a key hub for distributing AI and hurt competitors. Nvidia expects the deal to close in the first half of 2027 and says Hugging Face will keep supporting partnerships with a range of chip, cloud, and AI providers.

The broader antitrust probe began under Biden and has continued under Trump, a rare point of bipartisan agreement. Officials have also reportedly examined Nvidia's acquisitions of SchedMD and Run:ai, its licensing deal with Groq, and whether it offered customers more AI chips if they also bought its networking gear and Groq processors. Translation: investigators want to know if getting scarce chips came with a mandatory shopping list. France's competition authority is separately investigating, citing industry dependence on Nvidia's CUDA software and its stakes in cloud providers such as CoreWeave.

What to watch: whether Washington's quiet year of surveillance turns into any visible action, and whether regulators treat the Hugging Face deal as a software purchase or as the purchase of the road everyone else drives on.
