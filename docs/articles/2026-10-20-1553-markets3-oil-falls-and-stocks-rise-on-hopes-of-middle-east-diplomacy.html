Oil Falls on Peace Hopes While Tankers Keep Catching Fire in Hormuz

Crude retreated from $110 intraday highs on diplomacy hopes, though at least 18 ships have been fired on in two weeks and the November contract expires Tuesday.

The wire said: Oil prices fall as traders hope for diplomatic progress in Middle East · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 20, 2026 15:53 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Attacks on commercial ships in the Strait of Hormuz have escalated in recent weeks, alongside strikes on Saudi oil infrastructure, as tensions between the U.S. and Iran persist. Traders are now weighing the chance of diplomatic progress against a steady supply of bad news.

Why it matters

With WTI above $100 and Brent above $108, fuel and shipping costs stay elevated, which feeds directly into inflation that the latest data put at 3.74% year over year. A real peace deal would ease that pressure; another burning tanker would not.

Crude oil fell on Tuesday as traders bet on diplomatic progress between the United States and Iran, CNBC reported. U.S. West Texas Intermediate futures for November slipped 0.9% to $102.75 a barrel, well below a session high of $110.48. Brent, the global benchmark, fell 1% to $108.31 after touching $111.99. The market, in other words, found a reason for optimism in a week when optimism was not obviously supplied by events.

The events in question: Kpler, a ship tracker, counts at least 18 vessels fired on in and around the Strait of Hormuz over the past two weeks. Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps said two "non-compliant" oil tankers were set ablaze after hitting mines laid by its naval unit, according to the Iranian state-affiliated Tasnim news agency. Translation: the party that laid the mines is describing the ships that hit them as the ones at fault.

Diplomacy, meanwhile, is mostly rhetorical. Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmaeil Baqaei said Tehran would respond to any American strike "in equal measure, if not more forcefully," per Tasnim. CNBC noted that President Donald Trump said earlier this month that U.S. forces will not attack Iran before the Nov. 3 midterm elections. Investing.com separately reported an unconfirmed Qatar-Pakistan peace proposal that lifted stocks and weighed on oil. The word doing the heavy lifting there is unconfirmed.

Underneath the headlines, the physical market looks tight. Tamas Varga of PVM Oil Associates told CNBC that strength in near-term WTI ahead of contract expiry reflects falling Middle East export volumes as tanker attacks and Houthi strikes on Saudi oil infrastructure intensify. Translation: when the barrels you normally buy are on fire or stuck behind mines, the barrels you can actually get become more valuable, and the contract about to expire is the one that has to deliver them.

Varga also pointed to the U.S. decision to exempt the Russian shadow fleet from sanctions. The shadow fleet is the loose network of older tankers that moves Russian oil outside normal Western oversight. He said the exemption should push freight rates lower, which removes an obstacle to buying U.S. crude, so American grades are back in play as replacements for Middle Eastern supply. He added that U.S. commercial stocks were expected to have declined last week. Sanctions policy, it turns out, is also a shipping-cost policy.

The broader backdrop is not gentle. The latest data in the sources show the 10-year Treasury yield at 5.09%, the Fed funds rate at 3.88%, and CPI inflation at 3.74% year over year. Brent was quoted at $126.55 as of Oct. 14, so Tuesday's $108 is a decline only in the sense that a smaller bill is still a bill. The sources do not reconcile every price point, and the Investing.com report gave no confirmation of its peace proposal.

What to watch: the November WTI contract expires Tuesday, and expiry days tend to exaggerate whatever the market is already worried about. After that, the question is whether anyone confirms the Qatar-Pakistan proposal, or whether the next mine does the talking.
