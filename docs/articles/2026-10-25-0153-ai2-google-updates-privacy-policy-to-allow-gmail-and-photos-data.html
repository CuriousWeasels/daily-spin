Google Amends Promise Not to Use Your Gmail for Ads, Adds the Word 'Unless'

A privacy policy update dated October 16 lets data from Gmail, Drive and Photos feed ad personalization if users connect it to Google's AI features.

The wire said: Now Google says it might use your Gmail and Google Photos data to target ads · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 25, 2026 01:53 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Google's privacy policy used to say it did not show personalized ads based on content from Drive, Gmail or Photos. Android Authority spotted an October 16 update that adds an exception for users who make that data available to other Google services.

Why it matters

Anyone who has linked their email or photo library to Google's AI tools to get better answers may now also get better-targeted ads. The setting that controls this is opt-in, but the opt-in now has a second purpose.

Google has changed its privacy policy so that its longstanding pledge not to use Gmail, Drive and Google Photos for personalized ads now has an exception, according to The Verge and Ars Technica. The old text said Google does not show personalized ads based on content from those products. The new text says the same thing, "unless you choose to make data from these services available to other Google services that support personalized ads." It is the privacy-policy equivalent of a sign reading "No Parking" with a smaller sign beneath it.

The mechanism is a feature called Personal Intelligence, which Ars Technica says Google released in early 2026. Translation: it lets you hand your email, files and photos to Google's AI tools so they can give answers that sound like they know you. They do, which was the pitch. It connects to the Gemini app and to AI Mode in Search.

Here is where the exception earns its keep. Ars Technica notes that the Gemini app does not currently show ads, but AI search tools can carry sponsored content. So if your Gmail informs an AI Mode answer, and AI Mode is a place where ads live, the data is now part of the ad system. Google's policy simply says so, which is a form of progress.

Google disputes the framing. In a statement quoted by Ars Technica, the company said connecting apps is "entirely opt-in," that ads will stay relevant to your query, the context of the response and your interests, and that they "will not be based directly on your Gmail inbox or Photos library." Ars Technica described this as a roundabout way of confirming that Workspace and Photos data may inform ads. The operative word is "directly," which is doing the work of a load-bearing wall.

Some limits remain. The Verge reports that advertisers do not get access to your Drive, Gmail or Photos information, nor identifying details such as your name or email address. Google also says it will not tailor ads using sensitive categories such as race, sexual orientation, religion or health.

The policy's own example is mild: search for mountain bikes and you may see sports equipment ads on YouTube. That is ordinary ad targeting. The new part is that the inputs can include whatever you let the AI read, which may be a larger and more candid archive than a search history.

Users who would rather not participate have options. According to The Verge, you can disconnect individual apps from Search's Personal Intelligence features, or turn off Personalized Recommendations entirely through the Search Services Personalization settings page.

The arrangement is consistent with Google's business model, in which the product is free and the user is the inventory. The company did not break its promise so much as edit it until it was easier to keep. Watch for how many people read the settings page, and how many read the policy that explains why it exists.
