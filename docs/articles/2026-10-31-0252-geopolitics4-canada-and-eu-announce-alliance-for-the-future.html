Canada and EU Form 'Alliance for the Future,' Define Membership Status as 'Later'

Carney and von der Leyen announce a partnership open to like-minded countries, a Ukraine loan contribution and AI deals, while the 'associate membership' they keep naming still has no legal meaning.

The wire said: Canada and EU announce new alliance open to like-minded countries · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 31, 2026 02:52 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Canada has been looking for new partners amid a trade war with the United States and worry about relying on American firms, especially for defence technology. EU and Canadian leaders met in Montreal on Friday to deepen ties.

Why it matters

Canada is trying to cut its dependence on its largest trading partner while avoiding a fight with it, and the EU is testing a status that does not yet exist. Supply chains, AI access and defence technology are the practical stakes.

Canada and the European Union on Friday announced the Alliance for the Future, a partnership of like-minded countries that other nations may join, according to The Guardian. Prime Minister Mark Carney and European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen presented it in Montreal at the end of a two-day summit. It is described as a "protective canopy", which is a roof, but for geopolitics.

The concrete items were more modest than the canopy. Canada agreed to contribute to a €90bn support loan for Ukraine, and leaders said future talks would produce a more integrated market worth tens of billions of dollars, fewer trade and travel restrictions, and new educational opportunities. Brussels and Ottawa also agreed to bring Canada into Europe's supercomputing network, giving Canadian researchers access to European machines for training AI models. Translation: Canada rents computing power from friends rather than from the American companies it is trying to depend on less.

The motive is not subtle. A trade war with the United States has sent Carney looking for new partners, and The Guardian reported that reliance on American companies, especially in defence, has given Ottawa pause. It cited a report that Elon Musk throttled Starlink and Starshield speeds over Ukraine in mid-October, disrupting Ukrainian military operations. Carney has argued that defence technology could end up at the whim of political actors or companies with opposing interests. Carney also pointed to a planned merger between Canada's Cohere and Germany's Aleph Alpha, with headquarters in Berlin and Toronto and a Canadian federal stake of up to C$1.6bn.

Then there is "associate membership." Von der Leyen first floated the term and called it uncharted territory. DW reported that no other country holds the status and that Chancellor Friedrich Merz's government resists the label. The Guardian reported that Germany and others worked behind the scenes to remove the phrase from a draft statement, since some members fear a legally meaningless designation could confuse countries that actually want to join. Both leaders used it anyway, and a clearer definition is promised at a 2027 summit. Translation: the title has been awarded, and the job description is coming.

Carney told DW the term means "the closest possible partnership" short of full membership, since Canada is not in Europe and cannot accede. European Council president Antonio Costa called Canada "by far the most European of the non-European countries," a sentence that is both a compliment and a geography lesson.

Carney was careful to say the arrangement is not aimed at Washington. He told DW it is about diversifying partnerships, that the two sides are both close partners of the United States, and that the pact is "not against somebody. It's for." He also conceded that the scale of the US relationship would be "very hard to replace." Asked about the risk of further tariffs, he said Canada has been living with that for some time. This is the diplomatic version of building a second front door while insisting the first is still very much in use.

At home, the summit produced its own friction. Quebec's premier, Paul St-Pierre Plamondon of the separatist Parti Québécois, skipped it after Ottawa declined to let him speak publicly. He wrote that being allowed to serve shrimp cocktails at a reception was not adequate influence for Quebec. Canada, in short, is courting a bloc of 27 members while negotiating seating with one province.

What to watch: the 2027 summit, where the leaders are expected to explain what associate membership is. Until then, Canada holds a status that every participant can pronounce and none can define.
