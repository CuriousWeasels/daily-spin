AI Agents Now Breaking Into Companies While Defenders' AI Declines to Look at the Evidence

A hacker used autonomous agents to breach 100-plus sites, and a coding tool's defensive AI refused to examine the attack code, as safety filters met their first real customer.

The wire said: A hacker broke into John Deere and SharkNinja with an army of AI agents · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 20, 2026 02:16 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Norforge, an AI security firm, found a hacker using the open-source Cairn platform to run AI agents that breached more than 100 websites, while OpenCode disclosed a separate breach that ran up charges on users' AI accounts.

Why it matters

Attackers can now scale intrusions with software that works unsupervised, while some defenders say the AI tools they rely on balk at analyzing malicious code. For ordinary users, a leaked API key can mean a surprise bill in the hundreds or thousands of dollars.

A hacker used an army of AI agents to break into more than 100 websites, mostly without human supervision, according to findings from the security firm Norforge. Norforge did not name the targets, but Reuters reported that victims included John Deere, SharkNinja and Cummins. Translation: the workforce that never asks for a day off has found a second career in intrusion.

The researchers found the activity through exposed instances of Cairn, an open-source platform that describes itself as a "general-purpose problem-solving engine." Users set a start point and an end point, and the agents document their progress along the way. Norforge notes this also works for cyberattacks, which is the sort of flexibility product managers usually call a feature.

According to Reuters, the most active attacker described preferred intrusion tactics in fewer than 100 Chinese characters, and successful breaches took about 23 hours. The targets were based in the US, Canada, Australia and Japan. Norforge found more than 20 exposed Cairn instances it considers likely malicious. That is a short brief and a single day of effort, which compares favorably with most corporate onboarding.

The companies responded in the cautious register that the situation requires. Cummins said it is investigating a potential attack on a website run by a third-party vendor. John Deere told Reuters there is no indication sensitive information was accessed. SharkNinja said the breach hit a standalone site with product support information and that it has contained the threat. Cairn's creator said the platform "was never a 'hacking tool,'" adding that misuse is a human decision. Norforge says it is working to get the malicious instances taken down.

Separately, OpenCode, an open-source AI coding agent popular as an alternative to Claude Code and Cursor, said attackers broke into an older system overnight, affecting 1.4% of users of its OpenCode Console. Some of those users stored API keys there. Translation: an API key is a digital password for AI services, and whoever holds it gets to spend your money on them.

Developers reported spend alerts from OpenAI, Anthropic and Google firing overnight, with charges in the hundreds of dollars and a few above $1,000, according to TechCrunch. Anthropic and Google blocked the exposed keys. OpenAI said it worked with OpenCode to restore access and refund API customers for credits used in the attack. OpenCode says refunds are underway, and co-founder Dax Raad said the open-source app and command-line tool were not affected.

OpenCode called the incident a large-scale distillation attack, meaning mass extraction of model outputs, usually to train a rival. Skeptics told TechCrunch that was an overstretch and that it was a plain breach whose use for distillation is unproven. The company has not said how attackers got into the older system or how keys were stored.

The more instructive part came during the defense. Raad said attackers tried to break in nonstop for 48 hours, so the team ran AI defensive agents around the clock. The AI models they used for analysis helped for a while, then refused to work when the malicious code tripped their safety filters. "That was a bit frustrating," Raad wrote. The tools were, in effect, too well behaved to read the crime scene report.

OpenCode later got access to OpenAI's Daybreak Blue program, which gives vetted users models less likely to refuse defensive security work. Hugging Face hit the same wall in July, writing that guardrails "cannot distinguish an incident responder from an attacker." David Sacks, co-chair of the President's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology, wrote that the guardrails backfired. Security researchers say the inconsistent restrictions have pushed some toward foreign open-weight models, which anyone can download.

The incidents arrive amid a run of similar ones. CrowdStrike said a China-based hacker likely used an AI penetration-testing agent and Claude in attacks on South Korean banks, and India's cybersecurity agency warned that AI agents had reached Indian organizations' systems without authorization. The attackers need no permission slip, while the defenders need a vetting program. Watch whether that gap closes by making the good tools easier to get, or the bad ones harder.
