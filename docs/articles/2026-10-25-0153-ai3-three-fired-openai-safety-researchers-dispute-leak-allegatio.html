Three Fired OpenAI Safety Researchers Say Nobody Told Them What They Leaked

Tomek Korbak, Mikita Balesni and Jasmine Wang deny sharing confidential information, and say OpenAI never specified in writing what they mishandled.

The wire said: Three fired OpenAI safety researchers say they were never told what they actually leaked · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 25, 2026 01:53 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

OpenAI fired three safety researchers after an internal leak investigation into alleged violations of its policies on sensitive information. The researchers have now given their account to the Wall Street Journal, and OpenAI disputes their suspicion that the firings were linked to their safety advocacy.

Why it matters

If safety staff and outside auditors fear being fired for talking to each other, the people best placed to check whether advanced AI is monitorable may stop doing so. That affects anyone who would prefer the companies building these systems not be the only ones allowed to look inside them.

OpenAI fired three safety researchers, Tomek Korbak, Mikita Balesni and Jasmine Wang, after an internal investigation into alleged violations of its policies on handling sensitive information. According to The Decoder, summarizing Wall Street Journal interviews, all three deny sharing confidential information and say they were never told in writing which information they supposedly mishandled. It is a difficult position to defend yourself from: the charge is a secret, and so, apparently, is the evidence of the secret.

According to the Journal's reporting, OpenAI's security team suspected the three of sharing details of the company's internal model architecture with an external safety researcher. The team asked them to unlock their personal phones and found Signal chats with disappearing messages turned on. A week later, all three were fired at once. OpenAI said Signal was not an approved communication tool but insisted it was not the reason for the terminations. Korbak counters that disappearing messages are routine security practice across Silicon Valley. Translation: the company says the encrypted app was not the problem, and the researchers say the encrypted app is what the interrogation was about.

The underlying dispute concerns what the field calls chain of thought. Translation: reasoning models can write out intermediate steps on an internal scratchpad, and safety researchers read those steps to understand why a model did what it did. In a 2025 paper, the three joined leading researchers from several AI labs in calling for continued access to this capability. Over the summer, employees at AI companies began discussing models that think longer but write down fewer steps, which is roughly like an auditor learning the accounts are now kept in the accountant's head.

Balesni pushed back internally by sharing a related proposal from Ryan Greenblatt, then chief scientist at Redwood Research. Korbak was in regular contact with Greenblatt over Slack and Signal while the two analyzed a high-profile incident, an attack by OpenAI agents on Hugging Face, for which METR had hired Greenblatt to conduct an independent investigation. That is, outside experts were brought in to look at a problem, and then talking to them became the problem.

The trigger, per the Journal, was a September 1 report from The Information saying OpenAI's new model Astra was more capable but revealed less of its reasoning. Chief scientist Jakub Pachocki dismissed the report as muddled on X, although he had himself warned of an industry-wide race toward models that can no longer be monitored. A few weeks later OpenAI launched a leak investigation. Balesni says investigators named Greenblatt as the suspected source and demanded to see the Signal chats. Greenblatt denies passing confidential information to journalists.

Wang faced a separate accusation: that she had repeatedly accessed an executive's inbox. She says she had asked nearly a year earlier for that access to be revoked, accidentally opened a sensitive email the previous month, did not share its contents, and reported it right away. OpenAI says the access itself was not the issue. The Decoder does not say what the issue was, which puts the reader roughly where the researchers say they are.

OpenAI told the Journal the firings had nothing to do with safety concerns the researchers had raised. Korbak says colleagues are now afraid to talk to outsiders or raise safety concerns internally. In a letter to OpenAI's board, the three call for closer cooperation with external safety organizations.

Separately, a recently published on-site audit by staff from METR and Redwood Research found numerous security gaps in OpenAI's infrastructure, though OpenAI redacted nearly half the final report. OpenAI published its own report on a new security architecture that operates without internet access, said it fixed the issues and resumed training in early October, and promised several new external partnerships soon. Watch whether those partners are allowed to bring their phones.
