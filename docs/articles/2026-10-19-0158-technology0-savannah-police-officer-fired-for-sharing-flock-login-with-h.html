Savannah Officer Fired After Sharing Police Camera Login With ICE Agent Husband

Tyler Harpe ran 983 searches on 97 license plates through a system his wife's department says it never shares with ICE, according to local reports.

The wire said: A Savannah police officer gave her Flock login to her ICE agent husband · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 19, 2026 01:58 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Flock is a network of license plate readers that police departments use to track vehicles. The Savannah Police Department fired officer Amber Harpe in August after an internal investigation found she had given her login to her husband, an ICE agent.

Why it matters

Surveillance systems are only as restricted as the people holding the passwords, and a data-sharing ban means little if a login can be passed across the dinner table. Drivers have no way of knowing who is actually looking at where their cars have been.

The Verge reported that Savannah police officer Amber Harpe was fired in August after a department investigation found she had shared her Flock login with her husband, Tyler Harpe, an ICE agent. The Savannah Police Department says it does not share Flock data with ICE. It also says that handing out access violated its security protocols, which is the sort of thing a protocol exists to say.

Translation: Flock operates cameras that read license plates and log where and when vehicles pass. A department can search that record to find a car. The department had decided its record would not go to ICE, and the household apparently treated that decision as a suggestion about which spouse should hold the password.

According to Savannah's ABC affiliate WJCL, as cited by The Verge, Tyler Harpe ran 983 searches tied to 97 license plates while he had unauthorized access. The plates were overwhelmingly registered to Hispanic drivers. That works out to roughly ten searches per plate, which is a lot of interest in a small number of cars.

The Harpes defended themselves, saying they did not use the access for personal reasons and that the searches were strictly for law-enforcement purposes. Translation: nobody was stalking an ex, which is the bar this system's defenders tend to set. It does not address the question of who authorized the law enforcement, because the department says no one did.

The discipline process was not unanimous. Interim Major Max Nowinsky originally recommended an eight-hour suspension, roughly one shift, for Amber Harpe. Major Michelle Halford and Assistant Police Chief Robert Gavin disagreed and removed her from the force.

She was one of six SPD employees fired after the internal investigation uncovered Flock misuse. Four of them were later arrested. Harpe was not among them, so her outcome is a firing without an arrest, which is the middle tier of consequences for improperly sharing a database.

The practical lesson is that a policy declaring data will not be shared with a federal agency is enforced by passwords, and passwords are enforced by the honor system. The department caught this one through an internal investigation. How many similar cases exist elsewhere is not something the sources address, and it is not something a license plate reader can tell you.
