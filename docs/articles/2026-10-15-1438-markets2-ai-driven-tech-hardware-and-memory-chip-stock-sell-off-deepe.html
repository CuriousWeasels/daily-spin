AI Stocks Lose $1 Trillion in Four Days, Blame Interest Rates, China, and 2029

Twenty of the world's most valuable AI and chip stocks shed more than $1 trillion this week as borrowing costs rose and memory makers took the worst of it.

The wire said: Nvidia, AMD, Micron fall as AI stock sell-off deepens · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 15, 2026 14:38 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Chip and AI stocks had rallied for months on surging demand for data center hardware, with memory makers enjoying unprecedented price increases. This week, hot U.S. inflation data, rising Treasury yields and a Chinese memory announcement turned the mood sour.

Why it matters

The AI buildout is financed largely with borrowed money, so higher yields make it more expensive, and the stocks feeding it are a big part of many retirement portfolios. Whether the sell-off is a buying opportunity or a warning depends on whether demand actually weakens, and so far the evidence cited is mostly about financing.

Twenty of the most valuable AI and semiconductor stocks around the world have lost more than $1 trillion in combined value so far this week, CNBC reported. On Thursday Nvidia fell about 2.5%, AMD lost 6%, and Micron sank about 3.5%. A trillion dollars is the sort of sum that usually requires a war or a decade, and the market managed it between Monday and Thursday.

The sell-off spread well beyond Wall Street. SK Hynix dropped more than 10% in Seoul, Samsung Electronics fell 8.73%, and in Tokyo Advantest plunged almost 14%. In Europe, ASML was down 5.2% and ASM International 8.6%. Taiwan's TSMC, the world's largest contract chipmaker, fell a comparatively polite 1.81%.

The first culprit is the cost of money. Wednesday's U.S. inflation data came in hotter than expected, and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 1.88% to 26,692.87 while the S&P 500 shed 1.11% to 7,718.94. Official data show the 10-year Treasury yield at 5.21% and inflation at 3.74% a year. Translation: the government now pays investors a solid 5% to lend it money, which makes a risky bet on data centers have to try harder.

That matters because, as CNBC reported, the chief executives of Broadcom and Intel warned on Monday that financing could become a major headwind for AI. Broadcom's Hock Tan said it could be a bigger challenge than shortages of chips or electricity. Building AI infrastructure is expensive, and expensive things get more so when borrowed at higher rates. MarketWatch added that reported details about Anthropic's financial progress are feeding worries about the health of the AI ecosystem, though its excerpt gave no specifics.

Memory chips took the worst of it. Over five days, SK Hynix plunged 26%, Samsung tanked 22% and Micron dropped around 11%. The trigger was ChangXin Memory Technologies, a Chinese chipmaker, announcing a server memory product on a new architecture that could pack in more data. Investors concluded that Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron might lose market share, which is a lot to conclude from an announcement.

Analysts were less alarmed. Ben Barringer of Quilter Cheviot noted that if yields are only 50%, you are literally putting half the chips in the bin. Translation: making a chip once is not the same as making millions profitably. Kevin Cassidy of Rosenblatt said of CXMT's progress: "We don't think this is going to swing the supply-demand equation at all."

Then came 2029. A South Korean newspaper column cited two experts expecting a "flood of new supply" that would turn the memory market into a red ocean that year, and Elon Musk replied on X that China would be making "a LOT of RAM in 2028." CNBC noted that no other analysts have echoed a forecast that far out. The market responded to a prediction about 2029 by selling in 2026, which is either foresight or enthusiasm for round numbers.

There is a more sober case. Memory has historically boomed when demand outran supply and busted when factories caught up, and factories take three to five years to build. Counterpoint Research sees DRAM prices falling about 23% in 2028 from 2027, yet still roughly 230% above 2025. It also points to three- to five-year long-term agreements with upfront payments and price floors and ceilings, which should soften any downturn. A bust where prices are still more than three times higher is a bust few industries would complain about.

Dan Ives of Yorkville Ives called the pullback a buying opportunity, saying "rates are a financing question, not a demand question." He said he would worry about cuts to hyperscaler spending, canceled contracts or falling GPU rental prices. None of those has been reported yet. For now the market is panicking about the price of the loan rather than the thing it buys, which is at least a specific kind of panic.
