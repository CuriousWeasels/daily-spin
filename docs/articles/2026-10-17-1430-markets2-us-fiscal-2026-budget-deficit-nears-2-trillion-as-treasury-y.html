U.S. Posts Nearly $2 Trillion Deficit, Its 25th Consecutive Year of Not Balancing the Checkbook

The fiscal 2026 shortfall of $1.992 trillion is up 12% in a year, with interest alone now topping $1 trillion and the 10-year Treasury yielding 5.1%.

The wire said: U.S. budget deficit swells to almost $2 trillion for fiscal 2026, up 12% in a single year · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 17, 2026 14:30 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The Treasury reported Friday that the government ran a $1.992 trillion deficit in fiscal 2026, up from $1.775 trillion the year before. Long-term bond yields have climbed to levels not seen in 24 years, partly because of the war with Iran, which began Feb. 28, and heavy government borrowing.

Why it matters

Treasury yields set the price of mortgages and other long-term loans, so a government that borrows heavily makes borrowing more expensive for everyone else. With divided government expected after the midterms, relief through policy looks distant.

The United States ended fiscal 2026 with a budget deficit of $1.992 trillion, the Treasury Department reported Friday, about 12% above last year's $1.775 trillion. According to CNBC, it is the third-largest deficit on record, behind only 2020 and 2021, when the government was paying for pandemic stimulus. This year had no such excuse on offer, though receipts and outlays both set records, which is one way of describing a household that earned a record amount and still overspent it.

It is also the 25th consecutive year in the red. The last surplus came in 2001. Translation: a surplus means taking in more than you spend, a feat the government has not attempted successfully since before smartphones.

The largest spending items were Social Security at nearly $1.7 trillion and Medicare at almost $1.1 trillion. Net interest, meaning what the Treasury pays on its debt minus what it receives, topped $1 trillion, up from $970 billion. CNBC separately put gross interest on the national debt at more than $1.3 trillion. The debt itself passed $40 trillion this year and has more than doubled in a decade. Interest is now a line item large enough to have its own lobbying problem, except that its lobbyists are bondholders and they are already being paid.

On the revenue side, individual income taxes brought in more than $2.8 trillion, up about 7%. Corporate income tax receipts fell by roughly $70 billion to $381 billion, which CNBC attributed to tax cuts in last year's One Big Beautiful Bill. Customs duties hit a record in gross terms, but refunds ordered after the Supreme Court ruled many of the tariffs unconstitutional sent billions back to retailers. The tariffs were pitched as a way to shrink the debt. The refunds are the part of that plan that actually arrived on schedule.

Bond traders have noticed. The 10-year Treasury yield stands at 5.1%, up 114 basis points since the end of February. Translation: a basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point, so the government's borrowing cost has risen by a bit over one full point in eight months. CNBC's analysis attributes this to the Iran war, higher oil and diesel prices that raise the odds of Fed rate hikes, a steady flood of Treasury issuance, and competition from companies borrowing hundreds of billions to build AI infrastructure. Brent crude sits at $126.55, which does not help the mood.

Mortgage rates follow the 10-year, and the average 30-year fixed rate is hovering around 7.5%. Redfin reported that pending home sales fell 4% in September, the biggest monthly drop in three years. So the deficit reaches ordinary people the way most fiscal abstractions do: through a monthly payment.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has said the country can "grow our way out of" the debt and has called for a deficit below 4% of GDP by the end of Trump's term. The current deficit is about 6.2% of GDP. The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget estimates that balancing the budget through growth alone would take a decade of 7.2% annual growth. Second-quarter growth was recently revised up to 2.2% annualized, a figure that is respectable and about a third of what the plan requires.

The Congressional Budget Office adds that if rates run a half-point above its forecast, the 10-year deficit total rises by $1.9 trillion; a 1.5-point overshoot adds $6 trillion. The CBO expects the 10-year yield to average 4.3%, versus 5.1% today. Politics offers little rescue. Republicans are widely forecast to lose at least the House, Democrats want to reverse $1 trillion in healthcare cuts, and the president has pledged $5,000 checks to every adult citizen if Republicans sweep the election, according to CNBC.

CNBC's analysis stresses that this is not yet a debt crisis, and that high yields are also drawing buyers to Treasuries. Expensive borrowing is not the same as an emergency, merely its rehearsal. Watch the midterms on Nov. 3 and the 10-year yield, which will say what the bond market makes of the result.
