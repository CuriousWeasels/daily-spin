Fed Holds Rates, Skips Future Votes, Rearranges Reporters Alphabetically in Reverse

The Federal Open Market Committee held its rate at 3.75% to 4% on a 10-2 vote, with two dissenters wanting a hike, while Chairman Kevin Warsh announced the Fed will vote on rates less often.

The wire said: Federal Reserve issues FOMC statement · Federal Reserve

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 30, 2026 17:17 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The Fed raised rates in September under new Chairman Kevin Warsh after holding them in June and July, while inflation stayed well above its 2% target. Markets spent October guessing whether another hike would arrive this week.

Why it matters

A Fed that votes less often and signals less clearly makes borrowing costs harder to predict for mortgage holders, businesses and investors. Two dissenters and several economists are betting the next move is up, with inflation at 3.74% by the latest reading.

The Federal Reserve voted 10-2 on Wednesday to hold its benchmark rate at a range of 3.75% to 4%. According to the Fed's statement, the committee called economic activity strong, inflation elevated, and promised to deliver price stability. It did not say when.

The two dissents are the real story of the vote. Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack both preferred a quarter-point increase. CNBC reported that Cook had never dissented on a rate vote since taking her seat in 2022, and that governors rarely do. Translation: when the people who normally agree start disagreeing, the room is paying attention.

Chairman Kevin Warsh then held a news conference that CNBC's analysis described as a break from his early, cornered-looking appearances. He said that if the Fed had a firm conclusion about policy, it would announce it with much fanfare. He also said that while financial conditions are tighter, he is unwilling and unable to call them tight. Translation: the Fed has not decided to hike, and has not decided not to, and would like you to read the sentence carefully.

The deliberate vagueness has a history. Warsh was appointed by a president who called rate cuts a litmus test for the job, according to CNBC. After holds in June and July, commentators questioned his credibility. The September hike answered them, and then markets swung so far toward another hike that two top officials appeared to talk expectations back down. Warsh declined to confirm he had directed them. Fed insiders say vice chairs usually coordinate messaging with the chair, so the denial offers what CNBC called plausible deniability.

The bigger change is procedural. The Fed will still meet eight times in 2027, but one meeting will have no rate vote and no statement. In 2028 two meetings will be so designated. Translation: the Fed is keeping the meetings and removing the part where anything happens. That leaves six scheduled chances to move rates in 2027, plus emergency meetings, which the chairman can call at any time. CNBC's analysis noted this devalues Fedspeak, since a regional president's remarks matter less when a decision is months away, and those presidents lose a scheduled vote in their rotation.

Critics see a cost. Advocates of transparency argue that sunlight forces officials to sharpen their arguments. Robert Sockin of PGIM called holding a policy mistake and said a gradual Fed likely has to hike higher later, raising recession risk. Seema Shah of Principal Asset Management expects hikes in December and early 2027, while Jefferies' Thomas Simons declined to call Warsh either dovish or hawkish.

The press room has also been reorganized. Press conferences now run about half an hour, reporters get one question rather than two, and seating moved from loosely by outlet size to alphabetical in September, then reverse alphabetical this month. CNBC noted this keeps microphone bearers on their toes. It is the one Fed policy whose direction is perfectly clear.

The data will decide the next chapter. The 2-year Treasury yield sat at 4.67% and the 10-year at 5.05% as of Wednesday, both above the Fed's own policy rate of 3.88%. Markets are effectively saying they expect higher rates to come. The committee next meets in December, with a stack of new data in hand and, for now, a vote still scheduled.
