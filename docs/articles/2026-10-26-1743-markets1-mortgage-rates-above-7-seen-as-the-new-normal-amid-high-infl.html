Mortgage Rates Above 7% Declared 'New Normal' by Economists, Who Also Say Not to Call It That

Housing forecasters expect borrowing costs to stay above 7% well into 2027, ending hopes that the 3% mortgage will return from retirement.

The wire said: Mortgage rates above 7% are the 'new normal'—here's why · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 26, 2026 17:43 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Mortgage rates spent the years before 2022 at 3% or 4%, then jumped as inflation rose and the Fed tightened. CNBC reports that housing groups now expect rates to stay above 7% well into 2027.

Why it matters

Anyone waiting for a return to pandemic-era mortgage rates may be waiting a long time, and the monthly payment on a given house will stay far higher than it was a few years ago.

Housing economists have settled on a phrase for mortgage rates above 7%: the new normal. According to CNBC, the term has appeared in public comments from Lawrence Yun of the National Association of Realtors and Robert Dietz of the National Association of Home Builders. Fannie Mae forecasts rates could average 7.5% in 2027, while the Mortgage Bankers Association expects them to stay above 7%. The organizations that sell and build houses, in other words, are telling buyers to get comfortable with expensive money.

The mechanism starts with the 10-year Treasury yield. Translation: the interest rate the U.S. government pays to borrow for a decade, which lenders use as a yardstick for pricing mortgages. Mortgages are usually paid off or refinanced well before their 30-year term ends, so the 10-year is the closer comparison. The latest official figure puts that yield at 4.96%, which explains why a 7% mortgage does not feel like an aberration.

Inflation is the first culprit. When prices keep rising, bond investors demand higher yields to make up for the shrinking value of the money they get back later. CNBC notes that the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, the PCE price index, has not been below the central bank's 2% target since February 2021. Consumer price inflation was 3.74% year over year as of September, per the latest official data, so the target remains a place the economy visits only in forecasts.

The second culprit is crowding at the borrowing counter. Selma Hepp of Cotality told CNBC that heavy federal borrowing and the AI boom's appetite for data centers and power generation are competing for capital. She described a shift from a world with more capital than investment opportunities to one with tremendous competition for it. Translation: when everyone wants a loan, loans cost more, and the person buying a three-bedroom colonial is standing in line behind a server farm.

Then there is the matter of who buys mortgage bonds. Tom Graff of Facet said banks have grown wary of mortgage-backed securities after the 2022-23 rate spike produced steep losses, and the failures of Silicon Valley Bank and First Republic brought more regulatory scrutiny of bond holdings. Translation: banks got burned, and regulators are now watching them hold the matches. Meanwhile the Fed, which bought large quantities of Treasuries and mortgage bonds during the pandemic to push rates down, has stepped away from buying mortgage securities, according to TD Bank's Steve Kaminski.

Getting back to 6%, Hepp said, would likely take lower inflation, slower growth or less government borrowing pressure. Kaminski added that a weakening economy could also do it, since investors flee to Treasuries in a downturn and push yields lower. That is the housing market's current path to relief: a recession, or a miracle on the deficit.

Hepp herself objects to calling 7% the new normal, since nothing about that number is permanent. What is gone, she said, are the conditions that routinely produced 3% and 4% mortgages. For buyers, CNBC notes, waiting for much lower rates is a gamble, one in which the house is the stake and the casino is the bond market.
