Disney Buys Character.AI's Staff and Code, Leaves Lawsuits at the Curb

Court filings show Character.AI's bankruptcy was part of a deal letting Disney hire most of its employees and seek a technology license while the legal liabilities stay behind.

The wire said: Disney found a way to buy Character.AI without buying its lawsuits · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 20, 2026 15:53 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Character.AI, a chatbot startup, has faced lawsuits over chatbot safety and investigations by regulators. It filed for chapter 11 bankruptcy on October 12, and filings show the move is tied to a two-part deal with Disney.

Why it matters

If this structure works, buyers can take the people and technology of troubled companies without taking on the harms those companies are accused of causing. Regulators and injury claimants will have to decide whether existing corporate law can stop it.

Character.AI filed for chapter 11 bankruptcy this week, and court filings reported by Ars Technica, citing WIRED reporting, show the filing was part of an arrangement with Disney. Disney has hired most of the startup's employees, including its former CEO Karandeep Anand, and hopes to obtain a license to much of its technology. The legal liabilities, according to the filings, are designed to stay with whatever remains of Character.AI. It is a house sale in which the buyer takes the furniture, the staff and the blueprints, and the seller keeps the mold.

The structure is a variation on what some call a reverse acquihire. Translation: a buyer hires a company's key people and licenses its technology, rather than buying the company, so the target is left a husk. Companies often argue these are not mergers and so do not require notifying federal antitrust regulators. The practice has become more common in AI and has irritated Congress.

The deal has two linked transactions. In September, Disney paid the startup $5.5 million plus a refundable $2 million good-faith deposit, hired the majority of its staff, and agreed to let some of those employees help keep Character.AI running for five months. The second transaction runs through bankruptcy court, where Disney seeks a perpetual, worldwide, nonexclusive license to code, documentation, tools and other artifacts. That includes its trust and safety moderation stack, training infrastructure and age-prediction technology. User data and the website are not on the list.

That license will be auctioned in the coming months. If Disney wins, with approval from Character.AI, a committee of unsecured creditors and the courts, it pays an additional $12.5 million at closing. If it loses, it gets its $2 million back plus a breakup fee. To preserve what filings call a competitive process, Disney also agreed that a rival winner could interview and potentially hire up to 80 percent of certain employees Disney just hired, though Disney picks which ones. Experts quoted by the outlet doubt that moving staff between companies within months is realistic, or that the deal could easily be unwound.

Disney also drafted a court order, called the 363 Free and Clear Order, stating that the transaction is not a merger and that Character.AI keeps responsibility for any liability or obligation, including legal claims. Translation: the buyer is asking a judge to confirm in writing that it is not buying the problems. Character.AI's lawyers said Disney insisted on completing the deal through bankruptcy because of the startup's shrinking cash and its litigation and regulatory risks. Disney declined to comment.

John Newman, a University of Memphis law professor and former FTC competition official, said corporate law has long dealt with buyers who want every asset and none of the liability. But this deal, he said, is structured in a way that safeguards were not built to guard against.

It is not the startup's first such arrangement. In August 2024, Google reportedly paid $2.7 billion to license its technology and hired its founders and some researchers, though a spokesperson said the vast majority of employees stayed. Google was later named alongside Character.AI in several lawsuits, including one alleging a 14-year-old boy died by suicide after a chatbot cultivated an emotionally dependent and sexually exploitative relationship. A judge rejected Google's argument that it was a separate company, finding the plaintiff had sufficiently alleged aiding and abetting liability.

In January, Google and Character.AI agreed in principle to settle that case and others. A California probate court approved the overall settlement in July, and a referee approved each individual settlement on October 11, the day before the bankruptcy filing. Plaintiffs' groups declined to comment.

Character.AI's new CEO said in a declaration that the company struggled with profitability at all times. After an investment banker searched for a buyer starting in August 2025, the company heard consistent feedback that its product and users were impressive but that litigation and regulatory overhang made it unbuyable. A coalition of state attorneys general began investigating that November. Bankruptcy, the declaration says, is meant to reach a consensual resolution with injury claimants and the states while maximizing value for shareholders.

The company, in other words, was a good product attached to a bad liability, and Disney found the part of the deal where the two could be separated. What to watch is the auction, the judge's view of the free-and-clear order, and whether anyone outbids a company that already employs the people who know how the code works.
