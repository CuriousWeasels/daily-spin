Zelensky Says US Intelligence Sharing 'Works Now,' a Sentence Allies Rarely Need to Say

After talks in Oslo, Kyiv says early-warning data is flowing again, while Patriots, Starlink and Washington's Russia policy remain unresolved ahead of Witkoff's trip to St Petersburg.

The wire said: Zelensky says US intelligence sharing 'works now' after talks with Trump envoy in Oslo · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 21, 2026 02:09 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Ukraine accused the US of withholding intelligence on Russian missile attacks, which Secretary of State Marco Rubio called scandalous. Ties have soured further as Trump courted Russia on diesel and sanctions with US midterms approaching.

Why it matters

Early warning of ballistic missiles decides whether civilians reach shelter in time, and Patriot interceptors are the only effective defence against such missiles. How Washington balances Kyiv against Moscow will shape both the war and fuel prices at American pumps.

Volodymyr Zelensky said on Tuesday that US intelligence sharing on Russian missile attacks "works now", after talks in Oslo with Trump envoy Steve Witkoff, according to the BBC. The statement followed Kyiv's accusation that Washington had withheld key intelligence, which Rubio rejected as "scandalous". It is an unusual diplomatic achievement to thank an ally publicly for a service that was supposed to be running already.

Zelensky said Ukraine had not received warnings during ballistic attacks for three days, as he told NBC News. Rubio denied any pause in sharing and blamed a "software problem". Translation: the world's most advanced intelligence apparatus was briefly defeated by the same thing that defeats everyone else on a Tuesday. Zelensky noted that civilians rely on these alerts, and the BBC reported that five Russian S-400 missiles hit Kyiv on 12 October, reportedly before sirens or phone alerts sounded.

The politics are less tidy than the software. Trump has told Zelensky he is "not a good guy for us to deal with" and said Ukraine should get a new president, then someone else to negotiate for it. Earlier this month, Ukrainian negotiators arrived in Miami to learn that Trump had struck a deal with Vladimir Putin to open US markets to Russian diesel. The BBC called it a stinging rebuke to Kyiv, which is a polite way to describe being informed of your ally's new Russia policy by the news.

Then came sanctions. Trump waived wide-ranging Russia sanctions under the Graham Act two days before they were due to take effect, saying they would hinder peace efforts. Translation: the penalties meant to pressure Moscow were paused so Moscow could be persuaded, which has historically been the order in which persuasion works least. Kremlin envoy Kirill Dmitriev called it a historic moment, and former US ambassador William Taylor said Washington was offering carrots to Russia and sticks to Ukraine.

The motive, the BBC and NPR both suggest, is energy prices before next month's midterms. Fuel costs have risen since the US-led war on Iran began, and Trump has blamed Ukraine's strikes on Russian oil refineries. Experts questioned whether the diesel deal would lower US petrol prices. Vice President JD Vance said Ukraine was a partner "in some ways" and that it should stop bombing Russian energy, which is an unusual request to make of a country being bombed.

Hardware is the other sore spot. The Washington Post, citing an unnamed Ukrainian official, said only a couple of Patriot interceptors remained in Ukraine's stockpile, amid a global shortage after the Iran war. Rubio conceded some may not have arrived but said nothing was denied intentionally. Ukraine also reported speed limits on Starlink, which a bipartisan group of senators wants investigated; Reuters said the order came from the White House, which Rubio denied.

Oslo produced optimism of the carefully unspecified kind. Finnish President Alexander Stubb cited "genuine progress" without details, and a US official described "strong alignment at the working level". Witkoff now takes the proposals to Putin in St Petersburg, with Jared Kushner joining by video. Foreign Minister Sergei Lavrov has said the Kremlin will listen but rule out "any political concessions".

Meanwhile, Russian drone attacks continued, and Ukrainian officials said at least 10 people were killed on Tuesday, four in Kyiv and the capital region, four in Donetsk and two in Dnipro. Russia controls about 20% of Ukraine. Trump says it is time for Ukraine to end the war, while Zelensky says Ukraine is not losing it but is losing people. Next up is Putin, who has so far said nothing and, given the proposals, may reasonably feel no rush.
