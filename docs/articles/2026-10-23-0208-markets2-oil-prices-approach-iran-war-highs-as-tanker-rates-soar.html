Oil Nears War Highs as Tanker Rates Add Second Squeeze

Brent crude sits at $115.22 while Wall Street, per MarketWatch, begins pricing in a full regional conflict, and the VIX remains calm at 16.68.

The wire said: Oil prices approach Iran-war highs as soaring tanker rates trigger a second energy squeeze · MarketWatch

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 23, 2026 02:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Oil prices have climbed toward their highs since the Iran war began, and MarketWatch reports that soaring tanker rates are adding a second layer of energy costs. Investors are starting to weigh a wider regional conflict that could further restrict global supply.

Why it matters

Higher oil and shipping costs tend to feed through to fuel, goods and food prices, which matters when inflation is already at 3.74% and bond yields are elevated. If a wider war does arrive, the market's current calm may turn out to have been optimistic.

Oil prices are approaching their highs since the Iran war began, and MarketWatch reports that rising tanker rates are creating what it calls a second energy squeeze. The latest data show Brent crude at $115.22 a barrel as of October 20. Wall Street, the outlet says, may be starting to price in the scenario everyone fears: a full-scale regional conflict that chokes off more global energy supply.

The first squeeze is the price of the oil itself. The second is the price of moving it. Translation: tanker rates are what shipowners charge to carry crude from where it is to where it is needed, and when a region gets dangerous, ships become scarcer, insurance gets pricier, and the bill lands on whoever wanted the oil in the first place. A barrel is only useful once it has arrived, a fact markets tend to rediscover during wars.

For ordinary people, this is how a conflict thousands of miles away reaches a gas pump, a delivery fee and a grocery receipt. Oil is an input to nearly everything, and shipping is the thing that gets it there. The most recent inflation figure, 3.74% year over year as of September 1, was published before this latest leg higher, so it does not yet tell us how much of this is coming.

The backdrop is not especially forgiving. The 10-year Treasury yield stands at 5.13% and the 2-year at 4.81%, while the Fed funds rate is 3.88%. Translation: the market is demanding more to lend money for ten years than the central bank charges overnight, which is the kind of gap that usually signals investors expect inflation to be sticky or the government to borrow a great deal. The central bank, meanwhile, would prefer not to cut rates into an energy shock, and not to raise them into one either.

Stocks, curiously, are not panicking. The SPY, a fund tracking the S&P 500, was at 763.96 as of October 22, the Nasdaq at 26,702.86 on October 21, and the VIX at 16.68. Translation: the VIX measures how much volatility options traders expect, and 16.68 is roughly the reading of a person who has heard the smoke alarm and is checking whether it is just the toast. The sources do not say what the VIX was earlier in the conflict, so it is hard to call this complacency, but it is hard to call it alarm either.

The dollar index, at 121.4 as of October 16, adds another wrinkle. Oil is priced in dollars, so a strong dollar makes crude more expensive for much of the rest of the world, which would compound the squeeze outside the United States. Unemployment at 4.2% suggests the labor market has not cracked, which gives households some cushion but also gives the Fed less reason to rush to their aid.

What to watch next is whether tanker rates keep climbing and whether Brent clears its war high. If both do, the market's current serenity will be tested by a scenario it has apparently only half priced in. Investors have, to be fair, been given plenty of time to prepare, which is traditionally when they stop.
