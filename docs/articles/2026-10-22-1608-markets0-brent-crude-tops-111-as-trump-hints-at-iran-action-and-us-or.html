Oil Tops $111 as Washington Plans Iran Strikes After Voters Finish Voting

Brent crude jumped 2.8% to $111.81 as the U.S. ordered some staff out of Saudi Arabia and reports surfaced of post-midterm strike plans.

The wire said: Brent crude tops $111 after Trump hints at action on Iran, U.S. orders staff to leave Saudi Arabia · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 22, 2026 16:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The Iran conflict is entering its ninth month, with Gulf oil exports largely recovered but Iranian volumes near zero and Houthi attacks on Saudi targets escalating. Oil has swung around $100 a barrel for weeks.

Why it matters

Expensive oil feeds into fuel, shipping and food costs, and the next move depends on decisions that appear to be scheduled around an election calendar. Households are better cushioned than in past oil shocks, but the cushion has limits.

Oil prices rose Thursday after reports that the United States plans to resume strikes against Iran. CNBC reported that Brent crude futures gained 2.8% to $111.81 a barrel and nearly touched $113 earlier in the session, while U.S. West Texas Intermediate rose 1.7% to $98.83 after briefly breaking $100. The market, in other words, reacted to a plan that has not happened yet, which is what markets are for.

The trigger was a New York Times report, citing unnamed U.S. officials, that the U.S. and Israeli militaries have drawn up specific plans for renewed strikes after next month's midterm elections. The officials described a "short but intensive" operation. Translation: a war with a start date, an end date and, apparently, a polling-place buffer zone.

President Donald Trump said the U.S. is not negotiating with Iran and that something will "happen fairly soon," without elaborating. Asked about timing, he said, "Most people seem to think it's after the election." That is a notable way to describe a military timetable, as though it were a rumor about a restaurant opening.

On the ground, the picture is less abstract. The State Department ordered non-emergency government employees and their families out of Saudi Arabia, citing escalating Houthi attacks on civilian targets. According to Saudi state media, at least two people were killed and eight injured in an attack on King Fahd International Airport in Dammam. Reuters reported that Saudi Aramco told staff to evacuate its Dhahran headquarters and work remotely. Spokesman Tommy Pigott said the order applies only to Saudi Arabia, rejecting reports of a wider Gulf directive.

The oil flow itself is a more complicated story. MarketWatch reported that October flows out of the Persian Gulf nearly returned to pre-war levels, though traders are wary of a fragile recovery. A CNBC energy column adds that Iranian volumes are almost nonexistent, and that the paper price, the futures contract on traders' screens, can sit far below the price of physical barrels, which in some cases may be double. Translation: the number on the screen is the polite version.

Official data compiled alongside the sources put Brent at $115.22 as of October 20, so the Thursday level is a pullback from that figure, not a record. The same data show CPI inflation at 3.74% year over year as of September, and the 10-year Treasury yield at 5.12%. Higher oil lands on top of an economy that is already not short of price pressure.

Analyst David Wagner of Aptus Capital Advisors told CNBC that $100 oil is "very different" from 20 or 30 years ago. He noted that gasoline took roughly 5% of disposable income a decade ago and about half that now, and that prices remain below inflation-adjusted highs. That is true and mildly reassuring, as long as one does not live in Europe, where the same column notes diesel prices that have been sky-high without yet destroying demand.

The immediate calendar is earnings. SLB reports October 23, followed by Baker Hughes and Cheniere, with ExxonMobil and Chevron on October 30. The CNBC column expects big numbers but little guidance, since even companies that buy the world's best intelligence cannot forecast what a missile launcher will do. Watch for the one sentence executives will say the most: it depends.
