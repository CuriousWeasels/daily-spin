Alleged Ransomware Gang Member Books Holiday, Gets Itinerary Reviewed by Police in Two Countries

A 28-year-old Russian man tied to the Qilin gang was arrested in Japan, and German detectives are now looking at whether he is connected to the 2024 Synnovis hack that disrupted London hospitals.

The wire said: Russian man linked to deadly London hospitals hack arrested · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 17, 2026 01:44 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

In June 2024 a ransomware gang called Qilin attacked Synnovis, a blood testing provider serving London hospitals, disrupting pathology services for weeks. King's College Hospital later confirmed that one patient had died, with a long wait for a blood test result among the contributing factors.

Why it matters

Arrests in ransomware cases are rare, and prosecuting an attack on a hospital as a contributor to a death would set a precedent. If it works, criminals who target health services may face homicide-style charges rather than only extortion charges.

German police have detained a Russian man accused of being a core member of the Qilin cyber-crime gang, the BBC reported. Named in German press as Vladimir K, he is 28 and was arrested by Japanese police in May when he arrived for a holiday. According to German officials, his travel plans had been intercepted, which is the polite way of saying someone read his itinerary and it was not just him reading it.

The BBC says it can now confirm that German detectives are including the 2024 Synnovis hack in their investigation of him and his associates. Translation: he was originally wanted for something else, and investigators are now checking whether he also belongs in the hospital file. That earlier matter was a September 2024 attack on a German logistics company, in which data was encrypted and about $160,000 in cryptocurrency was demanded.

Synnovis is a pathology service run as a partnership between hospitals and the German company Synlab. When Qilin hit it on 3 June 2024, a critical incident was declared at some London hospitals. Blood transfusion services were severely disrupted, operations were cancelled and emergency patients were diverted elsewhere. In total, the BBC says, more than 10,000 appointments were disrupted over weeks.

King's College Hospital NHS Foundation Trust confirmed a year later that a patient had died unexpectedly. It said a number of contributing factors led to the death, including a long wait for a blood test result. The BBC describes the attack as the first cyber attack in history directly linked to a death. Separately, the BBC has learned that British police are examining whether its own inquiry may lead to an unlawful killing case, which would potentially be a world first for cyber-crime.

There is a reason nobody has done this before. A 2020 attack on a hospital in Düsseldorf prompted a negligent homicide investigation after an elderly patient died, but it was dropped. Medical experts concluded the patient would have died regardless, and prosecutors could not definitively prove the attack was the direct cause. Translation: proving that a hacker killed someone is harder than proving the hacker was a hacker.

Qilin is alleged to have hacked more than 4,000 organisations since 2024 and extorted more than $140m (£105m). The gang has been tracked since 2022 and is linked to attacks on schools, hospitals, councils and companies, as well as the Japanese brewer Asahi in October 2025. When no ransom was paid by Synnovis, the criminals published highly private data online. They told the BBC the attack was also a protest against the UK's lack of help in Russia's war on Ukraine. The BBC notes that claim was dismissed as an attempt to distance the group from its usual extortion tactics.

The arrest is also a lesson in geography. Hackers are very rarely arrested in Russia, whose government refuses to cooperate with Western law enforcement requests. Staying home is therefore the safest option for a ransomware professional, and the extradition of Vladimir K is a reminder that the system works best on people who book flights.
