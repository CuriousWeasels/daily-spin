Oil Is Flowing Again, Diesel Is Not, and Nobody Is Refunding Your Truck

Persian Gulf crude exports are nearly back to pre-war levels, but Ukrainian drone strikes on Russian refineries keep diesel at $6.32 a gallon.

The wire said: Oil is now flowing out of the Persian Gulf like before the Iran war. Why prices may still not come down anytime soon. · MarketWatch

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 22, 2026 02:03 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The U.S. went to war with Iran in late February, disrupting Gulf oil shipments through the Strait of Hormuz. Separately, Ukraine has spent months striking Russian refineries, and Russia was the world's second-largest diesel supplier before the attacks.

Why it matters

Diesel moves freight, farm equipment and delivery trucks, so a prolonged shortage feeds into the price of nearly everything on a shelf. The International Energy Agency says tightness could last well into 2027, which is longer than any campaign promise.

MarketWatch reported that oil flows out of the Persian Gulf in October nearly returned to levels seen before the Iran war began, though traders remain wary about how fragile the recovery is. This is the kind of good news that comes with a footnote the size of the article. Crude is moving again, and prices are still expected to stay elevated.

The reason is that crude oil and diesel are not the same product. Translation: crude is the raw material, and diesel is what you get after a refinery cooks it, and refineries are exactly what is currently being damaged. You can have all the oil in the world and still be short of the thing that runs a truck.

CNBC reported that more than 1,000 Ukrainian drones targeted Russia on Sunday alone, according to state media reports, setting ablaze an oil export terminal in St. Petersburg. Kyiv has also dropped its brief adherence to an energy ceasefire that President Trump declared on Oct. 11. Moscow ignored that truce from the start, which made it a ceasefire in the way a no-parking sign is a parking policy.

The numbers explain the stakes. According to Kpler data cited by CNBC, Russian diesel exports fell more than 75% to 141,000 barrels per day in September, from 608,000 a year earlier. Under the Trump-Putin deal to boost shipments, Russia agreed to supply about 75,000 barrels per day in October and 124,000 in November. Tatiana Mitrova of Columbia University called those volumes a rounding error against total demand.

Matt Smith of Kpler said the deal looks likely dead in the water, since the volumes are unlikely to return without an energy ceasefire. Ukraine, for its part, appears to have concluded that a plan to help Russia sell more fuel is not a plan it needs to help with. Strikes on Russian refineries and related infrastructure almost tripled to 31 in July from 11 in January, according to Rochan Consulting.

Ukraine has also gotten smarter about what to hit. Mitrova said a destroyed fuel tank takes a couple of weeks to fix, but a secondary refining unit needs equipment from outside Russia. "You cannot just order it on Alibaba," she said, noting that sellers must be compensated for the risk of violating sanctions. Translation: the parts exist, but the people who sell them would like to be paid for breaking the rules, and the wait is months.

Meanwhile, the average U.S. diesel price was $6.32 per gallon on Tuesday, nearly 75% above a year earlier, according to AAA. With midterm elections less than two weeks away, Trump has blamed Ukraine for the surge. CNBC noted, however, that the market was already strained by the Iran war, with diesel exports through Hormuz down about 540,000 barrels per day and global exports down about 1 million.

Options for the White House are limited. U.S. refiners are already running at maximum capacity, and the underlying problem is damaged refining capacity from two wars that show no sign of ending. For the markets, SPY closed at 773.09 as of Oct. 21, suggesting investors are more relaxed about all this than the people filling a tank.

The next thing to watch is whether anyone revives the energy ceasefire. Until then, the pump price is set less by the oil flowing out of the Gulf than by what is still burning in Russia.
