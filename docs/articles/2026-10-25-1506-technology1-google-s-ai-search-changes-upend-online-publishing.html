Google Tells Publishers the Web Is Thriving, Publishers Check Their Traffic and Disagree

Since Google put AI summaries atop search results, publishers report falling clicks, while Google says click volume is 'relatively stable' and offers pilot programs.

The wire said: Google’s AI search changes upended online publishing · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 25, 2026 15:06 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

In 2024 Google launched AI Overviews, which place an AI-written summary above the usual search links, and in May 2025 it added AI Mode, a chatbot-style replacement for standard search. Publishers who spent years optimizing for Google say their traffic has dropped since.

Why it matters

If fewer readers click through to the sources behind AI answers, the outlets producing the underlying reporting lose revenue, which could mean less of it gets produced. It also raises the question of who pays for the material AI search depends on.

The Verge reported that Google's AI search features have reshaped online publishing, and not in the direction publishers had hoped. For years the deal was simple: publishers filled Google's index with content, and Google sent readers back. Translation: the web was a restaurant that paid the delivery app to bring in customers, and the app has now begun serving the food itself.

The mechanism is straightforward. AI Overviews, launched in 2024, put a generated summary at the top of results, with source links below it. A reader who gets the answer in the summary has less reason to visit the page the answer came from. The Press Gazette found Overviews appeared on almost a quarter of news-related searches in May 2024, and by August they were displacing media sites that had previously held the top spots for some queries.

The numbers, as reported, are unpleasant. The Wall Street Journal said traffic to Business Insider, The Washington Post, HuffPost and the Journal itself has declined since 2022. Similarweb data suggested search traffic to The New York Times fell from 44 percent to 35.5 percent of its total over three years. Digiday reported that 19 members of the trade group Digital Content Next lost between 1 and 25 percent of Google traffic in a single month, from May to June 2025. DMG Media told the UK's Competition and Markets Authority that click-through rates fell by up to 89 percent between June and July 2025, which it attributed to AI Overviews. Pew Research found people are less likely to click links when they see an Overview.

Google's account differs. Search head Liz Reid said click volume remained 'relatively stable' and that AI search sends more clicks to sites with forums, videos, podcasts and user posts. Nick Fox, a senior vice president, told the BBC: 'From our point of view, the web is thriving.' Translation: total clicks may be fine, provided one does not ask which websites are receiving them.

The courts have not been much help. The Verge reported that US District Judge Amit Mehta dismissed an antitrust case brought by Chegg and Penske Media, which owns The Verge, and also rejected a proposed class action over AI Overviews from Arkansas newspapers. Publishers have had somewhat more success with regulators. Following an order from the UK, Google broadly rolled out a toggle letting sites opt out of AI Overviews and AI Mode without disappearing from regular search. It does not, per The Verge, fix falling traffic.

Google has made some concessions. Sources are now more prominent in both features, with pop-ups on hover and in-line links placed higher in responses more often. The company says 400 publishers, including The Washington Post, CNN, Axios and the Financial Times, are in its News AI pilot program, which licenses content for new AI features. The Information and Digiday reported that it is also testing payments for content used in Overviews and AI Mode.

Meanwhile the summaries are growing. Some now expand automatically instead of waiting for a user to click 'Show more,' pushing the blue links further down. Publishers are leaning on newsletters, social media and subscriptions, which is a polite way of saying they are trying to build a relationship with readers that does not run through the company that just redefined the relationship.

Whether licensing deals and pilot programs repair things remains, as The Verge put it, to be seen. For now the arrangement is that Google reads the whole web, summarizes it, and sends a few thank-you notes.
