Gunmen Attack Funeral at Jordanian Church; Officials Say Little Because Little Is Known

Eight people were killed and about 40 wounded in Karak, and by Saturday the three attackers had not been identified or caught.

The wire said: Gunmen open fire at a church during a funeral in Jordan, killing 7 and wounding 40 · NPR World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 24, 2026 14:57 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Three gunmen opened fire on Friday at a Greek Orthodox church in Karak governorate, southern Jordan, during a funeral. Jordan called it terrorism, and the attackers remain at large.

Why it matters

Attacks on Christian places of worship are rare in Jordan, a country that markets itself as stable and tolerant. The response will show whether the government can reassure its citizens, and the visitors who come to its biblical sites, before anyone knows who did this.

Three gunmen attacked a Greek Orthodox church in Karak governorate on Friday while a funeral was under way, according to NPR and Al Jazeera. Footage broadcast on Jordanian state television showed the attackers firing outside the church and running toward its entrance. The sources differ slightly on the toll: NPR's headline reported seven dead and 40 wounded, while Al Jazeera, citing Jordan's Petra news agency, said eight died, seven at the scene and one woman hours later.

Al Jazeera reported that 27 of the roughly 40 injured remained in hospital on Saturday, with one in critical condition after surgery. Survivors described hearing an explosion shortly after the service began. Two attackers arrived in a vehicle and were joined by a third carrying a semi-automatic rifle, and they fired for several minutes. A lawyer who survived, Nasser al-Burqan, told Reuters a gunman standing about two metres away shot at him. His son, also present, was later treated for a leg wound. One visitor at the hospital called it a massacre "in every sense of the word."

On the central question of who did it, the government has been consistent and unhelpful. Official statements refer to "unknown assailants." Interior Minister Mazin Al-Farrayeh blamed "extremists" with "no relationship to humanity" but named no group. Prime Minister Jafar Hassan cited "blind extremism" and a terrorist ideology without saying whose. Translation: the state has established that the attack was bad and has not yet established anything else.

The motive is equally unclear, which is what happens when the people who might explain it are still at large. Al-Mamlaka, the state news channel, reported that security forces were pursuing the two suspects who arrived by vehicle. Al Jazeera said the attackers were masked, though one survivor described seeing a man without a mask holding a handgun.

King Abdullah II promised the "harshest penalties" for those responsible and ordered a review of how the country's security procedures respond to terrorist attacks. That is a sensible order, and also a quiet admission that three armed men were able to fire for several minutes before anything stopped them. Reviews are what governments commission when they need to do something while the investigation is still finding its feet. Jordan declared three days of mourning, with flags at half-mast over government and public buildings.

The condemnation was broad. Al Jazeera listed statements from Azerbaijan, Iraq, Poland, Palestine and Qatar, plus the US embassy in Jordan. Ahmed al-Tayeb, grand imam of Al-Azhar in Cairo, called it a "heinous terrorist attack" and said it contradicts the teachings of the Abrahamic religions. Condemnations of this kind are quick to arrive and tend to be identical. The harder work is finding the attackers.

Context matters here. Jordan's Christians number about 300,000 in a population of 11.5 million, and Karak has long been home to many of them. Taylor Luck of the Christian Science Monitor told Al Jazeera that Christians and Muslims have lived side by side there for generations, and that an attempt to divide them would more likely strengthen the community. Jordan has seen violence in Karak before: in 2016, ISIL attacks there included a siege at a Crusader-era castle that killed 10 people, including seven police officers and a Canadian tourist.

What to watch next is simple: arrests, a claim of responsibility, and whether the security review produces anything more concrete than a review. Until then, Jordan has a mourning period, a list of foreign condolences and three men who are still out there.
