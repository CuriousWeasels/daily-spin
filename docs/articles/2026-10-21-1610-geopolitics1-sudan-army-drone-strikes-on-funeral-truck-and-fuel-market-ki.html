Drones Strike Sudan Funeral Convoy and Fuel Market; Wit Declines to Attend

More than 300 people were reportedly killed in drone strikes across West Kordofan and Abyei, raising tensions between Sudan and South Sudan over a region neither has managed to settle.

The wire said: Sudan army drones hit mourners and fuel market killing more than 300 people, sources say · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 21, 2026 16:10 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Sudan's army and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces have been at war since April 2023, a conflict that has killed hundreds of thousands and displaced more than 14 million. Abyei, an oil-rich border area, has been in legal limbo since South Sudan became independent in 2011.

Why it matters

Civilians, including those in a supposedly protected UN zone, are increasingly exposed to cheap drones supplied from abroad, while 20 million people already face acute hunger. A spillover into South Sudan would widen a crisis that existing diplomacy has failed to contain.

Drone strikes on Sunday and Monday reportedly killed more than 300 people in Sudan's West Kordofan state and in Abyei, the BBC reported. The Sudan War Observatory, which tracks the conflict, was told by sources that 150 people died on Sunday evening, including 145 when a truck carrying about 200 mourners was hit as it arrived in the town of Al-Dabab. A tent where mourners had gathered was also struck, killing five more. Abyei's information minister later put the death toll at the Monday market attack there at 153.

The Abyei strike hit Amiet Market, about 10 kilometres from Abyei town, at around 11:30 local time on Monday. A military source told the BBC the army carried out the attack, describing the market as a fuel supply site for the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces. DW reported that South Sudanese officials also blamed the army but offered no evidence. Sudan's army has not responded to the allegations about the Sunday strikes, but says it does not attack civilians even when it believes RSF fighters are among them.

That last position deserves a translation. Translation: the army says it does not target civilians, and also that the presence of suspected fighters does not change this, which leaves the word civilian doing a great deal of unpaid work.

The death toll in Abyei more than doubled in a day, from 73 to 153, according to the information minister, Yohana Akol Ngor, speaking to Reuters and the Associated Press. He said around 8,000 people had been displaced. A fuel depot appears to have been among the sites hit, and the resulting fire was still burning on Tuesday, DW reported. Emergency workers struggled to reach bodies because of the flames. BBC Verify examined satellite images showing extensive burnt buildings at the market, which older images show is usually full of trucks.

Médecins Sans Frontières said it received more than 20 wounded at Ameth Bek Hospital, including several critical cases and burn patients. A trader told the BBC the attack was a shock because Abyei is meant to be a weapons-free zone under UN protection, with more than 2,800 uniformed UN personnel deployed there since 2011. Presumably the protection is meant to be read as a statement of intent rather than a technical specification.

The politics around the strike are older than the war. Abyei was left unresolved by the 2005 peace agreement and again at South Sudan's independence in 2011. A promised referendum never happened because nobody could agree on who gets to vote. DW notes that the Ngok Dinka majority favours South Sudan, while Misseriya nomads who use the pastures favour Sudan. The territory has since been jointly administered, with a temporary UN force that has now stayed for 15 years.

South Sudan summoned Sudan's ambassador to lodge a formal protest and urged Sudan to refrain from actions that endanger civilian lives. Sudan's military alleges the RSF operates out of Abyei, and the UN's Abyei mission made a similar statement in May. Officials in Abyei and South Sudan dispute that. Both sides, as the BBC notes, have been supplied with drones by foreign countries despite a partial arms embargo.

UN spokesperson Stéphane Dujarric said he was alarmed by the continued deadly drone strikes, which are killing civilians and disrupting essential services. A separate drone strike near Khartoum's airport on Monday killed four people, including three children, AFP reported, citing a medical source. A military source said air defences downed other drones launched by the RSF.

Kordofan, rich in gold and oil, has become the pivotal front since the army retook Khartoum. What to watch next is whether South Sudan's protest stays on paper. An arms embargo that is partial, a peacekeeping zone that is weapons-free in name, and a border dispute that is 15 years old have all been tested this week, and none has yet produced a result.
