Sudan Drone Strikes Kill Scores at Funeral Transport and Market as Sources Disagree on Toll

Reports put deaths above 200 across West Kordofan and Abyei, while the warring parties and foreign suppliers continue as before.

The wire said: Sudan army drones hit mourners and fuel market killing more than 200 people, sources say · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 20, 2026 15:53 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Sudan's army and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces have been at war since April 2023, a conflict that has killed hundreds of thousands and displaced more than 14 million people. Drones have become the main weapon, and fighting has shifted to the oil- and gold-rich Kordofan region.

Why it matters

Cheap armed drones are making markets, roads and funerals more dangerous in a country where 20 million people already face acute hunger. A strike in Abyei has also pulled in South Sudan, raising fears of a wider regional conflict.

Drone strikes in Sudan's West Kordofan state on Sunday evening and in the disputed Abyei area on Monday have reportedly killed more than 200 people, according to the BBC. The Sudan War Observatory, a group that tracks the conflict, was told by sources that 150 people died in two West Kordofan locations. Abyei's information minister said a further 73 died at a market. The war has been running long enough that the main variable in each report is the number.

The accounts differ in detail, which is normal when the people who could count the dead are also the people being bombed. The BBC, citing the observatory, said a truck carrying about 200 mourners was hit as it arrived in Al-Dabab, killing 145, with five more killed at a funeral tent. The Guardian, citing a witness and an anonymous medic, put the toll in al-Dibab at 80, describing mostly commuters at a transport hub after market day. The witness said the drone struck twice, ten minutes apart. The spellings of the town also differ, and so far this is the only point on which nobody is dying.

The question of who did it is less contested than usual. The RSF-declared administration, known as Tasis, accused the army, according to the Guardian. The army has not responded to the West Kordofan allegation, but said it does not attack civilians even if it believes RSF fighters are among them. Translation: the army's position is that if you are near the enemy, the target is the enemy, and the rest is a classification issue.

In Abyei, a military source told the BBC the army carried out the strike and described the Amiet market as a fuel supply site for the RSF. Translation: a market where fuel was sold became a military target because of who bought the fuel. BBC Verify examined satellite images showing extensive burnt buildings at the market, which older images show is usually full of trucks. It also verified footage showing a large smoke plume and at least one petrol tanker. Medecins Sans Frontieres said it had received more than 20 wounded at a hospital in Abyei town, including burn patients.

Abyei adds a diplomatic problem. Sudan and South Sudan both claim the oil-rich area, a dispute unresolved since South Sudan became independent in 2011. South Sudan summoned Sudan's ambassador to lodge a formal protest, and there are fears the conflict could spill across the border. A formal protest is the diplomatic instrument for expressing alarm without moving anything heavier than a piece of paper.

The broader numbers explain why drones keep appearing in these stories. The Guardian reported that between January and April, drone strikes accounted for at least 880 civilian deaths, more than 80 percent of all conflict-related civilian deaths in Sudan. The BBC noted that foreign countries keep supplying both sides with drones despite a partial arms embargo. Translation: an embargo is a rule about where weapons may not go, and it works best on weapons that are not already there.

UN spokesperson Stephane Dujarric said the organization was alarmed by continued deadly drone strikes that kill civilians, including children. Last week, UN official Rosemary DiCarlo told the Security Council there was "no breakthrough on the horizon", according to the Guardian. She said the parties believe they can gain more on the battlefield and that outside support will let them keep trying. That is a fairly complete forecast: both sides expect to win, and both are being supplied to continue.

Watch next whether South Sudan's protest turns into anything more than correspondence, and whether the army answers the West Kordofan allegation. The drones, by contrast, have so far been quick to respond.
