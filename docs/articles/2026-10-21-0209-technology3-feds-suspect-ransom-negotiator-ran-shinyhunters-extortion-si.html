Ransom Negotiators Accused of Negotiating With Both Sides, Reportedly Without Telling Either

Federal investigators suspect executives at two firms hired to talk victims out of paying hackers were unusually close to the hackers, according to KrebsOnSecurity.

The wire said: Feds suspect ransom negotiator ran ShinyHunters extortion site · Hacker News

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 21, 2026 02:09 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

ShinyHunters, a hacking group, stole sensitive personnel data from the FBI's recruiting website in late September and has extorted more than $70 million this year, according to the FBI. Its alleged leader, a teenager in Jordan, was reportedly detained and is cooperating.

Why it matters

Companies hit by ransomware often hire outside negotiators on the assumption that these firms work for the victim. If some were closely tied to the extortionists, every ransom payment and every referral deserves more scrutiny.

U.S. federal investigators have identified executives at two ransomware negotiation firms who kept unusually close relations with the ShinyHunters extortion group, KrebsOnSecurity reported. Sources told the outlet that one American executive allegedly helped operate a website used to pressure victims into paying. Translation: the person hired to stand between you and the burglar may have been helping with the burglar's invoicing.

Ransomware negotiators are consultants a company hires after a breach to talk to the hackers, verify what was stolen, and arrange payment if the company decides to pay. The business model depends on the negotiator being on the victim's side. It also depends on the negotiator being very good at talking to criminals, which turns out to be a fine line to walk.

The case began with the FBI's own recruiting site, fbijobs.gov, which ShinyHunters hacked in late September. The stolen files reportedly included medical and psychological records, Social Security numbers, home addresses, and employee unit information. The bureau, which is not known for taking this lightly, began arresting and questioning people who had communicated with the group. Reuters reported on October 3 that the leader, known as Rey, had been detained by Jordanian authorities and was cooperating.

The thread then ran through Pepijn van der Stap, a suspected ShinyHunters member arrested in the Netherlands on September 15. According to a source, he was logged into Signal at the time, which gave police access to recent chats with contacts including negotiators in Canada and the United States. Dutch outlet RTL reported that the messages helped expose an alleged murder-for-hire plot, with no indication the orders were carried out.

On October 9, the FBI arrested Edward Dubrovsky, a 54-year-old Canadian cybersecurity executive, charged with threatening to disclose stolen data and interfering with interstate commerce through extortion, according to court records. He had recently published a book on handling cyber extortion that recommended always engaging with the hackers. Prosecutors have asked that he stay in custody pending trial, Politico reports. Charges are allegations, and the case records remain under seal.

A source told KrebsOnSecurity that the chats show Dubrovsky and at least one executive at a large U.S. consulting firm often knew about new ShinyHunters victims before the victims did. The source also said Dubrovsky knew the group had taken data from the FBI and did not tell the bureau before the group threatened to publish it. Being the first to know about a breach is a fine quality in an incident responder. It is a more awkward one in a person whose client has not yet been informed.

The consulting executive is suspected of helping run shinywire.com, an extortion site active from about May 3 to October 16 with entries for nearly 100 organizations. Each page urged victims to negotiate, displayed samples of stolen files, and warned that data would be published if they refused for seven days. KrebsOnSecurity is not naming the firm, described as prestigious with more than 1,000 associates, or the executives, because they have not been charged.

The report also touches on a business dispute. The Globe and Mail reported that Dubrovsky sued his former partner Daniel Tobok of Cypfer in Ontario, and Tobok denied the allegations and counterclaimed. Krebs's sources say Tobok was known to ransomware operators years ago by the nickname Ghostbyte, and cites a 2018 Dharma ransomware reply pointing a victim to that email address. Tobok has not responded to requests for comment, and it is unclear whether he ever used it.

The industry now faces an awkward question for its next sales pitch: how does a customer verify that the professional who promises to negotiate down the ransom is not, in some sense, on the other side of the table. The courts will take it from here.
