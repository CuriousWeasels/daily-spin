Israelis Vote in Record Numbers to Decide Who Will Spend Weeks Negotiating

Turnout is the highest in decades, but polls show neither Netanyahu's bloc nor Eisenkot's reaching the 61 seats needed for a majority.

The wire said: Turnout so far highest on record as Israelis vote in pivotal election · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 27, 2026 16:54 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Israel is holding its first national election since the Hamas-led attacks of 7 October 2023 and the wars that followed. The vote is widely seen as a referendum on Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, who faces former military chief Gadi Eisenkot.

Why it matters

The outcome will shape Israel's approach to Gaza, Iran and the occupied West Bank, and the coalition haggling could take weeks or months. A deadlock could send the country back to the polls, as it did five times between 2019 and 2022.

Israelis went to the polls on Tuesday in numbers that officials and reporters described as record-setting. According to Israel's Central Elections Committee, quoted by Al Jazeera, almost 45 percent had voted by 2pm, the highest at that hour in 50 years and 6 percent above the same point in 2022. The BBC reported that about 70,000 people had flown home from abroad to vote, which is a lot of airfare spent on a result that may not be known for some time.

Polling published on Friday, per the BBC, shows Netanyahu almost neck-and-neck with Eisenkot, who leads the newly formed Yashar Party. Neither bloc is projected to reach 61 seats, the majority of the 120-seat Knesset. Translation: the voting is the quick part, and the real contest begins afterward, when party leaders spend weeks deciding whom they dislike least.

Israel's proportional representation system means every government has been a coalition. Eisenkot heads a so-called change bloc whose members share little beyond the wish to remove Netanyahu, including former prime minister Naftali Bennett, Avigdor Lieberman and Yair Golan. Netanyahu's path runs through another alliance with the far-right and ultra-Orthodox parties of his current government, which the BBC calls the most right-wing in Israel's history.

The campaign has centered on security and democracy. The opposition has attacked Netanyahu over the failures that preceded 7 October, when nearly 1,200 people were killed and 251 taken hostage, and has demanded an independent inquiry, which he has resisted, saying it would be biased against him. He is also on trial on corruption charges, which he denies. Netanyahu has answered by presenting himself as "Mr Security", citing strategic victories and his relationship with President Donald Trump.

The final days brought some theater. Israel's Channel 12 reported that Netanyahu pushed for military action that could provoke Iran before the vote, and CNN reported he asked Trump to approve new strikes; he denied both. He also posted an AI-generated image of a nuclear bomb hitting Tel Aviv, which Eisenkot called "hysterical". Nothing says confidence in your record like a computer-generated apocalypse.

Arab parties, representing about a fifth of the population, could in theory be kingmakers. In practice, all but one of the leading Jewish parties have ruled out working with them, and Lieberman has rejected any arrangement in which they prop up a minority government. Al Jazeera reported that Netanyahu urged supporters to vote by warning of high turnout among Palestinian citizens, under the slogan "the right or Palestine". Eisenkot and Netanyahu have both ruled out partnering with Arab parties, citing a lack of shared values.

There were also complaints about access. The Association for Civil Rights in Israel filed a complaint reporting delays in at least 18 communities and asked the committee to investigate "whether these delays are deliberate". It also noted a large police and National Guard presence at a polling station in the Negev. The BBC said the Central Election Committee found no evidence of systematic tampering, and officials attributed long lines to high turnout and bad weather.

Exit polls are due at 22:00 local time, though the BBC cautions they can vary significantly. Votes from soldiers and reservists will mostly be counted from Wednesday, which may give right-wing parties a late boost. Pollster Mitchell Barak told Al Jazeera that high turnout raises the number of votes needed to clear the 3.25 percent threshold and would help incumbent parties.

If no bloc can assemble a majority or a viable minority government, new elections follow, with Netanyahu staying on as interim prime minister. The country has done this before. Watch for the late-counted soldier ballots and which red lines survive contact with arithmetic.
