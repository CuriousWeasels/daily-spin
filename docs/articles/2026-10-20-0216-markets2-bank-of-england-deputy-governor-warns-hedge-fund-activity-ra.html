Bank of England Official Notes Bond Market Now Owned by People Who Borrow to Own It

Sarah Breeden says hedge funds' growing, leveraged presence makes a bond shock spreading across markets more likely than before, as the IMF flags $13 trillion in hedge fund assets.

The wire said: Why hedge funds could be making a bond market shock more likely · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 20, 2026 02:16 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Government bond markets were once dominated by pension funds and insurers that buy and hold. Hedge funds now hold a growing share, and Treasury and gilt yields recently hit multi-decade highs.

Why it matters

Government bond yields set borrowing costs for mortgages, businesses and governments. If leveraged funds are forced to sell together, a local wobble could become a broader squeeze that reaches ordinary borrowers and savers.

Bank of England deputy governor Sarah Breeden told CNBC on Thursday that the likelihood of a bond market shock spreading from one market to another is "greater than what it was." The reason, she said, is that the investors in core government bond markets have changed. Pension funds and insurers are giving way to hedge funds, which she described as price-sensitive, leveraged and international. That is a polite way of saying the buyers of government debt are now the sort who may leave quickly.

Breeden, who handles financial stability at the central bank and sits on its rate-setting committee, spoke on the sidelines of the IMF-World Bank annual meetings in Bangkok. Her remarks echoed the IMF's Global Financial Stability Report, published Tuesday. The Fund put hedge funds' gross assets at about $13 trillion. It acknowledged that they support liquidity and efficiency, then warned that leverage and crowded trades could turbo-charge stress in a crisis.

Translation: leverage means borrowed money, and a crowded trade means everyone has had the same clever idea at once. This works well until everyone wants to leave through the same door. The IMF also said the stress could spill into areas such as banks' prime brokerage businesses, which are the units that lend to hedge funds and presumably would like to be repaid.

The trade drawing the most scrutiny is the basis trade. Translation: a fund buys actual Treasurys and sells the matching futures contracts, hoping to pocket the small price gap between the two. CNBC noted the gap is relatively small, so funds often borrow heavily to make the profit worth the paperwork. A tiny edge, multiplied by a large loan, is the financial industry's preferred recipe for both bonuses and incident reports.

The scale is not small. According to U.S. Office of Financial Research data cited by CNBC, hedge funds held a record 7% of the $30 trillion Treasury market at the end of 2025. That is roughly $2.1 trillion, or enough that their risk management has become everyone else's problem. For comparison, the buy-and-hold crowd that used to anchor the market did not typically need to sell in a hurry.

The backdrop is already jumpy. Earlier this month, 10-year and 30-year Treasury yields hit their highest levels since 2002, and the latest data show the 10-year at 5.09%. UK gilt yields also touched multi-decade highs, while French OAT yields have climbed on worries about the country's fiscal outlook, stoking fears of a fresh euro zone debt scare. A yield is the return investors demand to lend; when it rises, governments, mortgage borrowers and companies all pay more.

Markets themselves look calm for now, with the VIX, the so-called fear gauge, at 19.99. That is the sort of reading that suggests nothing is wrong, which is also what it suggests right before something is. Breeden did not predict a crisis. She merely observed that the system's shock absorbers have been partly replaced by passengers who bring their own accelerators.

What to watch next is whether regulators move from warnings to rules on leverage in basis trades. Until then, the safety of the world's safest asset depends in part on the patience of borrowers.
