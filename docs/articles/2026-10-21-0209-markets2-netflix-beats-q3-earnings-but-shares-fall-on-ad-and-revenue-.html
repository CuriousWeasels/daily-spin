Netflix Beats Earnings, Misses the Mood, and Watches Shares Fall 6%

The streamer topped profit estimates and matched revenue forecasts, but a trimmed ad outlook and worries about viewing time sent the stock lower after hours.

The wire said: Netflix posts third-quarter earnings beat, but shares fall on advertising and revenue guidance · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 21, 2026 02:09 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Netflix reported third-quarter results on Tuesday after a rough stretch that included layoffs of about 6% of staff, the departure of its chief content officer and a stock price nearly 50% below its all-time high. Investors have been watching for signs that viewing and advertising growth are holding up.

Why it matters

Netflix is a bellwether for streaming and a large holding in many index funds, so a drop of this size touches retirement accounts as well as subscribers. The ad business is meant to be the next big revenue source, and a slower rollout suggests the cheaper tier will take longer to pay off.

Netflix said on Tuesday that third-quarter earnings came in at 87 cents per share, ahead of the 82 cents analysts polled by LSEG expected. Revenue was $12.87 billion, exactly what analysts predicted, according to CNBC. Shares fell about 6% in extended trading, which is how markets say thank you when you beat one number and merely match the other.

Net income was $3.66 billion, up from $2.55 billion a year earlier. Netflix said part of the jump came from the timing of expenses. Translation: some costs landed in other quarters, and the profit looks better for the moment than it might on a perfectly even calendar. Revenue rose almost 12% from last year, helped by membership growth and a price increase that took effect in March. The company said customers reacted to the higher prices as they have to past increases, meaning they complained and then kept watching.

The trouble was the outlook. Netflix narrowed its 2026 revenue forecast to $51.1 billion, the low end of its earlier range of $51 billion to $51.4 billion and below the $51.2 billion Wall Street had expected. Narrowing a forecast sounds like precision. In this case it meant choosing the lower of the available numbers.

The bigger disappointment was advertising. Netflix now expects about $2.7 billion in ad revenue this year, down from roughly $3 billion. It blamed a slower-than-expected rollout of some ad product capabilities. Co-CEO Greg Peters said on the earnings call that the company's ad stack build-out was the fastest of any comparable one in the industry, but that it was "a bit behind our aggressive target." Translation: we are very fast, and also late to our own deadline.

Viewership is the other worry. The shareholder letter acknowledged "some investor concern about our view hours growth," and argued that engagement remains healthy. MarketWatch noted that Netflix cited intense competition, and CNBC pointed to YouTube as one of the rivals. The company's remedy is a three-step plan: deliver more entertainment value, use technology to improve the service, and improve monetization. The last item is the one that appears on the invoice.

Last week Netflix said it was cutting about 6% of employees, roughly 1,000 workers, and that Chief Content Officer Bela Bajaria was leaving the role. Former Disney executive Peter Rice has been named her successor. The company expects the head count reductions to weigh on fourth-quarter operating margin by about 120 basis points. Translation: 1.2 cents of every dollar of revenue, roughly, will go toward the cost of reorganizing.

Options traders had gone into the report in a hopeful mood. CNBC reported that options prices implied a 9% move after earnings, the largest since late 2022, and that call buying outpaced put buying. One seller of puts, a bullish bet, collected about $2 million in premium. The market did move by roughly the amount traders priced in, just in the unhelpful direction.

For the fourth quarter, Netflix guided to revenue up more than 11% to $13.44 billion. The company has now told investors that it is growing, that engagement is fine, and that the ad business will be larger eventually. Investors have asked, politely and by selling, to see some of that sooner.
