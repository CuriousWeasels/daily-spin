Disney Hires Character.AI's Staff, Leaves Lawsuits Behind in Bankruptcy Court

Disney paid $5.5 million up front, hired most of the startup's employees, and is bidding for a license to its code, while the legal liabilities stay with the shell.

The wire said: Disney agrees to hire the majority of Character AI’s workforce · TechCrunch AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 19, 2026 17:49 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Character.AI, a chatbot startup, filed for chapter 11 bankruptcy on October 12 after facing lawsuits over chatbot safety and investigations by state regulators. Its former CEO, Karandeep Anand, is now Disney's first CTO.

Why it matters

The deal shows how a buyer can take a company's people and technology while the legal claims stay behind, a structure that may matter to families suing AI firms and to regulators watching AI deals.

Character.AI filed for chapter 11 bankruptcy this week, and Disney is positioned to end up with most of its employees and a broad license to its technology. According to TechCrunch, citing a legal filing spotted by Bloomberg, Disney agreed to make offers to at least 60 employees, the majority of the workforce, at an estimated $60 million in annual compensation. The filing says the vast majority of those offered jobs accepted. Disney has acquired a company's talent and technology without acquiring the company, which is a considerable achievement in subtraction.

Ars Technica reported that the bankruptcy was not a sign of collapse so much as a component of the deal. Court filings describe two linked transactions. In September, Disney paid $5.5 million plus a refundable $2 million good-faith deposit, hired the majority of the staff including the CEO, and let some of them help keep Character.AI running for five months. The second transaction happens through bankruptcy court.

There, Disney seeks a perpetual, worldwide, nonexclusive license to code, documentation, tools and other artifacts, including the trust and safety moderation stack, training infrastructure and age-prediction technology. Translation: Disney wants the safety tools, which is a notable thing to want from a company facing safety lawsuits. The list reportedly excludes user data and the website. If Disney wins the auction and the courts approve, it pays another $12.5 million at closing. If not, it gets its deposit back and a breakup fee. TechCrunch separately reported a $20 million figure for assets, a number the Ars account does not break down the same way.

The structure matters because of what Disney did not buy. Disney drafted a proposed court order, called the 363 Free and Clear Order, stating that the transaction is not a merger and that Character.AI keeps responsibility for any liability or obligation, including legal claims. A motion from Character.AI's lawyers said Disney required the deal be fully completed through bankruptcy because of the startup's shrinking cash and its litigation and regulatory risks. Disney declined to comment to the reporters.

Ars calls this a variation on the reverse acquihire. Translation: a buyer hires the key employees and licenses the technology, leaving the target as a husk. Companies often argue these are not mergers and so need not notify antitrust regulators. John Newman, a University of Memphis law professor and former FTC competition official, said decades of corporate law address buyers who want assets but not liabilities, but the safeguards were not built for a structure like this.

Character.AI has done this before. In August 2024, Google reportedly paid $2.7 billion to license its technology and hired its founders. Google was later named in lawsuits, including one over a 14-year-old boy's death, and a judge found the plaintiff sufficiently alleged Google was liable for aiding and abetting. Google and Character.AI agreed in principle in January to settle that case and similar ones, and a referee approved the settlements on October 11, a day before the bankruptcy filing.

Character.AI's new CEO said the bankruptcy aims for a consensual resolution with injury claimants and a group of states investigating the company, while maximizing asset value. The company said it had struggled with profitability at all times. An investment banker found in August 2025 that buyers called the product impressive but the litigation and regulatory overhang made it unbuyable. Disney, it turns out, found a buyable version.

What to watch: the auction. The bid is a stalking horse, so a higher bidder could appear, and Disney has agreed to let a winner interview up to 80 percent of the staff it just hired. Experts quoted by Ars doubt that moving employees twice in a few months is realistic. For now, the company that kept the liabilities is the one with the fewest people left to answer for them.
