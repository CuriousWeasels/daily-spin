Meta Pauses Muse Sharing After 'Private' Links Turn Out to Be Searchable

Security firm Claroc found 6,800 shared Muse conversations through public DNS records, including loan applications and a confidential executive-search call.

The wire said: Meta Muse exposes users' personal and business secrets when they share conversations · Ars Technica AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 21, 2026 02:09 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Meta recently launched Muse, a general-purpose AI agent that Mark Zuckerberg said was built from the ground up for privacy and security. Users can share conversations through web links, which researchers say were far easier to find than anyone intended.

Why it matters

Anything you paste into an AI chatbot and then share by link may be less private than the word 'link' suggests. This is also the latest in a pattern in which sharing features at major AI companies exposed chats more widely than users expected.

Meta has paused the share feature in Muse, its new AI agent, after researchers reported that conversations shared through the feature could be found by anyone willing to run a search. Meta told Ars Technica and The Verge it is reviewing the report and considering changes to how the links can be discovered. The company said that sharing creates a page accessible to those with the link, which is accurate in the way that a house is accessible to anyone with the address.

The researchers are from the security firm Claroc. According to Ars Technica, they enumerated 6,800 Muse share links and opened a few hundred. Translation: they made a list of thousands of pages that were supposed to be known only to the people who received them, and then started reading.

The mechanism is unglamorous. Share links normally include a long, random string that nobody can guess, which is the entire security model. Claroc says Muse built each web address from a short title describing the conversation, followed by a random ID. Because that text sits in the hostname, it shows up in DNS records, the public directory that maps web addresses to servers. Claroc said the hostname usually revealed what a page contained before it was even opened.

The key tool was passive DNS, which Ars describes as a searchable historical record of domain lookups collected by commercial services. Translation: once someone clicks a share link, the address lands in a database that others can search. The random ID was doing its job. It was simply sitting next to a label saying what was inside.

What the researchers found was ordinary and therefore worse. Ars reported a candidate's notes for a confidential executive-search call with a frontier AI lab, including the lab's internal contact and the meeting time. There was a marketing company's internal revenue review marked for its CEO, a small-business owner's loan application with the amount requested, and a to-do list containing a bank fraud alert with a partial account number. There was also a real-estate investor's performance report with two property addresses, and a workplace's full-year vacation calendar listing dozens of employees by the week they would be away. "It took one query," the researchers wrote.

Claroc's co-founder and CTO, Asaf Aprozper, told Ars he reported the findings through Meta's bug bounty program on October 8 and also tried contacting people at Meta on LinkedIn. He got no reply either way. Meta, for its part, said it opened the bounty program so researchers could raise exactly these sorts of scenarios, which suggests the program works, at least as a source of scenarios.

The episode sits awkwardly beside Zuckerberg's claim last month that Muse was built for privacy and security. It is also not new. The Verge notes that conversations with ChatGPT, Claude and Grok have previously ended up on Google Search through sharing features. The industry has now independently discovered the same lesson several times, which is a form of peer review.

Meta has not said when sharing will return or what will change. The obvious fix is to stop putting a description of the contents in the address. Until then, users who wanted to send a colleague their loan application have the option of attaching it to an email.
