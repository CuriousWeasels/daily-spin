Anthropic Files to Go Public, Asks Investors Not to Think Transactionally

The AI lab reports $16.3 billion in first-half revenue, an $80.7 billion net loss, and a governance setup that leaves seven founders holding 50.1% of the votes.

The wire said: Anthropic officially files for its public debut · TechCrunch AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 27, 2026 16:54 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Anthropic, the maker of the Claude AI models, filed its IPO prospectus with the SEC on Monday after weeks of leaked drafts. It plans to list on the Nasdaq under the ticker ANT.

Why it matters

If the listing goes ahead, ordinary investors and index funds could end up owning a piece of a company whose fixed compute bills run to hundreds of billions of dollars, with almost no say in how it is run. The filing also shows how much of the AI boom rests on a single company's growth continuing.

Anthropic filed for an initial public offering on Monday, TechCrunch reported, and plans to trade as ANT. The filing shows revenue of $16.3 billion for the first half of 2026, against $1.2 billion in the same period a year earlier. The company has not yet said how much it wants to raise or at what share price, which is a bit like announcing a wedding and leaving out the date and the guest list.

The profit picture depends on which line you read. According to TechCrunch, the operating loss was $693 million for the half, down from $2.1 billion a year earlier. The Decoder reports that the second quarter alone showed a first GAAP operating profit of $974 million. Translation: GAAP means the standard accounting rules, so this is the version of profit that auditors will not laugh at. The net loss, meanwhile, was $80.7 billion, which TechCrunch attributes largely to noncash adjustments tied to convertible debt. Translation: the company did not spend that money. Its accounting says its own debt became more costly on paper, which is the sort of loss that is real in a spreadsheet and imaginary in a bank account. Anthropic still warns that it may not achieve or sustain profitability.

The customer figures are the part Wall Street will like. Per the filing, more than 11,900 customers spend at least $100,000 a year, about 1,900 spend $1 million or more, and about 250 spend $10 million or more. The Decoder reports that net revenue retention is roughly 900 percent. Translation: the average existing customer is paying nine times what it paid a year ago, a figure most subscription businesses see only in their dreams or in their cancellation emails. The Decoder also notes that no single customer exceeds ten percent of revenue, but five together account for 25 percent.

Then there is the bill. The Decoder reports that compute contracts with Broadcom, Google, Amazon, Microsoft and others total roughly $780 billion to $810 billion. Translation: compute is the rented computer power that trains and runs the models, and these payments are largely fixed. Revenue, with 80 percent billed per token processed, rises and falls with actual usage. The company therefore must keep growing quickly to pay for the growth it has already ordered. The Decoder adds that Anthropic left third-quarter figures out of the filing even though it already has them, and suggests that could mean growth slowed, though it also points to OpenRouter data suggesting demand recovered after an October model release.

Control is the other unusual feature. TechCrunch reports that seven co-founders, grouped as the Founder LLC, will hold 50.1% of shareholder voting power, which Anthropic says protects its AI safety mission. A Long-Term Benefit Trust will appoint most of the board. Voting power will not match economic ownership, so public shareholders will have little practical control. Translation: you may buy the stock, but you are being invited to the party mainly as a spectator.

The prospectus is also unusually candid, and unusually literary. MIT Technology Review notes that about 100 of its 335 pages cover risks, including that the technology could, in the filing's words, pose "catastrophic or existential risks to humanity." The company also writes that buyers should not think of the purchase "in purely transactional terms." Technology Review points out that one risk factor even considers how debates over model sentience and moral status could constrain operations or raise costs. In plain terms, if Claude wakes up, that might be a line item.

Valuation expectations are large. The Decoder, citing Yahoo Finance, says Wall Street expects about $2 trillion, up from a recent private valuation of about $965 billion, while MIT Technology Review puts the IPO valuation at more than $1.5 trillion. The sources disagree, which is a normal level of precision for a company that has not yet set a price. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley are leading the offering, and chip stocks including Nvidia, Broadcom and AMD rallied after the filing became public.

What to watch next is the missing pieces: the price, the amount raised, and the third quarter. Until then, investors are being asked to value a company that describes itself as a profitable business, a safety organization and a possible existential hazard, sometimes on the same page.
