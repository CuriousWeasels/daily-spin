ECB Holds Rates at 2.5%, Then Lagarde Announces She Is Leaving to Speak Freely

The central bank paused after two hikes as euro zone inflation hit 3.8%, and its president said she will quit in February 2027 to say things a central banker may not.

The wire said: European Central Bank keeps key interest rate on hold at 2.5% · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 30, 2026 03:06 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Energy prices jumped after the U.S.-Israeli war with Iran began in February, and the ECB raised rates in June and September, its first hikes since 2023. On Thursday it held steady, and Christine Lagarde said she will leave eight to nine months before her term ends.

Why it matters

Borrowing costs for mortgages, businesses and governments across the euro area depend on whether the ECB hikes again in December, and on who inherits the job. France's rising bond yields add the risk that the next president must manage a debt scare.

The European Central Bank left its deposit rate at 2.5% on Thursday, as markets expected, even though euro zone inflation reached 3.8% last month, the highest since September 2023, according to CNBC. Energy price inflation was 18.8%. The bank held, in other words, while the thing it is supposed to control kept rising, which in central banking is called a measured approach.

Translation: the deposit rate is what banks earn for parking money at the ECB, and it sets the tone for borrowing costs across the bloc. Christine Lagarde said the Governing Council debated a hike but unanimously chose to wait. Her reasoning was that underlying inflation and wages are broadly stable, and she told CNBC the bank is not seeing second-round effects, meaning higher energy costs spreading into wages and everyday prices.

The bank did not ignore the problem so much as note that markets were already tightening on its behalf. Lagarde said the average 10-year sovereign yield had risen about 35 basis points since the last meeting. Translation: a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so governments are paying roughly a third of a point more to borrow for a decade. She attributed part of the rise in long-term borrowing costs to hyperscalers, the giant tech companies, issuing more long-term bonds and competing for the same lenders.

Analysts read the pause as temporary. UBS's Dean Turner called it a hawkish pause rather than the end of tightening. Translation: hawkish means inclined to raise rates, so the bank is pausing while keeping its hand near the lever. Deutsche Bank's Mark Wall expects one more hike, to 2.75%, in December, and money markets priced about a 67% chance of one, according to LSEG data.

Then came the other news. Lagarde said she will step down on Feb. 7, nearly nine months before her term was due to end in October 2027. She said she wants the freedom of speech and expression to engage on matters that are not monetary policy, and that as ECB president she was bound by an obligation of neutrality. Central bank independence, it turns out, is paid for in opinions.

The timing also lands before the French presidential election, where CNBC said polling suggests Marine Le Pen is the frontrunner. Aberdeen's Felix Feather noted that the new French president will have no say in choosing the next ECB head. Lagarde has repeatedly downplayed any idea that she plans to run herself.

The succession is a political negotiation. The European Council appoints the top jobs after consulting the Parliament and euro area states, and nationality matters as much as résumé. Spain's Pablo Hernández de Cos and the Netherlands' Klaas Knot are widely seen as leading contenders. Knot is considered more hawkish, and Validus Risk Management said his appointment would likely support the euro but could add pressure to French bond spreads. Translation: a spread is the extra interest France pays over Germany, and it is at its highest since the early 2010s debt crisis.

Berenberg's Holger Schmieding said the ECB has no mandate and no appetite to fund French fiscal follies, and would only support France if Paris returns to more sustainable policies. That is a polite way of saying the safety net exists, but there is a form to fill in first. Chief economist Philip Lane and board member Isabel Schnabel are also due to leave next year, so the bank is replacing much of its leadership while energy prices and French debt are both unsettled.

The Governing Council gave Lagarde a standing ovation. Whether anyone will listen to her new opinions, she said, is another story. The first test of the ECB without her is likely to be December, when it decides whether to hike once more.
