Berkshire Rallies as Investors Rediscover Boring Things, Including Cash, Soda and Oil

Berkshire Hathaway's A shares closed at $798,000, 1.4% below their record, as a sell-off in tech left the conglomerate's unglamorous holdings looking brilliant.

The wire said: Berkshire shares buck market sell-off, rally to highest since Buffett said he would step down as CEO · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 18, 2026 14:56 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Warren Buffett told shareholders in May 2025 that he would step down as CEO at the end of that year, and Berkshire shares then fell more than 14% in three months on fears that a 'Buffett premium' was dissolving. Greg Abel now runs the company.

Why it matters

Berkshire is a bellwether for whether investors still want patient, cash-rich 'old economy' companies when tech wobbles. Its roughly $360 billion cash pile also earns more when short-term rates are high, which is good for Berkshire and less cheerful for anyone with a mortgage.

Berkshire Hathaway shares closed Friday at their highest level since Warren Buffett announced at the May 2025 annual meeting that he would step down as CEO. According to CNBC, the A shares ended at $798,000 and the B shares at $532.02, each about 1.4% below their all-time closing highs of $809,350 and $539.80. The market fell on Friday, and Berkshire rose more than 1%, which is the sort of thing a company can do when its main product is not enthusiasm.

The recovery has taken more than a year. After the succession announcement, the stock lost over 14% in three months as investors fretted that a 'Buffett premium' was disappearing. Translation: people had been paying extra for the man, and were now checking whether the company came with a receipt. B shares have cut their 17.5-point deficit against the S&P 500 since June 1 by almost two-thirds, and now trail by 6.0 points for the year.

CNBC points to several causes, most of them pleasantly dull. Keefe, Bruyette & Woods analyst Meyer Shields says higher short-term interest rates are helping, since Berkshire holds roughly $360 billion in cash and 'they'll be making more money' on it. Translation: the world's most famous pile of unspent money is finally being paid to sit there. The Fed funds rate is 3.88% and the two-year Treasury yields 4.77%, so waiting is a strategy again.

Insurance operations have enjoyed a quiet hurricane season. Chevron, a $19 billion holding, closed at a record after gaining almost 36% since early July, helped by higher oil prices, with Brent at $126.55. Coca-Cola, now a $37.6 billion position, also hit a record and is up 34% this year, perhaps helped by KFC's switch from Pepsi to Coke. Rarely has a conglomerate profited so thoroughly from weather that did not happen, expensive fuel and chicken restaurants changing soft drinks.

The less boring part is Bending Spoons, an Italian company that buys discarded internet brands such as AOL, Evernote and Vimeo, uses AI to cut costs, and spends the savings on more acquisitions. Berkshire disclosed a further $330 million purchase of 10 million new shares at $33, a discount of almost 12% to the prior close of $37.86. The announcement also revealed, apparently for the first time, a $600 million purchase at the July IPO. The shares closed Wednesday up 41% at $53.46 and ended Friday at $47.07.

CNBC's estimate, which Bending Spoons would not confirm, assumes Berkshire paid the $29 IPO price. That implies about 20.7 million shares then, around 30.7 million now, a stake of roughly 4.5%, and a paper gain of 55.3% on $930 million invested. The next portfolio disclosure, due next month, may settle the arithmetic. Who at Berkshire made the call remains unknown.

Analysts split on what it means. Morningstar's Greggory Warren sees a firm 'finally moving out of its comfort zone' to deploy capital. Barron's Andrew Bary notes that many holders want more stock buying given the cash pile, but that Bending Spoons was a surprise given its high valuation. Berkshire has now bought a stock at a large multiple of earnings, which for this company is the equivalent of showing up in a loud shirt.

The pattern is a familiar one. When investors tire of high-flying AI and tech names, they rediscover companies that sell soda, insure houses and pump oil. Berkshire's challenge is that its next big move now belongs to Abel, and the market will keep checking which parts of the stock were Buffett and which were just a very large pile of cash.
