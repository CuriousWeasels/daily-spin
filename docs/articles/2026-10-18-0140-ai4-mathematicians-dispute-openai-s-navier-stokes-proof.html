OpenAI's 166-Page Navier-Stokes Proof Impresses Everyone Except the People Who Can Read It

Mathematicians dispute whether the proof follows the method OpenAI claims, and OpenAI's own researcher says nobody knows what 10,000 agents contributed.

The wire said: Mathematicians say OpenAI doesn't understand its own Navier-Stokes proof · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 18, 2026 01:40 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

In early September, OpenAI presented a 166-page potential solution to the Navier-Stokes problem, one of mathematics' famous open questions. The Clay Mathematics Institute has been reviewing it since its release, and experts are now arguing over where it came from.

Why it matters

If AI systems can produce results that humans cannot easily verify, trace, or credit, the question of how anyone knows a result is genuine becomes as important as the result itself. It also bears on whether enormous compute spending is buying insight or just volume.

OpenAI has a potential solution to the Navier-Stokes problem, and several groups of mathematicians say it is not the solution the company described. According to Scientific American, as relayed by The Decoder, OpenAI claimed the 166-page proof followed the approach of Spanish mathematicians Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa. Experts say the resemblance does not survive contact with the details.

"When you go to the details, it is a different approach," said Gonzalo Cao-Labora of EPFL, who described the similarities as surface-level. Translation: it looks like the same recipe until you notice the ingredients, the oven and the dish are different.

The proof is written in Lean, a formal verification language. Translation: a program checks every logical step, which is excellent for correctness and no help at all for a human who wants to know what the argument is actually doing. The Clay Mathematics Institute, which has been reviewing the work since its release, is therefore checking something that is verifiable and not especially legible.

Then there is the question of provenance. Before publication, NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster accused OpenAI of building on work he and Anthropic's Levent Alpöge had done with publicly available models. OpenAI denied this, saying it never used Buckmaster's data. Camillo De Lellis of the Institute for Advanced Study said a definitive answer is probably impossible because language models are black boxes. A system that cannot explain its own reasoning also cannot easily testify about whose ideas it borrowed.

In early October, OpenAI added roughly 400 more results, which the math community can barely keep up with. Publishing them as a loose collection on GitHub angered many mathematicians, who felt it cheapened their field. Peer review runs on human attention, and 400 results at once is a denial-of-service attack with footnotes.

The effort itself was substantial: about 10,000 model instances running for roughly 88 hours and 130 billion tokens. Translation: tokens are the chunks of text a model reads and writes, so this was a very large amount of machine typing. Yet OpenAI researcher Noam Brown said nobody actually knows what the agent swarm contributed.

The reason is cost. Systematic comparisons at this scale are too expensive to run, so nobody has tested how long a single agent would need for the same problem, or what 10,000 agents offer over 1,000. Brown puts less than 10 percent of the success down to the multi-agent setup, and says the base model deserves most of the credit. The most expensive part of the experiment, in other words, may be the part that mattered least, though the company cannot say for certain.

The Clay Institute's review continues. Until it concludes, the proof remains a potential solution, produced by a method nobody can fully audit, using an approach its authors may have described inaccurately.
