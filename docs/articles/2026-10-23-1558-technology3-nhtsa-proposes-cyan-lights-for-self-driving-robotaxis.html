Federal Regulators Propose Cyan Lights So You Can Tell Nobody Is Driving

NHTSA wants a single blue-green marker lamp to announce that a robotaxi's driver is software, while quietly deleting the switches and levers that assumed a human existed.

The wire said: Feds want robotaxis to signal ‘I’m driving’ with cyan-colored lights · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 23, 2026 15:58 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration has proposed rewriting Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard No. 108, the rule governing vehicle lighting, to fit cars with no human driver. The draft includes an optional cyan marker lamp meant to show when an automated driving system is in control.

Why it matters

If adopted, a robotaxi would look the same to police and other drivers in every state, instead of each company inventing its own way to say no one is at the wheel. It also signals Washington wants fewer legal obstacles to cars without steering wheels, though it is still enforcing existing rules.

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration has proposed that driverless cars be allowed to display a cyan marker light, a blue-green glow meant to tell everyone nearby that software is operating the vehicle, The Verge reported. The agency says the lamps would signal this "to clearly and consistently signal to law enforcement and other road users." After years of debate over flashing text, audio alerts and custom lights, the government has answered with a color.

Why cyan? Cars already use red, amber, white, and flashing blue and red for brakes, turn signals, headlights and emergency vehicles. Translation: the rainbow is crowded, and cyan was the seat nobody had taken. There is also precedent. Mercedes-Benz won state-level approval for turquoise lights on its Level 3 Drive Pilot cars, then, according to The Verge, scrapped that approach in favor of a Level 2++ system. The color has outlived the product it was invented for.

The lights would be optional. What the rule would make mandatory is consistency: NHTSA would set the color, brightness and placement, so any company that adds them does so in a uniform, legal way. Waymo, Nuro, Zoox, GM and Mercedes could adopt the same standard, rather than each developing its own signal for the sentence 'please note there is no one in here.'

The proposal also deletes things. Current rules call for physical hazard switches, manual beam shifters and turn levers inside the vehicle. The agency proposes dropping them, since a car with no driver has nobody to flip them. It would also update the 12.8-volt testing standard so makers can build around modern LED systems without what The Verge called administrative hoops, and it is considering scrapping rules about whether high beams sit above or below low beams.

All of this belongs to a wider Department of Transportation push to speed up autonomous vehicle deployment, including cars without steering wheels or pedals. The government is, in effect, updating the rulebook so it stops requiring equipment for a person the vehicle was designed to remove.

Deregulation has limits, though. Last month NHTSA opened an audit query into the Tesla Cybercab to examine the data Tesla relied on when certifying it. At issue is the lack of sideview mirrors, pedals and a steering wheel, all of which the existing standards require. So the agency is rewriting the rules to allow cars without a steering wheel while checking whether a car without one followed the old rules.

The proposal is still a proposal. Watch for the comment period, and for whether carmakers embrace the color or let it join turquoise in the pile of lighting ideas that were briefly the future.
