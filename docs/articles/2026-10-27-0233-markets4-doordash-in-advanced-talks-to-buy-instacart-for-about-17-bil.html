DoorDash Eyes Instacart in $17 Billion Bid to Out-Deliver Walmart and Amazon

The Wall Street Journal reports the two delivery middlemen are in advanced talks, in a deal that would value Instacart at roughly $17 billion.

The wire said: A possible DoorDash-Instacart deal is all about keeping up with Walmart and Amazon, analysts say · MarketWatch

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 27, 2026 02:33 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

DoorDash, which brings restaurant food to your door, is reportedly in advanced talks to buy Instacart, which brings groceries to your door. MarketWatch reports analysts see the move as a response to pressure from Walmart and Amazon.

Why it matters

If the deal happens, two of the biggest names in app-based delivery would become one, which could affect the fees, prices and choices shoppers see when ordering groceries online. It also shows that even large platforms feel pressure from retailers that own the shelves, the trucks and the customer.

DoorDash was in advanced talks to buy Instacart in a deal that would value the grocery delivery company at roughly $17 billion, according to the Wall Street Journal, as relayed by MarketWatch. No deal has been announced in the material provided, and talks described as advanced have been known to end the way most dates described as going well do.

MarketWatch reports that analysts see the logic as defensive: keeping up with Walmart and Amazon. Translation: the two largest retailers already own the warehouses, the delivery fleets and the customer relationships, while DoorDash and Instacart rent access to other people's stores. Merging two rented businesses does not create an owned one, but it does create a larger one, which in corporate strategy is often treated as the same thing.

The appeal of a combination is fairly mechanical. DoorDash has customers who order dinner, and Instacart has customers who order the groceries they were presumably going to cook before they ordered dinner. Combine them and, in theory, one app handles both the failed intention and the backup plan. The sources provided do not give deal terms beyond the reported valuation, so questions of price per share, cash versus stock, and regulatory review remain open.

That last item matters. Antitrust regulators tend to take notice when one of the largest players in a niche buys another, and a deal between two leading delivery apps would presumably invite questions about competition. The sources do not say how regulators might respond, so any forecast would be speculation, a commodity the markets already produce in abundance.

The backdrop is not especially relaxed. The latest data show CPI inflation at 3.74% year over year as of September, and the 10-year Treasury yield at 5.0% as of October 23. Translation: groceries cost more, and borrowing to buy things costs more too. The Fed funds rate sits at 3.88%, so a $17 billion purchase is being contemplated in a world where money is not free, though it is considerably cheaper for those who can afford a $17 billion purchase.

Markets, meanwhile, are in a reasonably calm mood. The SPY exchange-traded fund, which tracks the S&P 500, stood at 765.37 on October 26, and the VIX, a gauge of expected market swings, was 19.45 on October 23. That is not panic, which leaves room for deals that might otherwise be considered too ambitious.

What to watch is whether the talks become an announcement, and whether regulators or customers object. Shoppers will likely learn the outcome the usual way: through a new fee that arrives with an explanation about enhanced value.
