Meta Updates Terms So Crawling Your Website Is Technically Your Idea

Starting October 30, business accounts must let Meta's bots crawl linked sites, and Meta says this has nothing to do with training AI on news, even though the text uses the word 'train'.

The wire said: Meta may use sweeping new commercial TOS to avoid millions in AI licensing fees · Ars Technica AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 17, 2026 14:30 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Meta is updating its commercial terms for business accounts on Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp and Messenger, effective October 30. Nieman Lab reported that the new language could let Meta crawl and train AI on websites linked to those accounts, including news publishers' sites.

Why it matters

If the terms work as legal experts fear, publishers could lose bargaining power over AI licensing fees, since Meta would already hold permission to use their content. Any business with a Meta account, from a newsroom to a shop, is covered by the same clause.

Meta will update its commercial terms on October 30 to authorize the company to use automated software to crawl websites associated with any business account that uses its platforms, according to Ars Technica and The Verge, both citing Nieman Lab. The terms say Meta may use the content it collects to 'develop, improve, research' and 'train' its products. Businesses that decline may 'stop all access or use' of Meta products for commercial purposes, which is the contractual version of 'the door is over there.'

Translation: a robots.txt file is the small text file a website uses to tell crawlers which pages are off limits. It has long worked on the honor system, which is a strong system until someone has a reason to stop honoring it. Legal experts told Nieman Lab the new terms could let Meta argue that its commercial terms override those instructions, and even override a licensing agreement.

Meta disagrees. Spokesperson Daniel Roberts said the company is 'consolidating' its commercial and self-serve ad terms, and that 'there is no change to how Meta uses web crawlers.' He added that content owners can still use robots.txt flags to state their preferences. Andy Stone, Meta's vice president of communications, wrote on X that the update is meant to pull accurate information about products for sale on third-party sites for ad and content recommendations, 'not about scraping news sites.'

That explanation is plausible for a store selling lamps. It is harder to apply to a newspaper, which sells the news and is not generally ranked by shoppers alongside lamps. The text also says nothing about advertising specifically. As Ars Technica notes, it never mentions AI at all, and the inference rests on one use of the word 'train' plus a long list of synonyms for scraping: bots, spiders, crawlers and user agents. Meta's lawyers were thorough about the spiders.

The stakes are mostly financial. Meta has followed OpenAI in signing AI licensing deals with CNN, Fox News, USA Today and People Inc. Those deals exist because the content is worth paying for. If a standard business-account clause already grants access, the price of a separate deal gets harder to defend. James Grimmelmann, a Cornell professor of digital and information law, told Nieman Lab the terms clearly give Meta permission to train AI on any content from publishers' sites, 'possibly' even in competition with them.

There is also the matter of the Meta One subscription, launched last month, which requires businesses and creators to pay to include links in posts and reels. Nieman Lab summarized the arrangement this way: Meta has begun charging users to post links to content it will be allowed to crawl for free. As business models go, it has the elegance of a toll booth that also photocopies your cargo.

The practical limits are clear. Nieman Lab says virtually every news organization has at least one Meta business account, and the terms offer no opt-out short of leaving. Publishers are also still fighting OpenAI over whether training without a license counts as fair use, and Ars Technica notes that Meta's terms could grant broad permissions whichever way the courts rule. Watch for whether any publisher quits Meta's business tools by October 30, or whether they all click 'agree' and hope the word 'consolidating' means what it usually does.
