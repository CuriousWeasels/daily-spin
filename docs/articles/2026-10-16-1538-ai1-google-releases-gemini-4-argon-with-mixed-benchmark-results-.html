Google's Gemini 4 Argon Aces Hardest Test, Provided Someone Sets Up the Test Correctly

Argon scores 97.5 percent on ARC-AGI-3 with vendor-specific features, but 49.8 percent without them, and its robot demos arrive with extensive written supervision.

The wire said: Google's Gemini 4 Argon is closer to the AI frontier than any Google model in months · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 16, 2026 15:38 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Google DeepMind released Gemini 4 Argon, a frontier model now being pitched for everything from general reasoning to robotics. ARC-AGI-3 is a benchmark of puzzle games meant to measure general problem-solving, and its scoring rules were disputed in July 2026.

Why it matters

Benchmark scores increasingly depend on the testing setup as much as the model, so a headline number tells buyers less than it appears to. Robotics claims likewise need failure rates and attempt counts before anyone can judge how ready they are.

Google DeepMind's Gemini 4 Argon scored 97.5 percent on ARC-AGI-3 at a cost of $4,800, according to ARC Prize, making Google the second lab after OpenAI to pass 95 percent. Only OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra scores higher, at 99.9 percent. The Decoder reported that Argon needed 62 percent fewer actions than the human baseline across the 25 public games, which is a polite way of saying it was efficient at games designed to be hard for machines.

The fine print is the test setup. The 97.5 percent came from the Provider Adapter Harness. Translation: a testing arrangement that lets each vendor use its own special features, such as retained reasoning and context compaction, which means the model keeps its thoughts and tidies its own memory between moves. In the Standard Harness, which allows only simple notes between rounds, Argon fell to 49.8 percent at low reasoning, at a cost of $5,800. It is the same model, with a different exam room and a higher bill.

This argument has history. In July 2026, OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol scored 7.8 percent in the standard environment, then 38.3 percent with OpenAI's own API features, beating Claude Opus 5 at 30.2 percent. OpenAI argued that benchmarks always measure the technical environment too, and ARC Prize co-founder Francois Chollet then allowed general, publicly available API settings. The result is a benchmark that now measures intelligence, plumbing, and how well the plumbing is documented.

Argon's standard run also had trouble finishing. API problems stopped it from completing all 55 games. It finished 42 at low reasoning and 46 at medium. At the highest setting it completed only 19, so ARC Prize published no score. The reported results still cover all 55 games by counting partial points from aborted runs, a grading policy many students would like to see applied to their own coursework.

Elsewhere the picture is more modest and probably more useful. Epoch AI gave Argon 165 on its Epoch Capabilities Index, which combines more than 50 benchmarks into one scale. That ties Fable 5.1 and sits just behind Opus 5.5 at 167 and GPT-6 Astra at 166. It is Google's closest approach to the top since Gemini 3.1 Pro in February, though Epoch's early estimates put Argon eighth on math and sixth on software engineering.

Google is also selling Argon as the one model robots need. According to The Decoder, it controls robots, builds simulated practice worlds from photos or sketches, and grades training videos. In one demo, a single photo of a table produced 77 tasks and more than 4,500 training episodes, lifting a real robot's success rate from 56 to 75 percent and cutting execution time from 35 to 24 seconds. Google did not say how many attempts sit behind the 75 percent.

The supporting prompts are less magical. The cup-righting task, described as zero-shot (meaning no prior examples), came with a camera image carrying a centimeter grid and pre-built commands such as grasp and lower until contact. The simulation prompts included long lists of prohibited shortcuts, like copying measured sensor values. Translation: the model is autonomous, once a human has written down everything it should not do.

Robotics scores also depend on who runs the test. On ERQA, a 400-question spatial reasoning benchmark, Google reports Argon at 87 percent, ahead of Claude Opus 5.5 at 82.3 percent. Artificial Analysis then published AA-Robotics, where models control a real arm across eight tasks, and Argon placed third with 55 out of 100, behind GPT-6 Astra at 69 and GPT-6.1 Sol at 65.

Argon is available through Google AI Studio and Antigravity for Google AI Ultra subscribers, and through the paid Gemini API tier. That is a reliable benchmark of its own: the only score no one disputes is the price.
