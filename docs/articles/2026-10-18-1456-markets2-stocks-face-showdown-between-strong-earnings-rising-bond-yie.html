AI Stocks Slide as Inflation Reminds Markets That Money Costs Money

The Nasdaq fell 4.5% last week, its worst since June, while the Dow rose 0.7% as investors fled to toothpaste-adjacent sectors.

The wire said: Stocks are heading for a high-stakes showdown, as strong earnings collide with rising borrowing costs · MarketWatch

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 18, 2026 14:56 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Technology shares have been propelled for months by enormous spending on artificial intelligence. Last week, hotter inflation data and fresh competition worries hit that trade, just as earnings season began.

Why it matters

If borrowing stays expensive, the companies building AI infrastructure with debt face higher costs, and index funds heavy in tech could keep swinging. Ordinary savers with retirement accounts own a lot of this, whether they remember choosing it or not.

Stocks had a rough week, and the cause was largely tech. According to CNBC, the Nasdaq dropped 4.5%, ending a four-week winning streak and marking its worst week since June. The S&P 500 fell 2%, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average rose 0.7% as investors moved into healthcare, consumer staples and other sectors that do not need a data center to justify their existence.

The trigger was inflation. September's consumer price index rose 3.7% from a year earlier, above the 3.6% economists expected. Translation: prices are rising a bit faster than forecasters said, which is the one thing forecasters are paid to avoid. Core inflation, which strips out food and energy, also came in slightly hot.

Producer prices were no comfort. CNBC reported the producer price index, a measure of what businesses pay for goods and services, jumped 1.3% for the month and 6.3% for the year, the largest gains since 2022. Higher inflation, partly driven by elevated oil prices, suggests the Federal Reserve may keep interest rates high for longer. The latest data put Brent crude at $126.55, the 10-year Treasury yield at 5.1% and the Fed funds rate at 3.88%, so the cost of money is no longer a rounding error.

That matters because the AI buildout runs substantially on borrowed money. Broadcom CEO Hock Tan warned Monday that access to capital could become the industry's next major constraint, according to CNBC. Translation: the chips are available, but the loans are the scarce part. CoreWeave, a so-called neocloud that rents out computing power, has seen total debt rise above $51 billion, and its shares fell 25% last week. Its bond yields and credit default swaps, a kind of insurance on debt, also rose, which is how lenders say they would like to discuss this further.

Competition added to the mood. Memory chip stocks including Micron, Sandisk, Western Digital and Seagate fell after Chinese rival CXMT announced progress on a more efficient chip structure, raising fears of a supply glut. The iShares Semiconductor ETF lost ground for nine straight sessions, its longest streak in more than a decade. Cybersecurity stocks also suffered, with Palo Alto Networks down more than 18% and CrowdStrike down more than 17% after reports that OpenAI is in early talks to buy Trail of Bits.

Not every large technology name suffered equally. Meta rallied after releasing new AI models, though it still finished the week down more than 1%. Microsoft closed at a record high Tuesday for the first time in nearly a year, after previously falling by more than a third. Both ended the week down more than 1%, which on this particular week qualified as a victory lap.

Banks offered the earnings-season counterpoint. Goldman Sachs reported equities trading revenue up 72% and investment banking fees up 21%, yet shares gave up an early rally. Wells Fargo beat earnings estimates but missed on revenue, and Bank of New York beat on both and raised its outlook. CNBC's Jim Cramer said investors seem more worried about the economy ahead than the quarter just reported, which is a polite way of saying the results were good and nobody cared.

MarketWatch noted that the idea that AI stocks have been immune to rising bond yields is not quite right, according to one strategist. Investors now have a week of earnings to test whether profits can outrun interest rates. If they cannot, diversification, a strategy that everyone admires most when it is working, will receive its annual standing ovation.
