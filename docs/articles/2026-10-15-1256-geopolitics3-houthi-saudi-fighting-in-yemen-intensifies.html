Saudi Arabia Weighs Asian Cup Delay as Houthi Strikes Empty Riyadh's Skies

The Asian Football Confederation says it can wait until the end of November to decide whether a January tournament survives a war that has closed the capital's airport.

The wire said: Fighting in Yemen intensifies: What’s the latest on the battlefield? · Al Jazeera

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 15, 2026 12:56 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Yemen's Houthis and the Saudi-backed Yemeni government have resumed full-scale fighting after a 2022 ceasefire broke down in recent months. The Houthis are now striking Saudi airports and oil sites, and the kingdom is trying to keep its showcase events on the calendar.

Why it matters

Saudi Arabia has spent heavily on becoming a global host of sport and investment conferences, and those plans depend on visitors believing the country is safe to fly into. Meanwhile, civilians in Yemen face food shortages and overwhelmed hospitals, and oil exports are exposed on a shipping route the Saudis now rely on more heavily.

The Asian Football Confederation said on Thursday that the 2027 Asian Cup, due to start January 7 in Jeddah, Riyadh and al-Khobar, could be pushed back a year to 2028 if needed. AFC president Sheikh Salman bin Ebrahim Al Khalifa said organisers are in "no hurry" to decide, and that they can wait until the end of November, according to Al Jazeera. He added that everything is, for now, "on schedule," which is a position that can be held with great confidence by anyone who does not look out a window in Riyadh.

The window in question has been fairly busy. Al Jazeera reported that Houthi attacks on Riyadh's King Khalid international airport over the weekend killed 14 people and wounded more than 300. Authorities say the airport will stay shut at least until Saturday, and several airlines have cancelled flights. Translation: the capital of a country hosting a 24-team continental tournament currently has no way for the teams to arrive by air.

The strikes are not limited to airports. The Houthis' military spokesman, Yahya Saree, said the group hit Aramco in Dhahran, along with Riyadh airport and a refinery in the capital, and promised more attacks on Saudi airports and oil facilities. A Saudi Ministry of Energy official confirmed an Aramco building in Dhahran was hit, causing a "limited fire" and no casualties. Government-aligned forces also said they intercepted a drone headed for Medina and a missile fired toward Riyadh on Thursday.

Al Jazeera's Yousef Mawry described the logic as symmetrical: air strike for air strike, capital for capital, airport for airport. That favours the Houthis, he said, because Sanaa's airport has been closed for years and has no Yemeni airliners, so Saudi Arabia "has much more to lose." In other words, it is hard to threaten the air traffic of an airport that has no air traffic.

The economic pressure is deliberate. The Houthis have also hit Saudi-linked shipping near the Bab al-Mandeb strait, a Red Sea chokepoint (Translation: a narrow stretch of water through which a great deal of trade must pass and which is therefore very easy to bother). Saudi Arabia has leaned on that route for oil exports since Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz in March, according to Al Jazeera. The kingdom's cabinet, led by Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, pledged not to allow threats to navigation there.

The sporting calendar is already giving way. The Six Kings Slam exhibition, featuring Novak Djokovic, Carlos Alcaraz and Alexander Zverev, has moved to December. Three domestic league matches were postponed this week, and an Asian Champions League match involving Al-Nassr started an hour late after a missile interception. A Saudi investment conference nicknamed "Davos in the desert" is scheduled for later this month, though Al Jazeera said it is unclear whether it will go ahead.

Players are starting to weigh in. Australia says it is "closely monitoring" the situation. The Korea Professional Footballers Association, led by Lee Keun-ho, voiced "serious concern" and said no tournament's value should take precedence over the lives and safety of players and fans, according to Yonhap.

On the ground in Yemen, the stakes are far less glamorous. Government forces launched a counteroffensive called "Dawn of Yemen" to retake coastal territory in Taiz governorate and claimed 2,309 "targeting operations" and 460 Houthi vehicles destroyed. Al Jazeera's correspondent said the pattern since early September has been a Saudi-backed advance followed by a Houthi counterattack. Meanwhile, the UN's World Food Programme says wheat flour stocks in besieged Taiz are nearly exhausted, and the Red Cross reports some hospitals admitting more than 700 weapon-wounded patients in a single week.

The AFC has given itself until the end of November. The calendar, as it happens, has no say in how the war goes.
