SpaceX Buys Old Walkie-Talkie Airwaves, Decides It Would Also Like to Be Your Phone Company

After roughly $20 billion for EchoStar spectrum, SpaceX agreed to buy 800 MHz licenses for $8 billion, and an analyst says a terrestrial network is now the plan.

The wire said: SpaceX’s ambitious, expensive gamble to replace AT&T, T-Mobile, and Verizon · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 18, 2026 01:40 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

SpaceX has been buying radio spectrum to expand Starlink beyond satellite dishes and into mobile service. Last week it agreed to buy low-band 800 MHz spectrum, on top of its earlier purchase of close to $20 billion in EchoStar spectrum, according to The Verge.

Why it matters

If SpaceX builds a real phone network, the three big US carriers could face a competitor with deep pockets and no obligation to be loved. Customers who have spent years shopping among nearly identical bills might finally get a reason to shop.

SpaceX has agreed to buy 800 MHz spectrum for $8 billion, The Verge reported, adding to its earlier purchase of close to $20 billion in EchoStar spectrum. The Verge also noted that the FCC approved deploying 15,000 next-generation Starlink satellites. A rocket company that once said it was in the space business has now spent more than $25 billion on radio licenses, which is a lot to spend on a sideline.

The new spectrum is a piece of the old Nextel network, the push-to-talk walkie-talkie service. According to the interview, Sprint had to give it up as a condition of its sale to T-Mobile, and it eventually landed with Grain Management. Translation: regulators let the fourth carrier disappear on the condition that someone else get the leftovers, and the leftovers sat on a shelf until a buyer with a rocket program showed up.

Walter Piecyk, an analyst at LightShed Partners, told The Verge that this is the moment people realized SpaceX will build a terrestrial network, meaning actual ground infrastructure. His reasoning is simple physics and simple economics. Satellites work well when a dish sits on a roof, but a phone has far less power and often sits indoors. Low-band spectrum travels farther and penetrates buildings better, which is why carriers prize it. Piecyk said the FCC approval of the earlier EchoStar deal also implied that satellites alone would not do the job.

There is also the matter of cooperation. Piecyk said the existing carriers are not going to give SpaceX a wholesale agreement, meaning a deal to rent their networks. Translation: when your competitor will not lend you its towers, you buy the airwaves and build your own. He added that SpaceX earlier floated alternatives, such as femtocells, which are small local cells, instead of big towers. He described this as a diversion, which is a polite word for a plan that is no longer the plan.

He also described a pattern. SpaceX bought some spectrum, received positive feedback from investors, and then went back to EchoStar for more. In his telling, the company learns as it goes, and the telecom market he calls a trillion-dollar enterprise value business in the US alone is a large thing to learn on.

The Verge's host is rightly cautious. The telecom industry, he noted, has a vast graveyard of pivots, bad ideas, and failed acquisitions, because wanting to build a network and paying to build one are different activities. Spectrum is only the license. Towers, backhaul, devices, and customer service still cost real money, and customers still call when the signal drops.

What to watch next is whether SpaceX can turn a stack of licenses into coverage that people will pay for. The incumbents have, in the interviewer's telling, not responded especially well so far. If the new entrant manages to be more popular than a phone company, that bar is admittedly not tall.
