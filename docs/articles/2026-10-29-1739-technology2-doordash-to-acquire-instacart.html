DoorDash to Buy Instacart for $17 Billion, Promises This Is About Main Street

The all-but-cash deal, valued at $65 a share, would merge the two biggest names in delivering things to your door, and is not expected to close until late 2027 at the earliest.

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 29, 2026 17:39 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

DoorDash, best known for restaurant delivery, and Instacart, best known for grocery delivery, announced a definitive agreement under which DoorDash will acquire Instacart. The announcement came alongside DoorDash's third-quarter earnings report.

Why it matters

If regulators approve it, two of the main apps for getting food and groceries delivered would become one company, which affects prices, memberships, and pay for gig workers. The long timeline means shoppers will not feel much change soon, but antitrust reviewers will have plenty to read.

DoorDash announced a definitive agreement to acquire Instacart, formally Maplebear Inc., for $65.00 per share, an equity value of about $17 billion. According to the companies' joint announcement, that is a 26% premium to Instacart's closing price on October 22, 2026, the last trading day before media reports about a possible deal. Translation: a premium is the extra amount a buyer pays over the market price so that shareholders will agree to be bought, a form of persuasion that works best in dollars.

The consideration will be at least 75% cash. Instacart holders can elect cash, DoorDash stock, or a mix, but DoorDash stock is capped at 25% of the total. If too many people ask for stock, everyone gets prorated. Translation: you may choose your preferred form of payment, provided enough other people did not choose it too. The options work out to $65.00 in cash, 0.3372 DoorDash shares, or $48.75 in cash plus 0.0843 shares.

The cash has to come from somewhere. DoorDash said it will use cash on hand and debt financing, and it has a commitment letter from JPMorgan Chase for a 364-day senior unsecured bridge loan. Translation: a bridge loan is short-term borrowing that covers the gap until permanent financing arrives, and 364 days is the kind of oddly specific length that suggests someone read a rulebook. The deal has no financing condition, meaning DoorDash cannot cite money trouble as a reason to walk away, and the company says it is targeting a capital structure that keeps investment-grade ratings.

The companies' case is that this is mostly about helping local stores survive against giant online retailers that build their own fleets, warehouses, memberships, and AI shopping assistants. DoorDash CEO Tony Xu said grocery is one of the hardest things to get right online, citing the ripe avocado and the ice cream that must arrive before it melts. It is a fair point, and also a point that applies equally well to two companies that currently compete to deliver the same avocado.

The announced benefits are mostly consumer-facing. Instacart customers could reach more than 50,000 additional stores on top of nearly 100,000 now. DashPass and Instacart+ members would pay one membership fee instead of two, which DoorDash estimates would save them about $100 million a year combined. Notably, that is a saving that exists because customers currently pay for two memberships, and the merger would make that unnecessary by owning both.

DoorDash also made post-close commitments: no grocery exclusivity, so grocers can use any delivery platform, and Instacart Shoppers can keep their current flexibility and choose whether to take DoorDash orders. DoorDash expects to keep the Instacart brand, enterprise technology products, and app. Until closing, the companies remain separate and independent, which is the legal way of saying they are still supposed to be rivals.

Closing is expected at the end of 2027 or the first half of 2028, pending Instacart stockholder approval, expiration of the waiting period under the Hart-Scott-Rodino Act, and approvals in places including Canada and Israel. Translation: Hart-Scott-Rodino is the US law that gives antitrust agencies time to decide whether a merger is a problem. Holders of about 19% of Instacart's shares have already agreed to vote in favor.

The kicker is the calendar. A deal announced in October 2026 that may not close until 2028 gives regulators a long runway and gives customers a long time to order groceries from two apps while being told they are about to become one.
