CoreWeave's Bonds Turn Distressed, and the Whole Neocloud Industry Checks Its Own Balance Sheet

After a week of falling shares and a pulled IPO, executives expect most of the hundreds of AI cloud start-ups to vanish, which is awkward for Nvidia, their biggest booster.

The wire said: CoreWeave’s debt is becoming the whole AI industry’s problem · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 20, 2026 02:16 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

CoreWeave and other so-called neoclouds rent out Nvidia-powered computing capacity to AI companies, funding the buildout largely with debt. Last week, investors began to doubt how much of that debt the industry can carry.

Why it matters

The AI buildout runs on borrowed money, so when lenders get nervous, projects stall and the companies that depend on them wobble. If a shakeout hits, it could also push compute prices down, which is good for customers and less good for whoever financed the data centers.

CoreWeave had a bad week. According to The Verge, its bonds slid into distressed territory, a common definition of which is investors demanding 1,000 basis points more in yield than government bonds pay. Translation: lenders now want an extra 10 percentage points a year to hold the company's debt, which is what people charge when they have started to read the fine print. Its shares fell more than 20 percent, and Bloomberg reported a quarter of its market value gone, with a measure of its credit risk at a record high.

The company owed more than $35 billion as of its second quarter, The Verge reported, partly because it pioneered using GPUs as collateral for loans. Translation: it borrows against the chips it buys with the borrowed money, a structure that works nicely right up until someone asks what the chips are worth. It lost more than a billion dollars in the first half of the year, a detail that makes the interest payments more interesting.

Matters were not helped when cofounder Brannin McBee told the Financial Times that CoreWeave wants investment-grade debt so it can borrow from insurers, whose capital he described as "unlimited." Investors heard a heavily indebted company announcing a plan to find more people to lend to it. The Verge notes that the goal is not unreasonable, since CoreWeave secured an investment-grade chip loan earlier this year, but the phrasing was not calming.

The Verge's author is not ready to declare the bubble burst, and offers a reason. CoreWeave has $103.7 billion in remaining performance obligations, meaning contracted future revenue, about 40 percent of it due in 2028, with cancellation allowed only in limited circumstances. Microsoft, OpenAI and what is probably Nvidia generated nearly three-quarters of second-quarter revenue. Translation: the customers are few, large, and legally committed, which is comforting unless one of them is OpenAI.

The Decoder, citing The Information, says the real trouble is industry-wide. Hundreds of neoclouds are chasing customers, and two executives predicted only seven to ten will survive a few years. Australian provider Firmus pulled its IPO. Microsoft backed out of leasing 23,000 Nvidia chips at a site in Loughton, England, where The Telegraph reported power would not arrive until the 2030s. Nscale says its Microsoft relationship remains "strong and long-term." Bloomberg reported some investors want to cut Nscale's target valuation from about $30 billion to $16 billion to $19 billion.

Nvidia has the most exposure. Customers outside the big cloud providers, including neoclouds, brought in $40 billion in the July quarter, 45 percent of its data center revenue and more than double a year earlier. The chipmaker supports them with equity investments, chip allocations, loan guarantees, and a team led by Jensen Huang's son-in-law that helps find sites with available power. One executive said Nvidia has already revised its guarantee terms after larger neoclouds turned down the first offer. In plain terms, the company selling the shovels is now also helping the miners arrange financing and find a mine.

The industry is not united in its concern. CoreWeave CEO Michael Intrator reportedly blamed smaller rivals such as Firmus, Lambda and Nscale for trying to raise enormous capital without CoreWeave's execution or scale. Gartner predicts 80 percent of independent neoclouds will cease independent operations by 2030, and Bernstein estimates only 35 percent of announced North American data center capacity will be built.

Then there is oversupply. In February 2025, Satya Nadella said he expected a glut of compute from 2027 and that "prices will fall." That is good news for anyone renting compute, and a problem for anyone who borrowed against it. For now, The Verge notes, some people are buying the dip, which is one way to describe a view on 2027.
