Albania's Prime Minister Blames Foreign Hands for Protests; Protesters Point to Their Own Hands, Holding Rent Receipts

Student-led unrest has spread to at least seven cities, with 152 arrests and 71 injured officers, while Edi Rama rules out resigning and Brussels asks everyone to calm down.

The wire said: Albanian protests turn violent: What we know · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 16, 2026 15:38 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Albania has seen months of 'Flamingo' protests that began over a Kushner-linked luxury tourism project on the Adriatic coast and grew into a wider anti-government movement. This week, student demonstrations over education costs and living expenses turned violent.

Why it matters

Albania is among the leading candidates for EU membership, and prolonged instability could complicate that path and hurt the tourism that its economy depends on. The standoff also tests whether a leaderless protest can turn into actual political change.

Protesters and riot police have clashed in Albania's main cities this week in scenes the BBC described as unseen for decades. According to the BBC, government buildings and some private businesses have been attacked, and the unrest has spread from Tirana to Durrës, Shkodër, Lezhë, Vlora, Gjirokastër and Korçë. Police told DW that 152 people were arrested, 324 are under investigation and 71 officers were injured over three days.

The immediate trigger was a student movement complaining about tuition fees, textbook costs and the general price of being alive. Secondary schools joined, and the crowds grew. Masked protesters threw stones, police answered with water cannon, tear gas and batons, and a floor of the education ministry was set on fire, according to the BBC.

The background is a long summer of Flamingo protests. For some 135 evenings in a row, Albanians gathered in Tirana holding pictures of a pink flamingo and asking Prime Minister Edi Rama to resign. The cause was a luxury development linked to Jared Kushner, Donald Trump's son-in-law, near Vlora, which demonstrators said would endanger the birds. Translation: a bird became the face of a campaign about who gets to build what, and for whom.

The flamingo has since been joined by more ordinary grievances. Samanta, a 22-year-old master's student, told DW she earns 540 euros, cannot imagine affording an apartment in the luxury towers rising over Tirana, and shares rent with strangers because her family emigrated. Her summary of the protest strategy was blunt: "You didn't listen to us for four months while we protested peacefully. Now you'll listen as the city burns." Several protesters told the BBC that months of peaceful demonstration were met with ridicule, which is a known way to make the next demonstration less peaceful.

The government's explanation is that someone else did it. Rama stood in a fire-damaged College of Europe building, a few meters from his office, and blamed the opposition and its masked proxies. He later spoke of "foreign hands" that do not want Albania in the European family, adding that the timing is no coincidence. The BBC noted that allegations against Serbia are unfounded. Translation: when a government cannot explain why young people are in the streets, the cause is often located abroad, where it cannot testify.

Opposition leader Sali Berisha, 82, rejected the accusation of orchestrating anything, then offered his own list of suspects, mockingly: "Greece, Iran, Serbia or someone else." He also said his Democratic Party fully supports the protest. The BBC noted scepticism that he could have organised crowds this large, since his loyalists have turned out in far smaller numbers. The students, for their part, told DW they want the entire political class of the past 35 years gone, which includes both men.

That creates the problem analyst Mark Marku identified. Even if Rama resigned and elections followed, he said, new leadership is not guaranteed: "You cannot vote for a protest." Civil society figure Rigels Xhemollari proposed a caretaker government under a non-partisan prime minister, with elections in May 2027. Rama has said governments are changed in elections, which is true, and which is the sort of statement that sounds different depending on who holds the ballot box.

Brussels has urged calm and dialogue, and the UK has advised visitors to avoid demonstrations. The BBC reported the violence is contained for now, though Tirana itself warned it damages tourism and the EU path. Watch whether the protesters acquire a platform, or whether the government acquires a better explanation than foreign hands.
