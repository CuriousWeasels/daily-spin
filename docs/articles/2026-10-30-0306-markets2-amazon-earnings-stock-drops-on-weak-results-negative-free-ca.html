Amazon's Cloud Booms, Its Cash Goes Negative, and Wall Street Chooses to Be Upset

Shares fell more than 6% after hours as AWS grew 39% but capital spending hit $77.3 billion in one quarter and free cash flow turned deeply negative.

The wire said: Amazon’s stock drops — even in the face of a bold new cloud forecast · MarketWatch

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 30, 2026 03:06 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Amazon reported third-quarter results on Thursday showing faster cloud growth, weaker retail and advertising, and a spending bill for AI data centers that keeps climbing. Microsoft and Alphabet reported earlier in the week, with their own cloud businesses also accelerating.

Why it matters

Amazon is one of the largest companies in major stock indexes, so a market that doubts AI spending will pay off affects anyone with a retirement fund. The quarter is a reminder that the AI boom is currently funded by enormous cash outflows, with the returns promised for later.

Amazon shares fell more than 6% in extended trading Thursday after the company reported third-quarter earnings of $1.89 per share against the $1.96 analysts expected, according to CNBC. Revenue was $201.94 billion, just under the $202.31 billion forecast. The company's cloud unit beat expectations, which is the sort of news that normally ends well, and did not.

The cloud unit, Amazon Web Services, brought in $45.98 billion, up 39% from a year earlier, against a $45.29 billion forecast. CNBC said that is the fastest growth since the fourth quarter of 2021, and AWS operating income rose 55% to $17.77 billion. AWS now makes up almost 23% of Amazon's revenue, a record. Translation: the part of Amazon that rents computers to other companies is working well enough that it is slowly becoming the company.

The trouble is the price of the computers. Capital expenditures, or capex (Translation: money spent on buildings, servers and other things you cannot return), reached $77.3 billion in the quarter, up from $35.1 billion a year earlier and well above the $58.7 billion analysts expected, according to FactSet. Amazon held its 2026 capex forecast at $220 billion. Microsoft trimmed its own forecast to $170 billion from $175 billion this week, and Alphabet held at $195 billion to $205 billion.

The result is negative free cash flow (Translation: more cash going out than coming in, after paying for the equipment). Amazon recorded an outflow of $42.4 billion over the trailing twelve months, a 386% swing from a year ago, and the second straight quarter in the red. The company said the shortfall was mainly due to AI-related purchases of property and equipment. The prior quarter's figure was a negative $7.6 billion, so the trend line has a certain confidence to it.

Elsewhere in the building, things were softer. Online sales rose 2% to $68.47 billion, missing the $69.68 billion estimate and slowing from nearly 10% growth a year earlier. Advertising rose 11% to $19.64 billion, short of the $20.82 billion consensus. Amazon moved its Prime Day sale into the second quarter in several countries to avoid clashing with the FIFA World Cup, which CNBC said affected both units. Even a shopping holiday has to check the sports calendar.

Fourth-quarter guidance of $240 billion to $247 billion has a midpoint below the $244.5 billion Wall Street wanted, according to LSEG. That is a modest miss for the holiday quarter, but investors in a spending race tend to read every small gap as a statement about the large one.

CEO Andy Jassy offered reassurance on the call. He said Amazon has a clear line of sight to attractive financial returns, expects AWS growth of over 40% in 2027 and 2028, and sees AWS becoming a $1 trillion revenue business in five to 10 years. He also said Amazon expects to spend meaningfully more in 2027, which is the kind of sentence that makes the first half of the previous one do a lot of work. Trainium, Amazon's in-house AI chip, will be offered outside AWS late in 2027, a strategy Google has used with its own chips, according to CNBC.

The market's message is a familiar one: growth is welcome, but it must arrive before the invoice. For now, Amazon is a company with a faster-growing cloud, a bigger bill and a thinner cushion. The next test is whether 2027's larger spending produces the 40% growth that is currently on the slide deck.
