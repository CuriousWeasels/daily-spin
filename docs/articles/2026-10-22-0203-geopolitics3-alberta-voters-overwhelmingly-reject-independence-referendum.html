Alberta Votes 76% to Stay in Canada, Separatists Count the Other 24% as Momentum

Unofficial results show roughly three in four voters rejected starting an independence process, and the losing side has already pivoted to removing its own premier.

The wire said: Alberta voters overwhelmingly reject plan to leave Canada in referendum · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 22, 2026 02:03 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Alberta held a referendum on Monday with 10 questions, one of which asked whether to remain in Canada or begin a legal process that could lead to a second vote on separation. Unofficial results from Elections Alberta, with nearly 1.9 million votes counted, put the remain side at about 76%.

Why it matters

The result removes, for now, a source of uncertainty for investors and for Albertans worried about capital flight, but separatists say they are playing a long game. Premier Danielle Smith now faces pressure from the movement inside her own party.

Albertans voted by roughly 76% to 24% against beginning a process toward leaving Canada, according to unofficial Elections Alberta results reported by The Guardian. About 1.9 million votes had been counted, and the agency said the remaining results could take days. Roughly 100 of 1,400 polling stations ran late, which delayed the count by more than two hours, a modest inconvenience next to the political one.

The question was not technically about independence. It asked whether to remain in Canada or start a legal process that could lead to a second vote. Translation: this was a vote on whether to schedule a vote. Separatists were frustrated by the distinction, though the electorate appears to have understood it well enough to decline both.

Prime Minister Mark Carney, who grew up in Edmonton, said Albertans had "chosen Canada" and promised to speed up trade diversification, major infrastructure and an energy buildout with Alberta at the center. Before the vote he had warned that a vote to "send a message" was "a dangerous bluff." Translation: a protest vote is only free if nobody counts it. Conservative leader Pierre Poilievre, whose seat is in the province, told supporters that Albertans were not "quitters."

Pollster David Coletto told the Guardian that voters saw the exercise as a distraction from what they wanted their government to handle, including threats from the Trump administration. He described the mood as a rocky marriage in which few want a divorce. A radio host put a price on the sentiment, posting that someone owes Albertans $130 million for what he called a theatrical waste of taxpayer money.

The separatists reached for the silver lining. Keith Wilson, a movement leader, said at least a quarter of Albertans feel the province needs to be treated better, a reading that converts a landslide defeat into a polling baseline. David Parker, a longtime far-right organizer, was blunter, calling the result bleak and saying it should not be passed off as anything but a defeat.

The more consequential fight is inside the United Conservative Party. The Guardian reported that Smith owes her job largely to separatists in the party who ousted her predecessor, Jason Kenney, and that she has spent recent months trying to please both separatists and federalists. The referendum became a liability, with her party's support falling and a by-election loss in a former stronghold.

Now one separatist leader, Jeffrey Rath, told Global News that independence will remain a dream while Smith, whom he called an "avowed federalist," stays in power, and said he would push for a special general meeting to remove her. Smith, for her part, welcomed the early results as support for a stronger Alberta in a united Canada. The strategy of backing both sides of a question has the drawback that one side eventually gets to vote on you.

Calgary Mayor Jeromy Farkas, an opponent of independence, cautioned that no result will permanently settle the matter, because the separatist movement sees this as a long-term project. About 43% of eligible voters, or 1.3 million people, had voted early or by mail, pointing to higher turnout than the 2021 referendum. Watch the party meeting: the next vote on Alberta's future may be among the people who lost this one.
