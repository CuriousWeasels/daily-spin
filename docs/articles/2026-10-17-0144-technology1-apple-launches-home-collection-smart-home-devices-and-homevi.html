Apple Finally Sells Its Own Smart Home, After 12 Years of Telling Others To

The Apple Home Collection, built with LG and Schneider Electric, starts at $200 and arrives with HomeView, a smart display that critics say has an app problem.

The wire said: Why Apple is finally making its own smart home devices · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 17, 2026 01:44 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Apple has run its HomeKit platform for 12 years, relying on other companies to make the actual devices. This week, at an event in New York, it launched its own lineup of cameras, thermostats, sensors, a lock and a video doorbell, plus its first smart display.

Why it matters

If you already live in Apple's world, the company is now offering a single recommended set of home gadgets, at a price. If you don't, Amazon's Ring and Google's Nest still have a head start and a lower bill.

Apple announced the Apple Home Collection this week, according to The Verge: cameras, thermostats, sensors, a smart door lock and a video doorbell, all codeveloped with LG Electronics and Schneider Electric. It also unveiled HomeView, its first smart display. For more than a decade, Apple's position was that other companies should build the devices while Apple supplied the platform. That position has been revised, and the revision costs between $200 and $400 per gadget.

The Verge notes that after 12 years of HomeKit, Apple's smart home still lagged Amazon and Google, both of which support more products and own popular brands, Ring and Nest. Apple had no device of its own to point to as the Apple experience. Translation: Apple had a smart home platform with no storefront window, and customers kept asking what to put in it.

Kaiann Drance, Apple's vice president of product marketing for home, told The Verge the timing came down to two technologies. Matter, the open standard Apple helped found, provided the ecosystem. AI provided a more conversational Siri. Drance said that with Siri AI, it comes together in a way the company had not been able to deliver before. Translation: the hardware waited until the voice assistant could be trusted to answer a question about the thermostat.

Drance compared the old arrangement to a buffet, where you pick from any brand supporting HomeKit or Matter and hope it all goes together. The new lineup is the chef's choice. The Verge points out that nothing in the Collection does something other Apple Home devices cannot, in theory. In practice, Apple is saying that every feature will be present in one place, much as Google's Pixel phone shows Android developers what Android can do.

The pitch is one-tap setup, no separate account or app, and end-to-end encryption, meaning only the owner can read the data. Every device supports Thread, a low-power mesh network that keeps gadgets connected if Wi-Fi drops, and the cameras keep recording to onboard storage during an outage. The LG lock is only the third to offer hands-free Home Key unlocking over UWB. Apple is also working with ADT, through its retail stores, on installation.

There is a catch, and The Verge identifies it. The lock, thermostats and sensors are Matter-compatible, but the cameras use Apple's HomeKit Secure Video rather than Matter, which the reporter calls the biggest lock-in. Camera history also requires an iCloud Plus plan. Translation: the home is open and interoperable, except for the part that watches your front door and bills monthly.

Research from Parks Associates, cited by The Verge, finds the biggest barrier to buying smart devices is that people see no value in them, followed by privacy, security, complexity and cost. Apple's lineup addresses most of those, but not the last one. Elizabeth Parks of Parks Associates noted that Apple owners are very loyal, which is the sort of demographic fact that makes a $200 doorbell commercially reasonable.

A second Verge piece raises a separate question about HomeView. The device has plenty of computing power and is always plugged in, which makes it look like a natural place to run apps. Yet The Verge says it relies almost entirely on CarPlay. Why Apple built a home hub and declined to make it an app platform is, for now, a question the company has not answered in the sources, and the Verge hosts spent a podcast episode wondering about it.

The Verge says it has yet to test the devices, so the promises of easy setup and fewer offline gadgets remain promises. Watch for whether Apple adds Matter support for its cameras, which the Thread radios hint at, and whether HomeView ever learns to run something other than a car dashboard.
