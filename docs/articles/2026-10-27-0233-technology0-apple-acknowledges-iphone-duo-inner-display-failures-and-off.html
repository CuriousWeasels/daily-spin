Apple's First Foldable Folds, Then Breaks, Then Is Replaced 'On the Spot'

Apple says a 'small number' of $2,000 iPhone Duos have faulty inner displays, and has declined to say how small that number is.

The wire said: Some iPhone Duo screens are already failing · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 27, 2026 02:33 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Apple launched the iPhone Duo, its first foldable phone, last week at a starting price of about $2,000. Within days, photos appeared online of inner screens marred by bright lines, and Apple has now acknowledged a display problem.

Why it matters

Anyone who bought or is considering a first-generation foldable now knows the fix is a free replacement, but only if the defect shows up quickly and a spare unit is in stock. A repair outside that path reportedly costs about half the price of the phone.

Apple has acknowledged that some iPhone Duo units have a problem with the inner display, days after the company's first foldable phone went on sale. In a statement shared with The Verge, Apple said it is aware of "an issue impacting the inner display" on a "small number of iPhone Duo units" and told affected owners to contact AppleCare for a replacement. It is the corporate version of saying the fold has a few creases to iron out.

The symptoms, according to Ars Technica, are bright lines that start just below the middle of the screen and run to the bottom and right edges. Some units also show dead pixels along the crease. Translation: an OLED (the type of screen where each pixel makes its own light) is a stack of delicate layers, and when one is damaged, pixels get the wrong voltage. Some then glow at full brightness forever, and others go permanently black.

Ars reports that most affected devices fail within about a day, though some owners say they opened and closed the phone only a few times. Some say they had not even finished setup. The BBC adds that other owners reported cracks along the central crease and a crunching noise when opening the device. That is a lot of drama for a product whose main job is to be opened.

The cause is not officially known. iFixit has torn down a Duo and, as Ars described it, checked the usual suspects, including the cable that carries data and power across the hinge. The cable has "accordion-like pleats," which doesn't quite match the damage pattern. The Verge says iFixit also showed how the screen might be pushing against a center bar in the affected area. Apple has not explained anything of the kind.

Apple's statement did find room to say that early user response has been "incredibly positive" and that developers are rapidly updating apps for the new form factor. It also said, per The Verge, that most units aren't susceptible and that the issues appear quickly on units with the defect. This is a reassuring approach to a defect: if your phone survives the first day, you are statistically among the lucky.

How many phones are affected is unclear. Apple hasn't said, and the BBC notes that some images of faulty devices may have been made with artificial intelligence, so not every broken Duo online is necessarily real. Replacement experiences appear to vary. One user in Australia told The Verge an Apple Store swapped the phone "on the spot," and a New York owner posted a photo of his new handset next to the broken one. Ars cautions that spares may run short.

The timing is awkward. The Duo is Apple's most expensive iPhone ever, at up to £3,199 for top models, and the BBC calls it Apple's first major iPhone redesign in almost two decades. It was unveiled by new CEO John Ternus, who took over from Tim Cook in September. Ternus told an audience last month that other foldables felt like "two phones awkwardly stuck together". It is now a fair question what a foldable that stops working feels like.

Apple is hardly alone. The BBC points out that Samsung's Galaxy Fold had broken screens reported by reviewers in 2019. Hinges are hard, and the industry has been learning this in public for years. Apple has spent years developing its own, and its customers are now doing the last round of testing.

What to watch next is whether Apple gives a number, explains the cause, or changes anything in manufacturing. Until then, owners of a working Duo hold a very expensive phone with a very good warranty.
