Brazil Declares Lula Winner, Loser Declines to Participate in the Ending

Flávio Bolsonaro lost the runoff 51.79% to 48.21%, and has since surrendered his passports, though not the argument.

Catch up

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva won a fourth term as Brazil's president in a runoff against Senator Flávio Bolsonaro, son of former president Jair Bolsonaro, who is under house arrest after a trial over an attempted coup. Flávio Bolsonaro has not conceded.

Why it matters

Brazil is Latin America's largest country, and a loser who disputes the result without evidence tests whether its institutions hold. The outcome also shows that the region's recent populist trend is not unstoppable.

Brazil's Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva won a fourth term as president, beating the far-right senator Flávio Bolsonaro with 51.79% of the vote to 48.21%, according to The Guardian. The paper notes Lula is the first candidate to win a runoff after losing the first round. Brazil has now completed the democratic part of its election, and is moving on to the part where someone has to agree it happened.

Flávio Bolsonaro has not agreed. NPR reported that his party accepted the result, while he himself would not concede, and that President Trump called the vote "rigged" without offering evidence. Translation: the party has read the electoral court's announcement, the candidate has read it too, and only one of them has decided to treat it as a draft.

The party's statement is a small masterpiece of the form. It said it recognises the result proclaimed by the electoral court, but believes "the questions raised need to be properly clarified" through political debate. Translation: we accept that you won, and we reserve the right to keep talking about it. The Guardian points out that his father also did not concede four years ago, so this is less a reaction than a family tradition.

Bolsonaro's first legal theory was to annul the election. He argued that a two-hour extension of voting, meant to help people who could not reach the mandatory free public transport, had harmed the result. The Guardian notes that his own campaign had also asked for the extension. Folha de S Paulo reported that allies and lawyers told him the argument was legally weak, and that he was persuaded to drop the plan, though he has not publicly withdrawn the demand.

Then came the passports. Within hours of the loss, Supreme Court justice Alexandre de Moraes, who oversaw the trial of Bolsonaro's father, ordered him to surrender all his passports within 24 hours and banned him from leaving the country. The ruling cites only a federal police investigation into possible money laundering involving betting companies, which police suspect were used to launder about £680m. Bolsonaro says the move was based on his brother Eduardo's remark on a US podcast that he favoured Flávio leaving Brazil if he lost. Flávio says: "I never said that." Eduardo lives in self-imposed exile in the US.

The timing matters because of what happens next. When his Senate term ends early next year, Bolsonaro leaves public office for the first time in 24 years and loses the special immunity that comes with it. He is also under investigation over what happened to £20m he was recorded asking a disgraced banker to provide for a biopic about his father. A film about a father's career is, it turns out, an expensive thing to be asked about.

Bolsonaro says he will file another case to disqualify Lula's ticket over alleged abuse of economic power, similar to cases his lawyers brought during the campaign. Having lost the vote and the annulment plan, he is now trying the third remedy, which is to lose in court as well. What to watch: whether he formally concedes before his immunity expires, and whether the passports are the last thing he is asked to hand over.
