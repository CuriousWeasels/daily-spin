U.S. Borrows $2 Trillion a Year, Finds Lenders Want 5% to Keep Answering the Phone

With a $1.992 trillion deficit and the 10-year Treasury near 5.1%, higher borrowing costs are reaching mortgages, while AI giants borrow just as eagerly and investors call it a bargain.

The wire said: Analysis: Why a $2 trillion deficit and 5% Treasury yields are becoming America's biggest economic challenge · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 28, 2026 17:28 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The U.S. government ran a deficit of $1.992 trillion in fiscal 2026, and long-term Treasury yields have climbed about 114 basis points since the Iran war began on Feb. 28. Meanwhile, tech giants are issuing record amounts of debt to fund AI infrastructure.

Why it matters

Treasury yields set the price of mortgages, so the 10-year near 5% means the cost of buying a home and of servicing federal debt both rise. Investors, meanwhile, are being offered high yields on AI-linked corporate bonds, along with a concentrated bet on the AI story.

The U.S. government ran a deficit of $1.992 trillion in the 2026 fiscal year, up 12% from last year, according to Treasury data cited by CNBC. That is roughly 6.2% of gross domestic product. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has argued the country should aim for under 4% by the end of President Trump's term, a target CNBC describes as almost impossible to hit. It is a goal in the way a gym membership in January is a goal.

The 10-year Treasury yield traded at 5.1% on Friday, up 114 basis points since the end of February. Translation: a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so the government's borrowing cost rose by about 1.14 percentage points in eight months. CNBC attributes the rise partly to the war with Iran, which lifted oil and diesel prices and made bond traders ask for more yield in case the Federal Reserve has to raise rates.

The second cause is supply. Investors want extra compensation for holding long-term bonds because they expect the government to keep issuing them. They also face competition from companies selling hundreds of billions in debt to build AI infrastructure. Translation: the Treasury is no longer the only borrower with a shopping cart and a deadline, and lenders have noticed they can be choosy.

Ordinary people feel this through mortgages, which track the 10-year yield. The average 30-year fixed rate is hovering around 7.5%, and Redfin said pending home sales fell 4% in September, the biggest monthly drop in three years. The government borrows at 5.1% and the household at 7.5%, which is what economists call a spread and what buyers call a reason to keep renting.

Higher rates also feed back into the deficit. The Congressional Budget Office estimates that rates a half-point above its forecast would add $1.9 trillion to deficits over ten years, and 1.5 points above would add $6 trillion. The CBO expects the 10-year to average 4.3%, versus 5.1% today. Bessent's answer is growth: 'we have to grow our way out of it,' he said in Dallas. The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget calculates that this would take a decade of 7.2% growth. Second-quarter growth was recently revised up to 2.2%.

Politics offers little relief. Republicans are widely forecast to lose at least the House in the Nov. 3 midterms, and Rep. Hakeem Jeffries, the likely next speaker, wants to reverse $1 trillion in healthcare cuts. Trump, for his part, pledged $5,000 checks to every adult citizen if Republicans swept the election, which is a distinctive approach to deficit reduction. CNBC notes that divided government sometimes yields surprising compromises, but the starting points are far apart.

Meanwhile, the other big borrowers are having a fine time. CNBC reports that hyperscalers such as Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft are leading corporate issuance, and the VettaFi AI Hyperscaler Bond Index yielded 6.51% at the end of September. Hyperscalers issued $93 billion of debt in 2025, against an annual average of about $35 billion from 2020 to 2024. Some fixed-income experts call it a 'generational opportunity.' TCW's Steven Purdy said such highly rated companies typically cannot be bought at these yields.

There are caveats, which the experts supplied themselves. Meta's free cash flow turned negative for the first time since its 2012 IPO, and the company announced plans to sell up to $95 billion of stock as well. Oracle's debt was downgraded in July to the lowest tier of investment grade, and CoreWeave's bonds fell in October. JPMorgan forecast in June that more than $4 trillion of AI-related debt could be issued through 2030. Cerity's Joseph Matus warned that most investors already own these companies through stocks, so the bonds add yield but not much diversification.

So the picture is two enormous borrowers, one government and one industry, each promising that growth will cover the bill. CNBC notes that expensive borrowing is not the same as a crisis, and that higher yields are also drawing buyers to Treasuries. The next test is whether the politics can be fixed before the arithmetic needs it. The first checkpoint is Nov. 3.
