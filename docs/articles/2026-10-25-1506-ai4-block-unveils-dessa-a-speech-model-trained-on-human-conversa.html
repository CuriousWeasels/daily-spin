Block Builds Voice AI That Learned to Talk Without Reading, Like Your Least Bookish Relative

Jack Dorsey's Block unveiled Dessa, a speech model trained only on human conversation, and is asking safety researchers to test it before anyone else gets to talk to it.

The wire said: Jack Dorsey’s Block made an AI model that talks to you like an awkward new friend · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 25, 2026 15:06 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Block, the company behind Square and Cash App, released a voice AI model called Dessa from its research lab. It says Dessa was trained entirely on human speech, with no text data, and can run on a high-end laptop.

Why it matters

Most voice assistants convert your speech to text and discard tone, so a model that hears emotion could make them less irritating. The same ability could also make a voice agent better at persuading people, which is why the safety testing matters.

Block announced Dessa, a speech model it says was built from scratch on nothing but human conversation. According to The Decoder, the company calls it the first AI model trained entirely on human speech with no text data at all. Block says it works directly on audio instead of transcribing first, which is a bit like learning a language by living in the country rather than by reading the airport phrasebook.

Translation: most voice AI is a pipeline. Your voice becomes text, a language model reads the text, and a separate system turns the answer back into a voice. Block argues that step wastes information. In the company's blog post, as reported by TechCrunch, it says current voice AI "turns your voice into text and throws away" a huge amount of information. Sarcasm, hesitation and a shaky voice do not survive being typed up.

Dessa is also full-duplex, meaning it can listen and talk at the same time, as people do and as interrupted dinner guests do. The Decoder says it was trained on a single NVIDIA GB200 NVL72, which is a rack of chips rather than a data center. The team says it runs locally on a MacBook Pro M4 Max "or better." Translation: the sentence is about running on your own machine, but the phrase "or better" still carries a hardware budget.

The demos are where the story gets human. The Verge says the conversations are mostly natural but occasionally awkward and uncanny. In one, the model initially mistakes the speaker for someone named Ronaldo. In another, it goes off on a tangent about Bryan Singer's Superman films during a chat about Christopher Nolan. In a third, a person tells Dessa about a surprise trip to Paris and, per the website, it responds with what sounds like teary gratitude. TechCrunch noted that when the bot said it was tired from work, it sounded tired. That is realism, though perhaps not the kind anyone asked a computer for.

Block gives three reasons for building it: curiosity, better understanding between humans and AI, and safety. The safety argument is that AI is gaining power and independence while staying, in the team's words, nearly deaf to emotion. The Decoder adds that Dessa is not public yet, and the team wants AI security researchers to look for unexpected risks first. TechCrunch's reading is that this likely includes cases where a voice agent could trick the person on the other end. A model that can hear how you feel is also, in principle, a model that can work out which tone will get you to say yes.

Skeptics have fair points. The Decoder notes that earlier attempts at emotional voice AI, including Hume AI's EVI, Kyutai's Moshi and OpenAI's updated Advanced Voice Mode, have not exactly transformed the field, and that sounding emotional is not the same as being intelligent. TechCrunch adds that it is unclear whether Dessa's emotion is steered by tags, as in some rival speech tools, though Block says the emotion is built in. Also, with no text in training, it is fair to ask how much Dessa knows beyond what people say aloud to each other, which admittedly includes Bryan Singer's Superman films.

The business logic is not hidden. TechCrunch points out that Square already uses voice AI to take orders, and a model that runs on-device could power more business and consumer features at lower cost. A payments company has built a voice that can tell when you are upset, which is a sentence that deserves its own safety review. For now, access goes to a small group of researchers and anyone else willing to fill out a form.
