Meta Seeks $95 Billion From Investors, Offers Everything Except a Vote

The company plans to sell new shares to fund AI infrastructure, using a non-voting class that leaves Mark Zuckerberg's control untouched.

The wire said: Meta plans up to $95 billion in stock sales to fund its AI buildout · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 26, 2026 17:43 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Meta is spending heavily on AI data centers and chips and now plans to raise money by selling stock, per The Information and a Meta announcement. The sales span common shares, convertible preferred shares, and a new class of non-voting stock.

Why it matters

Existing shareholders face dilution (a smaller slice of the company each), while the structure shows how founder control can survive even a giant capital raise. It also hints at how costly the AI race has become, even for one of the richest companies on earth.

Meta plans to sell up to $95 billion in new stock to pay for its AI buildout, according to The Information. A Meta announcement, as relayed by The Decoder, breaks the total into three pieces. It is the sort of figure that normally appears next to the word 'nation' rather than 'social network.'

The first piece is about $22.5 billion from selling Class A common stock. The second is $12.5 billion from depositary shares, each representing one-twentieth of a new preferred share. Translation: investors buy a small slice of a slightly fancier share, which Meta says will automatically turn into ordinary common stock in August 2029 unless it is converted earlier.

The remaining $60 billion is the big one. It would come from ongoing sales on the open market once new Class C shares begin trading, likely no earlier than the first quarter of 2027. Translation: Meta plans to sell shares in a steady drip, rather than all at once, which is how you raise a small country's GDP without anyone shouting.

The Class C shares are the interesting part. Meta currently has Class A shares with one vote each and Class B shares with ten votes each, and entities controlled by CEO Mark Zuckerberg hold most of the Class B. Class C, which Meta announced last week, carries no voting rights at all. That lets the company raise money without diluting Zuckerberg's control.

Put plainly, new investors are being invited to fund one of the largest infrastructure projects in corporate history in exchange for a claim on the profits and no say in how the money is spent. This is a familiar arrangement in corporate finance, and it has the pleasing property of making the word 'shareholder' mean exactly as much as the company would like it to.

There is also a circular note. According to the announcement, Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom have expressed non-binding interest in buying up to $5 billion of Meta stock. These are companies that sell chips and related hardware for AI buildouts, so Meta would be raising money partly from the people it intends to pay. 'Non-binding' means they have said they are interested, not that they have signed anything.

For ordinary investors, the practical question is dilution. Issuing more shares spreads the company's earnings across a larger pile, so each existing share owns a smaller piece unless the AI spending produces enough extra profit to compensate. Meta is betting that it will. The sources do not say what happens if it does not, though the non-voting shares suggest the answer will not be decided by the people who bought them.

Watch for the first quarter of 2027, when the Class C shares may begin trading and the $60 billion drip begins. By then the market will have a clearer view of what the data centers are producing, and whether the price of admission was a bargain or merely a very large admission.
