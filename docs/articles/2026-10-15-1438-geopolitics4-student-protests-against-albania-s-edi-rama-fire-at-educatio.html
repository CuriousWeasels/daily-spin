Albanian Students Set Ministry Ablaze, Prime Minister Notes He Was Elected, Not Overthrown

Three days of protests over tuition and jobs have burned government buildings in Tirana, and Edi Rama says he will not resign.

The wire said: Albania: Fire at Education Ministry as students protest · DW

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 15, 2026 14:38 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Student protests began in Tirana on Tuesday over the cost of education and poor job prospects, and turned violent on Wednesday when the Education Ministry caught fire. They come on top of months of anti-corruption demonstrations against Prime Minister Edi Rama's government.

Why it matters

A government that has held power for 13 years is facing its most serious street challenge in years, and how it responds will show whether Albania's EU ambitions come with accountability at home. For students, the underlying problems of tuition costs and thin job markets remain unsolved.

Albania's Education Ministry caught fire on Wednesday during a second day of student protests in Tirana, according to DW. Riot police responded with tear gas and water cannons against thousands of demonstrators, some of whom threw bricks at the building. Police reported around 40 detentions and 13 injuries, including six officers, early on Wednesday, though DW noted the unrest continued long after those figures were released.

By the third day, Al Jazeera reported, protesters had set fire to several buildings across the city, including the prime minister's office, ministry buildings and bank branches. Reuters, cited by DW, said a bank near the Education Ministry also burned. The two outlets differ in scope: DW describes the ministry and a nearby bank, while Al Jazeera lists more targets, which is consistent with a situation that kept changing as it was being reported.

Deputy Prime Minister Albana Kociu called it "orchestrated violence" and said the attacks were not how young people should express themselves. She also described them as the "old habits of those who use young people to mask their own political incompetence." Translation: the protesters are not really protesters, and the people who are really responsible are the other party. This is a standard move in a country where, DW notes, Rama's Socialists and Sali Berisha's Democrats have alternated in power for more than 30 years, so there is always an opposition handy to blame.

Student organizers say their concerns are conditions at universities, the cost of higher education, financial support and employment after graduation. Afrim Krasniqi of the Institute for Political Studies told Al Jazeera that young people protested peacefully for nearly three months, yet their demands were largely ignored, while the government often responded with discrediting campaigns rather than genuine dialogue. In other words, the peaceful phase had a full run and the government did not notice it.

The background is a summer of unrest, sometimes called the "Flamingo Revolution," which began over a construction project on a protected stretch of the Adriatic coast where flamingos live. Al Jazeera adds that the plan is for a luxury resort, with Jared Kushner involved. Rama won a fourth term last May, but has since faced anger over corruption allegations involving senior officials, according to Al Jazeera.

Rama, prime minister since 2013, told Reuters: "Last year, we had elections, not a revolution." This is technically accurate, and it is also the kind of sentence that tends to appear shortly before the next protest. Democratic elections do determine who governs, but they do not always settle whether the people governed feel anyone is listening.

Analysts quoted by Al Jazeera are cautious about what comes next. Sokol Lleshi of the University of New York Tirana said change is more likely from a broader coalition than from students alone, and that elite defection from the ruling class could give the discontent an institutional outlet. Translation: protests work best when some of the people in power decide to switch sides. He noted the current protest does not appear to replicate December 2018, when student protests over education costs forced Rama to dismiss eight ministers and announce the "Pact for the University," though some demands went unmet.

Both outlets point to France, where school students have spent weeks protesting, as a backdrop. DW says the timing is conspicuous. Al Jazeera notes the protests have spread to universities in Elbasan and Shkoder. Meanwhile Albania remains among the Western Balkan states closest to EU membership, and Al Jazeera says citizens are frustrated by the slow pace of integration and the lack of tangible improvement in daily life. The bloc is, for now, still a promise rather than a paycheck.
