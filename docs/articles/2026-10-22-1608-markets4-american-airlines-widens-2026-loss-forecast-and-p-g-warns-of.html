American Airlines Posts Record Revenue, Still Expects to Lose Money, Blames Jet Fuel

The carrier widened its 2026 loss forecast to as much as $1.60 a share and will slow growth next year, as fuel costs swallowed the best sales in its history.

The wire said: American Airlines forecasts bigger loss in 2026 on fuel spike, plans to slow growth next year · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 22, 2026 16:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Jet fuel prices have jumped since the start of the Iran war, and airlines have been cutting profit forecasts all month. American is the fourth major U.S. carrier to do so, after Delta, United and Southwest.

Why it matters

Fuel is the largest airline expense after labor, and airfares are already up more than 20% from last year, according to airline reports and federal data. Slower capacity growth at American could mean fewer new seats and fewer new routes, and so less downward pressure on fares.

American Airlines said Thursday that it now expects an adjusted 2026 loss of between $1 and $1.60 a share, CNBC reported. In July, its range ran from a loss of 65 cents to a profit of 65 cents. Translation: the best case in the old forecast was a profit, and the best case in the new one is a smaller loss.

The cause is jet fuel. American's fuel bill rose 67.5% in the third quarter from a year earlier, to $4.64 billion. Fuel is the biggest cost for an airline after labor, so a spike of that size is less a line item than a change of business model, from selling flights to subsidizing them.

The odd part is that sales were excellent. Third-quarter revenue rose 19.3% to $16.34 billion, above the $16.22 billion analysts expected, according to LSEG estimates cited by CNBC. CEO Robert Isom called it the strongest year-over-year revenue growth of his career. The adjusted loss was 28 cents a share, better than the 35 cents expected, which in earnings season counts as good news. The net loss was $269 million, up from $114 million a year earlier.

Ordinary travelers have been funding some of this. Airfare is up more than 20% from last year, and the company still lost money, which suggests the fuel increase has outrun even the price increases. Delta, United and Southwest all cut their outlooks this month, so American has company, though not the kind it wanted. Delta and United have been profitable rivals, and American has trailed them considerably.

The company's response is to slow capacity growth next year. For the fourth quarter, it forecasts a loss of 50 cents to $1.10 a share, with revenue up as much as 22.5% and capacity up 5.5% to 6.5%. Translation: capacity means seats flown, and growing it more slowly means fewer new seats chasing the same expensive fuel.

Isom said he has great confidence in a return to profitability in 2027. That rests partly on premium travel, which executives said remains strong, and on strategy: new lounges, cabins with more premium seats, seat-back screens returning to narrow-body jets, and a deeper tie-up with Alaska Airlines in trans-Atlantic and trans-Pacific joint businesses. The airline has also named a new network chief this month, part of a management shake-up. It is also close to ordering wide-body planes, and CFO Devon May said the Boeing 787 and the Airbus A330 and A350 are under consideration.

Market data shows why the confidence is a bet rather than a plan. Brent crude was at $115.22 as of Oct. 20. Whether 2027 is profitable depends less on lounges than on where that number goes.
