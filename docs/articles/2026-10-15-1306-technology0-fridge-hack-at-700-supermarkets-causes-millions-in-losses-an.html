Hackers Heat 700 French Supermarkets to 50°C, Exploiting Passwords Anyone Could Calculate

Internet-connected refrigeration controllers from Danfoss, Dixell and Copeland had known critical flaws, according to Ars Technica, and the fix was mostly basic security.

The wire said: Fridge hack costing millions and sparking fires stemmed from poor security · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 15, 2026 13:06 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Earlier this week, a mass hack of refrigeration systems at an estimated 700 supermarkets in France caused them to malfunction, spoiling food and in some cases starting fires. French authorities say the attackers compromised controllers that technicians use to administer store-wide systems remotely.

Why it matters

Refrigeration is not usually considered critical infrastructure until it stops, and then the bill lands on grocers and, eventually, shoppers. The incident shows how an unpatched, internet-facing device can turn a software flaw into a physical hazard.

An estimated 700 supermarkets across France saw their refrigeration systems suddenly malfunction earlier this week, according to Ars Technica. The result was spoilage of what the outlet called untold tons of perishables and, in some cases, fires. It is a rare cyberattack in which the victim's primary complaint is that the yogurt got warm.

French authorities say the targets were Internet-connected controllers, the boxes that let technicians manage a store's entire fleet of refrigerators and freezers from afar. Translation: a convenient remote control for the cold aisle, which turns out to be convenient for other people too. The unknown attackers then ran the defrosters long enough for temperatures to reach as high as 50° Celsius (122° Fahrenheit). By the time anyone noticed, food was ruined, and some electrical systems had caught fire.

According to Ars Technica, the attack did not require brilliance. Security advisories and public research reports show that all three controller models reportedly hacked, sold by Danfoss of Denmark and by Dixell and its US parent company Copeland, contained critical vulnerabilities. These flaws let attackers log in remotely with the same rights as administrators. Translation: the visitor gets the keys to the building and the title to the freezer.

The Danfoss case is instructive. Until recently, its controllers used a credential that changes each day, which sounds rigorous until you learn how it is made. The password is generated by publicly available firmware from the date. A hacker willing to analyze the code can calculate it for any device that has not received a recent update. Translation: the password rotates daily, but the recipe is posted online, so the rotation mainly adds ceremony.

There is also a scale problem. Per Ars Technica, the daily passwords are identical across models in hundreds or thousands of stores. One afternoon of reading code therefore buys access to a very large number of walk-in freezers. For attackers, this is economies of scale. For grocers, it is the same economy, pointed the other way.

Copeland and Dixell controllers had their own troubles. Ars Technica reports that a raft of critical vulnerabilities fixed in the past few months also gave similar remote access. The word fixed carries weight here, since a patch only protects stores that install it. Many controllers sit in back rooms, quietly running old software because nothing visibly broke, until something visibly did.

The lesson, as Ars Technica frames it, is the familiar one: basic cybersecurity would have prevented this. That means updating devices, not deriving passwords from the calendar, and not leaving administrative controls reachable from the open internet. Each is inexpensive compared with a fridge fleet that reaches 122 degrees. Watch for whether manufacturers and retailers treat the next advisory as a notice or as a suggestion.
