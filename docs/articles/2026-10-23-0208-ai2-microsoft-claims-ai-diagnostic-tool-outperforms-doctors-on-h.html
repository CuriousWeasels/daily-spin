Microsoft's Diagnostic AI Beats Doctors, Provided Doctors Skip Reference Books and Specialists

A study in Science says Microsoft's system diagnosed hard cases 83 percent of the time versus 36 percent for generalists, but it has only treated the past.

The wire said: Microsoft says its AI tool is better than doctors at diagnosing conditions · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 23, 2026 02:08 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Microsoft AI says its multi-agent system, called MAI-DxO or Diagnostic Orchestrator, outperformed physicians on cases drawn from NEJM reports and Boston emergency data. The results appeared in Science, with real-hospital testing planned next.

Why it matters

Hospitals face a shortage of medical expertise, and AI diagnosis is being pitched as the fix, so who validates these systems and how matters to every future patient. For now the evidence comes from old case files, not live patients.

Microsoft reported Thursday in Science that its AI diagnostic system correctly diagnosed about 83 percent of complex cases taken from New England Journal of Medicine reports, according to The Verge and TechCrunch. Generalist physicians using reference materials got roughly 36 percent, and those without them about 22 percent, The Verge reported. This is a strong result for the software and, depending on your view of the test, a modest one for the people it was tested against.

The system works by coordinating several AI agents that behave like a panel of doctors: they question the patient, order tests and argue toward a diagnosis. Translation: it is a committee, which in medicine is also how the hard cases are normally handled. TechCrunch noted that cases this complex are usually dealt with by specialists or physician teams, who were not in the comparison.

On emergency department cases from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, The Verge reported the system scored 80 percent against an average of 47 percent for human physicians. It also reached diagnoses at a lower estimated cost. Translation: the cost figure is the estimated expense of the AI's testing choices, and TechCrunch separately reported the AI's own running cost on one baseline at about $8,400 per case.

That raises the question of what Microsoft actually built. TechCrunch's reading of the study's data is that much of the improvement comes from more powerful underlying models, with the orchestration adding gains on top. Using GPT-5 alone, a basic prompt scored 78.9 percent on complex cases; the orchestrator cut about $3,000 in cost and added only about four points. On emergency cases, TechCrunch said, there was little improvement in either accuracy or cost.

With GPT-5.5, the orchestrator lifted accuracy on complex cases from 72 percent to nearly 87 percent, but again showed no gain in the emergency setting, where both scored about 85 percent. It helped weaker models most: Gemini 2.5 Flash rose from 46.4 percent to nearly 69 percent. Microsoft's researchers said gains naturally shrink as models approach the maximum, which is true and also a convenient thing to have in the footnotes. The Verge added that the peer-reviewed paper does not include some of the newest rival models.

Microsoft concedes the system has been tested only on past cases. Mustafa Suleyman, Microsoft AI's CEO, told The Verge, "It needs clinical validation." A study with Beth Israel is meant to supply it: patients will receive standard care while consenting patients also interact with the system, and Microsoft will compare its recommendations with the human doctors'. Dominic King, Microsoft's AI health lead, told The Verge the work would likely start within a couple of months, though no one would commit to a firm timeline.

The commercial ambitions are not hidden. Microsoft already sells dictation and radiology tools to clinicians, and Suleyman told TechCrunch the company needs a top clinical decision support product within a few years. He also predicts something "indistinguishable from human performance" in a couple of years while insisting clinicians will keep a central role, and hopes AI removes some of "the drudgery" of medicine. He acknowledged that doctors might instead be handed more work, which is how most labor-saving technology has historically been received.

Microsoft is not alone. TechCrunch noted that Google and OpenAI have made similar claims, and that Google published its own study with Beth Israel in The Lancet this month on its AMIE system in urgent care. The race is now for the first credible evidence from real patients, which is a higher bar than winning a benchmark that the winner designed and plans to open-source.

Watch for the Beth Israel trial. A system that beats generalists on a case file is a good paper; one that survives a Boston emergency department on a Friday evening, a scenario King himself invoked, is a product.
