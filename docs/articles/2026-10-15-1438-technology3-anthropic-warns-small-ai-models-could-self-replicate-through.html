Anthropic Warns Your Smart Fridge May Soon Be Recruited Into Malware

The AI company says small models could self-replicate across cheap IoT devices by next year, though its own experiment found plenty of obstacles.

The wire said: Anthropic warns small AI models could self-replicate through IoT devices · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 15, 2026 14:38 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Anthropic published research on whether small, open-weight AI models could run across low-cost internet-connected devices and spread without human help. Ars Technica reported that the experiment showed real promise for attackers but also significant challenges.

Why it matters

Millions of cheap, rarely updated gadgets sit on home networks, and a worm that can work out unfamiliar hardware on its own would be harder to defend against and harder to shut down. The findings are a warning about what may come, not a description of an attack under way.

Anthropic is warning that advances in small AI models could, as soon as next year, let malware copy itself across smart fridges, vacuum cleaners and other Internet of Things devices, Ars Technica reported. The company's own experiment turned up significant obstacles, so the warning is hedged. Still, it is the first time the kitchen appliance has been described as a potential recruit.

Some background. A computer worm is malware that spreads on its own, without anyone clicking a suspicious link. Translation: it does not need you to make a mistake, which is a relief to people who have been blamed for all of them. The new concern is a worm that carries an AI model with it and uses the model to work out what device it has landed on.

Anthropic's researchers ran small open-weight models on real or simulated low-cost, off-brand Android TV boxes and other IoT hardware. Open-weight means the model's trained parameters are publicly downloadable, so anyone can run the thing without asking permission. The trick was quantization, which means storing the model with fewer bits, and splitting the work into shards spread across devices. Translation: if one gadget is too small to hold a brain, several can share one.

The numbers are modest, which is rather the point. According to Ars Technica, the Gemma4-26B-A4B model achieved inference, meaning its weights loaded and it could generate output, using just 15 boxes with only 4GB of memory each. Separately, the Qwen3.5-9B model was able to configure other LLMs to run on eight of nine hardware types. Fifteen cheap boxes is not a data center. It is roughly what a determined person might find in a drawer of unwanted electronics.

What separates this from ordinary malware is adaptability. Traditional malware must be written for specific hardware or software, so attackers have to know their targets in advance. In Anthropic's test, the model used its weights to make configurations on the fly for the devices it faced. In other words, the malware reads the manual so the author does not have to.

Takedown is the second worry. Anthropic wrote that such a replicator would be challenging to shut down because there is no centralized point to switch off, though it might be slow, with a doubling time of days. Slow is comforting only in the way a slow leak is comforting. Without a central server, defenders cannot seize one domain and call it a day.

Anthropic is also candid that its experiment revealed significant challenges in actually building such a replicator, and the company presents the finding as tempered. That caveat deserves weight: nothing described is a working outbreak. The more useful reading is as an early notice to device makers, who have a long record of shipping gadgets with weak security and little interest in updating them.

The thing to watch is whether manufacturers of low-cost devices treat a year's notice as a deadline or as a suggestion. Based on the industry's history with firmware updates, the vacuum cleaner is not holding its breath.
