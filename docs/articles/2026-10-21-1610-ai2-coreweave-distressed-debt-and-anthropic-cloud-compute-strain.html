AI Industry Discovers Borrowing Money Is Fine Until Someone Asks About Repayment

CoreWeave's bonds hit distressed territory while Anthropic's leaked IPO filing reveals a $750 billion cloud appetite and landlords who increasingly see it as a rival.

The wire said: CoreWeave’s debt is becoming the whole AI industry’s problem · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 21, 2026 16:10 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

CoreWeave, a company that rents out AI computing power, has borrowed heavily using its chips as collateral, and its bonds and shares fell sharply last week. Separately, a leaked S-1 filing, the document a company submits before an IPO, shows Anthropic struggling to secure cloud capacity.

Why it matters

The AI buildout is increasingly financed by debt, so stress at one heavily borrowed company can spread to partners, suppliers and anyone holding their shares or bonds. If the cash stops flowing, retirement funds and insurers sit closer to the problem than most people assume.

CoreWeave had a bad week, according to The Verge. Its bonds crossed into distressed territory, a label usually applied when investors demand 1,000 basis points more in yield than government bonds pay. Translation: 10 percentage points of extra interest, which is what lenders charge when they would like a word with your family. Shares are down more than 20 percent since Tuesday's close, and Oracle fell almost 10 percent over the same stretch.

The week was not helped by cofounder Brannin McBee, who told the Financial Times that CoreWeave wants investment-grade debt so it can borrow from insurers, whose capital he called "unlimited." Translation: the company with more than $35 billion in debt would like access to the largest remaining pile of cash. The Verge notes this is not unreasonable, since CoreWeave already secured an investment-grade chip loan this year. It is also the sentence that makes investors check how much further the company intends to go.

The mechanism matters. CoreWeave pioneered using GPUs, the chips that power AI, as collateral for loans, and its business is selling computing power, which it has not yet made money on. It lost more than a billion dollars in the first half of the year. Bloomberg quoted MetLife's John Yovanovic saying the issue is not that credit profiles changed, but that "another year and a half of supply" is coming. Translation: more debt is on the way.

The Verge's author, who has previously doubted the business, declines a victory lap, and the reason is practical. CoreWeave has $103.7 billion in remaining performance obligations, meaning contracted future revenue that customers can cancel only in limited circumstances. About 40 percent is due in 2028. Microsoft, OpenAI and probably Nvidia generated almost three-quarters of second-quarter revenue. The first two are the stated customers, and CoreWeave does not name the third. Microsoft and Nvidia are likely good for it, the author argues, partly because a CoreWeave collapse would hurt them too. OpenAI is the shakier name.

Next door, Anthropic's problem is the opposite: it wants more computing and cannot get enough. An S-1 filing leaked to The Wall Street Journal says AWS, Google and Microsoft increasingly view the Claude maker as a competitor rather than a customer, TechCrunch reported. Anthropic says it could spend more than $750 billion on cloud contracts, some still under negotiation. Being a customer and a rival at once is awkward when the other party owns the building.

So Anthropic is leasing its own data centers and using third-party operators, though the filing worries those operators have a "limited operating history." Translation: the backup landlords are newer and less proven, which is the exact profile of CoreWeave. The figures are large. Anthropic reported an $80.7 billion net loss in the first half against $16.3 billion in revenue, with two customers making up about a fifth of sales. Third-quarter revenue reportedly reached $16.8 billion, up 45 percent from the previous quarter, and a November IPO is reportedly the target.

The filing also warns that Anthropic could be held liable for damages from harmful AI behavior beyond its insurance coverage. That is a notable thing to tell prospective shareholders, though it is also the sort of disclosure lawyers require. Taken together, the stories describe an industry whose customers need vast capacity, whose suppliers borrow to build it, and whose lenders are only now asking when the bills get paid. Some people, The Verge notes, are buying the dip. The music continues for now, provided nobody checks the sheet music.
