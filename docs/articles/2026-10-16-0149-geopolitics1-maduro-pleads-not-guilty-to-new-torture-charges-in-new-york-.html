Maduro Rejects Torture Indictment; Judge Enters Not-Guilty Plea for Him Anyway

The deposed Venezuelan leader and his wife face new torture charges in a Manhattan court, as their lawyers continue to argue that a US military raid cannot be followed by a US trial.

The wire said: Nicolás Maduro tells New York court new torture allegations are ‘false’ · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 16, 2026 01:49 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

US forces seized Nicolás Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores, from their Caracas home in January and flew them to New York, where they face drug trafficking charges. Prosecutors have now added torture-related counts.

Why it matters

The case will test whether a US court can try a former foreign head of state seized by force, a rare move that sets a precedent other governments will study closely. It also leaves the trial date unclear, since the two sources give different ones.

Nicolás Maduro told a New York court on Thursday that the new torture allegations against him were "false accusations," according to The Guardian. Judge Alvin Hellerstein then entered a not-guilty plea on his behalf, after Maduro twice answered a request for a plea by criticizing the indictment instead. It is a common courtroom arrangement: the defendant is invited to say two words and chooses to say several hundred, and the judge supplies the two words himself.

The new indictment, filed last week, adds conspiracy to commit torture to the earlier charges of narco-terrorism, drug trafficking and weapons offenses. Translation: narco-terrorism is the accusation that drug trafficking was done in partnership with groups the US government has designated as terrorists, which turns an ordinary drug case into a much larger one. Prosecutors allege Maduro and Flores "created and maintained a violent system of repression," in the BBC's reporting of the indictment.

The allegations are serious and specific. The indictment lists methods including electric shock, asphyxiation, rape, prolonged isolation and starvation, and says three Americans were among the victims. The Guardian reports that Maduro is accused of personally recommending methods, receiving updates on particular victims, and threatening to kill one prisoner's child. Flores, a former attorney general, is accused of helping develop the system and ordering the torture of specific people. Both deny the charges. Flores told the court, "I am completely innocent. I am not guilty."

The torture counts matter legally as well as morally. The Guardian notes that torture can be prosecuted outside the country where it occurred, whatever the victims' nationality, but that US prosecutions of foreign officials on such charges are exceedingly rare. It cites only a few precedents, including the son of Liberia's former president Charles Taylor and a former prison warden in Assad's Syria. Adding the counts gives prosecutors a case with named victims, including Americans, rather than one resting only on cocaine.

The defense strategy has not changed. Maduro's lawyers argue that a sitting head of state is immune from prosecution and that seizing him by military force was illegal. Maduro has previously called the charges a tool for "imperial" plans to reach Venezuela's oil reserves, the BBC reported. The legal question, in plain terms, is whether a court can try someone who was brought before it by an army, and the answer will be of interest to every government that has ever worried about the answer.

Two details remain unsettled. The BBC reported that Maduro and Flores appeared in beige jail uniforms and seemed to have lost weight in detention in Brooklyn, and that Flores failed to win release on bond despite citing heart problems. On timing, the sources disagree: the BBC says the trial is scheduled for June 2027, while The Guardian says lawyers on both sides want to move it from 1 June to 14 June, without naming a year. One of those calendars is going to be disappointed.

The next thing to watch is the ruling on immunity. If the judge accepts it, the torture indictment becomes a very detailed document with nowhere to go. If he rejects it, the court will have decided that being captured is not a defense, which is a doctrine nobody had to write down until now.
