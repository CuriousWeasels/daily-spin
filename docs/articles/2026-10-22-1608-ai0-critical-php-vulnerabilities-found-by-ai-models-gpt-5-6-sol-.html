AI Finds 20-Year-Old PHP Flaw; Internet Asked to Unplug Itself Until Further Notice

Two bugs in the language behind much of the web, found with OpenAI and Google models, are being exploited broadly, and officials are suggesting some sites simply go offline.

The wire said: OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol and Google's Gemini 4 Argon found critical PHP flaw affecting millions of websites · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 22, 2026 16:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

PHP, a programming language used by an estimated 70 percent of websites by some accounts, was patched this week for two critical flaws dubbed PHP2Shell. AI models from OpenAI and Google helped uncover the bugs, which had gone unnoticed for roughly two decades.

Why it matters

If you run a website, especially an old one, you need to patch or go offline, and some abandoned corners of the internet may simply not come back. It is also a preview of a world in which AI finds, and attackers weaponize, long-buried flaws faster than humans can fix them.

Two critical vulnerabilities in PHP, the programming language behind a large share of the web, were patched on Wednesday after being found with help from AI models from OpenAI and Google. According to Ars Technica, the flaws sat undetected for more than a decade. The fix arrived roughly in time for Amazon to report that they were already being "broadly exploited in the wild."

The scale is the point. The Verge reports that by some accounts up to 70 percent of websites use PHP in some capacity, including Wikipedia, WordPress and Drupal. Translation: when this language catches a cold, the internet gets a fever, and a surprising number of local bars lose their dog photos.

The bugs are Remote Code Execution flaws, according to security firm watchTowr, as relayed by The Verge. Translation: an attacker can make your server run their instructions instead of yours. PHP's developers rated the severity 9.2 out of 10, which in security terms is the point at which the advisory stops suggesting and starts pleading.

Hence the unusual official advice. The NSA told organizations using PHP to update immediately or take systems offline, and Britain's National Cyber Security Centre gave similar guidance. Ars Technica reported that some European governments were pulling sites down while they patched, and The Verge noted that Dutch and Finnish agencies did so as a precaution. Translation: the recommended cybersecurity strategy was, briefly, to turn it off.

Attackers did not wait for the memo. Amazon said it spotted an exploitation attempt four days before public disclosure. Cloudflare CEO Matthew Prince said his network saw thousands of attempts in the 10 hours before the announcement, "hundreds of thousands" in the two hours after, and "millions" by the following night, as quoted by The Verge. Disclosure, it turns out, is also the starting gun.

The flaws are not new. Wiz said they have existed for about 20 years, but exploiting them required "an exceptionally complex chain of steps" at the edge of what human researchers could manage. Wiz also explained that language models can automatically adapt an exploit across PHP versions, configurations and memory layouts, which turns an artisanal hack into a mass-production line.

OpenAI says a researcher at Calif used GPT-5.6 Sol under its Patch-the-Planet initiative to find the flaws before attackers did, and a GitHub tool lets operators check whether their version is affected. Wiz found them independently using its Wiz Atlas platform plus Google's Gemini 4 Argon and GPT-5.6 Sol. The fix was coordinated over two months in five phases with PHP developers, Wiz, Cloudflare, CISA and the White House. The Decoder notes OpenAI wants this to become a repeatable framework, and expects similar findings to "increase sharply in the coming months."

The cleanup has a catch: the flaw reaches back about 20 years of PHP versions, many no longer supported. The Verge reports that the Internet Archive was briefly knocked offline and recovered, while two Japanese mobile games, World Quest and Save My Demonic World, are shutting down because developers say their old tech cannot be patched. For abandoned sites, the security update is effectively a retirement notice.

The Verge quoted Wiz's Ami Luttwak calling it the most severe AI vulnerability ever found. The defenders got a head start, per the plan. The attackers, per Amazon, got four days. Watch next for how many sites patch, how many quietly vanish, and whether the next batch of AI-found bugs arrives with a longer lead time.
