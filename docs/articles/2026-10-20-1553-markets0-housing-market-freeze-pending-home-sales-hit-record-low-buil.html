Home Buyers Discover 7% Mortgage Rates, Promptly Discover Their Current Apartments

Pending home sales fell 7.3% in September to a record low as the 30-year mortgage rate climbed from about 6.8% to 7.6%, and builders are cutting prices anyway.

The wire said: Pending home sales sink to 25-year low as buyers flee high mortgage rates · MarketWatch

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 20, 2026 15:53 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Mortgage rates rose sharply in September, pushing the average 30-year fixed rate past 7%. New data from the National Association of Realtors, the Census Bureau and home builders shows buyers and builders both pulling back.

Why it matters

Higher borrowing costs are pricing out buyers and slowing construction, which can drag on economic growth and keep housing supply tight. Anyone hoping to buy, sell or build should expect a slow market into next year.

Pending home sales, which count signed contracts on existing homes, fell 7.3% in September from August and 11.3% from a year earlier, according to the National Association of Realtors. MarketWatch described the result as the lowest level on record, a 25-year low. The housing market has not so much cooled as been placed in a walk-in freezer.

The cause is not mysterious. According to Mortgage News Daily, cited by CNBC, the average 30-year fixed rate began September around 6.8% and reached 7.6% by month's end. Translation: a buyer who signed in late September agreed to pay noticeably more every month for the same house, which is a reliable way to make the house feel less like a dream and more like a spreadsheet.

Lawrence Yun, the NAR's chief economist, said that while job growth and income gains can support purchases, affordability is more affected by changes in mortgage rates. In other words, your raise is welcome, but the bank has already spent it.

Regionally, the damage was broad. The biggest monthly drops came in the South, down 8.2%, and the West, down 9.2%. CNBC noted that the South is usually the most active market and the West the most expensive, so the pain landed in both the busiest aisle and the priciest one.

Builders are not having a better time. Single-family housing starts and building permits fell much more than analysts predicted, per CNBC. Starts dropped 4.56% to an annualized 859,000, according to the Census Bureau, the lowest since September 2025, while permits, a gauge of future construction, fell 4%. Translation: annualized means the monthly pace multiplied out to a year, so nobody is actually building 859,000 homes in a single afternoon.

Sentiment is worse still. The National Association of Home Builders reported its October read at the lowest in 14 years, with buyer traffic the weakest component. MarketWatch reported that four in 10 builders are cutting prices to lure buyers, even as construction costs rise. That is the classic squeeze: selling for less what costs more to make, a business model usually associated with charity.

Ben Ayers of Nationwide Financial said he expects construction activity to remain moribund into 2027, with builders leaning on price cuts and incentives that eat into margins. He added that low starts could be a modest drag on economic growth. Robert Dietz of the NAHB said demand persists but higher rates, construction costs and weak consumer confidence are keeping buyers on the sidelines.

The wider backdrop does not offer much relief. The 10-year Treasury yield stood at 5.09% as of October 16, the Fed funds rate at 3.88%, and CPI inflation at 3.74% year over year. Mortgage rates tend to follow long-term yields rather than the Fed's rate, which is why cheaper short-term money has not translated into cheaper houses.

What to watch next: whether rates ease enough to coax buyers back, or whether builders keep discounting until the price cuts become the new sticker price. Until then, the most active participants in the housing market are the people refreshing listings.
