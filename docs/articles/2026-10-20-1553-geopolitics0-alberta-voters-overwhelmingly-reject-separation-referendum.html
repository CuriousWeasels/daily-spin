Alberta Votes 76 Percent to Stay in Canada, Separatists Declare Quarter Victory

Unofficial results show Albertans rejecting a path toward independence by roughly three to one, though the movement says it is only getting started.

The wire said: Alberta voters reject plan for referendum to leave Canada · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 20, 2026 15:53 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Alberta held a nonbinding referendum on Monday asking whether to remain in Canada or begin a legal process that could lead to a later independence vote. The question was one of 10 on the ballot, and Premier Danielle Smith called the vote under pressure from separatists in her own party.

Why it matters

The result removes, for now, the prospect of a split in a G7 country that holds the world's fourth-largest oil reserves, at a time when Canada is in a trade war with the United States. It also leaves unresolved the grievances over revenue and energy policy that fueled the campaign.

Voters in Alberta have overwhelmingly rejected a proposal to begin a process toward independence from Canada. According to unofficial results from Elections Alberta cited by the Guardian, with more than 1.5 million votes counted, 76 percent backed staying in Canada and close to 24 percent supported starting the separation process. Canadian broadcasters CBC and CTV projected a decisive federalist win, which is the polite term for a result nobody is going to need a recount for.

The question was less dramatic than it sounds. Translation: this was not a vote on leaving. It asked whether Alberta should stay in Canada or start the legal steps toward a future, binding vote on separation, according to DW. Separatists were frustrated that the ballot was not an explicit independence question, which is a little like complaining that the audition was not the actual play, after losing the audition by 52 points.

Prime Minister Mark Carney, who grew up in Edmonton, said Albertans had made a clear choice and that they chose unity. He said the result would speed up efforts to diversify trade, build major infrastructure and make Canada a global energy powerhouse with Alberta at its centre. Translation: the federal government would like to be told it is loved and then be allowed to proceed with pipelines and port plans.

Keith Wilson, a leader of the separatist movement, took a more flexible view of the numbers. He said the figures could fluctuate, but that it was fair to say at least a quarter of Albertans feel the province needs to be treated better. Rounding is a venerable political tradition, and 24 percent is, in the right light, at least a quarter. Members of the movement told the Guardian they would keep pushing.

The grievances behind the campaign are concrete. DW reported that independence-minded Albertans say too much of their revenue flows to the federal government and that Ottawa has treated the province unfairly on energy and environmental policy. Alberta has about 5 million residents, almost half of them in Edmonton and Calgary, spread over a territory almost double the size of Germany.

Foreign interest added a layer. DW reported that Alberta independence advocates held meetings at the US State Department and received coaching from Republican operatives. It also reported that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent called Alberta a natural partner and that Elon Musk posted that Alberta should separate. President Donald Trump has repeatedly suggested Canada become the 51st state, so offering helpful advice on how to make a piece of it leave is consistent, if not subtle.

The politics at home are more awkward for Premier Smith. The Guardian noted that she owes her job in large part to separatists in her party who ousted her predecessor, Jason Kenney, and that she has tried to placate both camps in a province where a majority is not interested in secession. The referendum became a liability, with her party's support falling and a one-time conservative stronghold lost in a byelection. She had said she hoped the vote would settle the question for good.

Calgary Mayor Jeromy Farkas, an opponent of independence, was less optimistic about permanence. He told the Guardian that no result would permanently put the matter to bed and that the movement sees this as a long-term project. Elections Alberta said remaining results could take days. Roughly 100 of 1,400 polling stations had delays, so even the count of a lopsided result ran late.
