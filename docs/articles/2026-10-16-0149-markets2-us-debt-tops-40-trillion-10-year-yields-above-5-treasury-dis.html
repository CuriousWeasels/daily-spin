Treasury Advises IMF to Check Own Forecasting Record Before Commenting on $40 Trillion Debt

A new Treasury counselor called the IMF's forecasts 'messy' after its chief warned that growth alone cannot fix U.S. debt, which sits above $40 trillion with the 10-year yield at 5.19%.

The wire said: U.S. Treasury dismisses IMF debt warning and 'messy' forecasting · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 16, 2026 01:49 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has said America can 'grow our way out' of its debt. IMF chief Kristalina Georgieva said in Bangkok that strong growth will not be enough, and Treasury counselor David Zervos replied on Thursday by listing the IMF's past forecasting misses.

Why it matters

Higher Treasury yields feed directly into mortgage rates, now around 7.5%, and into the government's own interest bill. Whoever wins the argument over forecasts, borrowers are already paying for the debt.

The Treasury Department has responded to an IMF warning about U.S. debt by questioning the IMF's forecasting record. David Zervos, newly appointed counselor to the Treasury Secretary and formerly chief market strategist at Jefferies, told CNBC that 'it's good to remember how messy their forecasts have been in the past.' He was replying to Kristalina Georgieva, the IMF's managing director, who said on Wednesday in Bangkok that strong U.S. growth would not put rising federal debt on a sustainable course.

The exchange has a clear lineage. Bessent told CNBC in August that the U.S. could 'grow our way out' of a rising debt-to-GDP ratio, after government debt passed $40 trillion. Translation: debt-to-GDP compares what the country owes with what it produces in a year, so in theory the economy can outgrow the debt without anyone paying any of it down. Georgieva said, in effect, that the arithmetic is less generous than the theory.

Zervos came with a list. He said the IMF's World Economic Outlook had world growth at 3% in 2008, before the first global contraction in the post-war era, and was positive on Southeast Asia before the 1997 collapse. He added that it was very negative on Russia in 2022, which then barely registered a dip, and very positive on Argentina after a $57 billion bailout, which was basically in default again by 2020. His advice was to 'take it all with a grain of salt.'

It is a fair list, and also not an answer. Whether the IMF has been wrong before is a separate matter from whether 6% deficits are sustainable. CNBC's analysis puts the fiscal 2026 deficit near $2 trillion, roughly 6.0% to 6.2% of GDP, against Bessent's own stated goal of getting below 4% by the end of Trump's term. CNBC calls hitting that target 'almost no way' likely.

The growth route also has a price tag in plausibility. The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget estimates it would take a decade of 7.2% growth to balance the budget by growth alone. The U.S. economy was recently revised up to 2.2% annualized for the second quarter, and the IMF projects 2.3% for 2026 and 2027. Getting from 2.3% to 7.2% would, in CNBC's words, imply an almost unimaginable transformation of the economy. Bessent and others point to AI as the likely engine.

Meanwhile the bond market is not waiting for the debate to resolve. The 10-year Treasury yield is 5.19%, up 123 basis points since the end of February. Translation: a basis point is a hundredth of a percentage point, so the government's long-term borrowing cost has risen by more than a full point in under eight months. CNBC attributes that to the Iran war and the resulting oil prices, a steady flood of government bond issuance, and competition from companies borrowing hundreds of billions to build AI infrastructure.

The consequences show up in housing. Mortgage rates track the 10-year yield, and the average 30-year rate is about 7.5%. Redfin reported pending home sales fell 4% in September, the biggest monthly drop in three years. The Congressional Budget Office expects the 10-year to average 4.3% over the next decade, compared with 5.3% today, and estimates that rates a half-point higher would add $1.9 trillion to deficits, and 1.5 points higher would add $6 trillion.

Political rescue looks distant. Republicans are widely forecast to lose at least the House in the Nov. 3 midterms, and Trump has pledged $5,000 checks to every adult citizen if they win. Democrats' likely House leader, Hakeem Jeffries, wants to reverse $1 trillion in healthcare cuts. CNBC concludes that economics alone cannot solve the problem and the politics must be fixed too, a task for which no one has yet issued a forecast, messy or otherwise.
