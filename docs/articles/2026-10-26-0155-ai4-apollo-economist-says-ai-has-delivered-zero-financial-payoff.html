Apollo Economist Finds AI Has Made Corporate America Outside Tech Exactly As Profitable As Before

Torsten Slok says the S&P 493 shows flat profit margins and flat revenue per worker, despite what he called massive investments and widespread hype.

The wire said: AI delivers ‘zero financial payoff’ to most companies, Apollo economist says · TechCrunch AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 26, 2026 01:55 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Apollo Global Management's chief economist published a note examining large US companies outside the biggest tech firms. He found no sign that artificial intelligence is lifting their profits or productivity.

Why it matters

Companies are spending heavily on AI on the promise of higher margins, and investors are pricing in that promise. If the payoff stays invisible outside tech, the case for all that spending gets harder to make, and workers may find the efficiency pitch applied to them without the efficiency.

AI has not improved the bottom line of any big corporation outside the technology industry, according to a note by Torsten Slok, chief economist at Apollo Global Management, as reported by TechCrunch. Apollo is the private equity firm that used to own TechCrunch, a disclosure the outlet made itself. Slok's verdict, in his words, was that the technology has delivered "zero financial payoff" to corporate America outside of tech.

Slok built his case around what he calls the S&P 493. Translation: take the S&P 500, the index of 500 large US companies, and remove Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia and Tesla. What remains is the part of the economy that is supposed to be buying AI and getting richer, rather than selling it or building the chips.

In that group, Slok found that profit margins and revenue per worker appear to be flat. Translation: the companies are earning about the same on each dollar of sales, and each employee is bringing in about the same revenue as before. If AI were working as advertised, both numbers would be expected to drift upward, and the note says they have not.

The obvious defense is that the rest of the economy is simply slower to adopt new technology. Slok sounded skeptical of that. He argued it will be difficult for corporate America to turn what he called increasingly cheap intelligence into higher revenue growth and higher profit margins. Cheap, in other words, is not the same as valuable, a distinction that has historically been lost on both vendors and buyers.

His deeper argument is about what a business actually runs on. "AI may be a great calculator, but in business, intelligence is mostly judgment," he wrote. He added that AI does not understand accountability, trust, culture, unwritten rules, ethical trade-offs or fairness, all of which shape reputational risk and ultimately drive revenue growth and profit margins. This is a polite way of saying that the part of the job the software cannot do is the part customers pay for.

TechCrunch noted that recent studies have also found AI has yet to deliver significant productivity gains. Slok's note is one economist's reading of aggregate numbers, not a verdict on every deployment, and a flat average can hide individual companies doing well. But a technology widely described as transformative is expected to show up somewhere in the totals eventually.

For now, the clearest financial beneficiaries appear to be the seven companies Slok removed from the sample, which sell the tools, the chips and the cloud space. Everyone else is in the position of a customer who has bought a very expensive calculator and is still waiting for it to decide what the numbers should be.
