Decade-Old PHP Flaws Found by AI Prompt World to Switch Off Websites

Two critical bugs in the language behind roughly 70% of websites triggered government alerts and outages, though no successful attacks have been reported.

The wire said: What is PHP and why did it spark a global cyber-security scramble? · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 24, 2026 14:57 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Researchers at Wiz and Calif, using OpenAI tools, disclosed two serious flaws in PHP, a server-side language that runs much of the web. The PHP Foundation issued patches on Wednesday, and agencies urged operators to update or go offline.

Why it matters

Any site running unpatched PHP, including in routers and other equipment nobody remembers owning, could eventually be exposed, and AI is expected to surface more such flaws. How the warnings are communicated may decide whether people act on the next one.

Two serious security flaws in PHP, a programming language that quietly assembles web pages behind the scenes, set off a global scramble this week. According to the BBC, governments, telecom firms and website operators took systems offline or rushed to install fixes. No successful exploits had been reported as of Friday morning, which is a calm way to describe a week of unplugging things.

Some background. PHP dates to the early 1990s and, according to security firm Wiz, powers 70% of the world's websites, including Wikipedia and WordPress. Translation: if the internet were a building, PHP is a lot of the plumbing, and nobody had checked the pipes in a while. The language is maintained by the PHP Foundation, a non-profit funded by the individuals and companies that depend on it.

The flaws, tracked as CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, were rated 9.2 out of 10 for severity by researchers. Translation: a CVE is a catalogue number for a known security bug, and 9.2 is the sort of score that makes people cancel weekend plans. The PHP Foundation warned that under the right conditions an unauthenticated attacker could remotely run code on servers. Wiz said the bugs are in the language itself and affect versions released over the last decade. Calif researcher Ryan Austin called it "the most serious vulnerability I've ever found".

What changed was the tooling. The BBC reported that researchers at Calif and Wiz used advanced AI tools from OpenAI to analyse code. Salil Kanhere of UNSW Sydney put it plainly: "The underlying bugs weren't new; what changed was our ability to find them." The code had been there for years, waiting for a reader with infinite patience and no lunch break.

The fallout was broad. Wikimedia took down some systems as a precaution, and the Finnish government, Edinburgh council and some BBC pages went offline. The Dutch cyber-security agency pulled its own website, which is the cyber-security equivalent of the fire brigade evacuating the fire station. In the UK, tech minister Baroness Lloyd of Effra told the House of Lords that 32 government services were unavailable and four partly so, none of them large public-facing services. The government stressed this did not mean they were hacked.

Japan's SoftBank reportedly suspended mobile earthquake and tsunami alerts, according to the Mainichi newspaper, and urged customers to find other ways to receive warnings. Orange suspended some services globally while deploying fixes. In Chepstow, garage owner Dean Presley told the BBC he could not print MOT paperwork and had gone back to writing it by hand. Nothing says digital transformation like a pen.

The attack traffic was real even if the breaches were not. Cloudflare chief Matthew Prince wrote that attempts went from thousands in the 10 hours before the announcement to "millions now". Amazon said it was targeted in multiple attempts days before disclosure but its sites ran normally. Details of the flaw reportedly leaked early, forcing the announcement. Experts warn that publication of technical details makes more attempts likely and that the impact could take months to become clear.

The human side was less tidy. IT administrator Adam Mudd told the BBC that sometimes it took him longer to take a site down than to fix the problem. Loganaden Velvindron in Mauritius said many PHP versions in use were end-of-life, meaning unsupported and requiring web apps to be rebuilt. The NCSC's Olly Whitehouse called the event "unprecedented". Security professionals also dealt with false alarms: UK cyber expert Daniel Card said they were flooded with "rubbish intel" about working attacks, and one major security firm retracted a warning that hackers had a working exploit.

That points to the next problem. Mark Maunder of Wordfence said the industry must "calibrate the messaging" because AI will keep finding scary bugs, and too many alarms risk "alert fatigue". Translation: if every warning is a five-alarm fire, people stop answering the door when the building actually burns. The patch is out, the AI that found these flaws is not going away, and the sysadmins would like some sleep first.
