Alberta Votes 76% to Stay in Canada, Separatists Find Silver Lining in the Other 24%

Unofficial results show roughly three in four Albertans rejected starting a path to independence, and the separatists are already planning to remove the premier instead.

The wire said: Alberta voters overwhelmingly reject plan to leave Canada in referendum · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 22, 2026 16:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Albertans voted Monday on 10 questions, one of which asked whether to remain in Canada or begin a legal process that could lead to a second vote on separation. The province has long-running grievances with Ottawa, and its separatist movement has grown inside the governing United Conservative Party.

Why it matters

The result removes, for now, the prospect of a long period of constitutional uncertainty in Canada's oil-producing province, which investors and businesses had worried could prompt capital flight. It also shows that Albertan frustration with Ottawa is real but does not translate into wanting to leave.

Albertans voted overwhelmingly to remain in Canada, according to unofficial results from Elections Alberta reported by The Guardian. With nearly 1.9 million votes counted, roughly 76% backed staying and 24% backed starting a process toward separation. The separatists responded by describing a three-to-one defeat as evidence that a quarter of the province is dissatisfied, which is technically true and also how every losing campaign describes arithmetic.

The question was not, strictly speaking, about independence. Translation: voters were asked whether to begin a legal process that could lead to a second vote, which is a referendum about whether to hold a referendum. This frustrated separatists, though the margin suggests the extra step was not what stood between them and victory. It was one of 10 questions on the ballot, covering immigration, healthcare, education and the province's constitutional powers. Elections Alberta said the remaining results could take days.

Prime Minister Mark Carney, who grew up in Edmonton, said Albertans had chosen unity and said the result would let the country accelerate efforts to diversify trade and build major infrastructure. Before the vote he had warned that a ballot cast to send a message would be a dangerous bluff. Conservative leader Pierre Poilievre, whose seat is in Alberta, told supporters that Albertans were not quitters. It is rare for the prime minister and the opposition leader to deliver the same message in the same province, and it suggests how seriously Ottawa took the exercise.

Pollster David Coletto told the Guardian that Albertans saw the exercise as a distraction. He described a general sadness about energy spent on the vote rather than on what they see as more real threats, including the Trump administration. His summary was that the marriage may be rocky, but very few want it to end. One radio host put a price on the exercise, posting that someone owes Albertans $130 million for a theatrical waste of taxpayers' money.

The political damage may land closer to home. Premier Danielle Smith, who holds her job in large part because separatists within the United Conservative Party helped oust her predecessor Jason Kenney, has spent recent months trying to placate both separatists and federalists. The Guardian reported that the referendum became a liability for her party, whose support has fallen, including a by-election loss in a former stronghold. She said Albertans voted for a stronger Alberta in a united Canada, a line that gives the winning side most of the credit.

Separatist leader Jeffrey Rath told Global News that independence will remain a dream as long as Smith, whom he called an avowed federalist, stays in power. He plans to push for a special general meeting of the party to remove her. So a movement that lost a province-wide vote by about 50 points now intends to win a vote among party members, a much smaller electorate where the rules are friendlier.

Calgary Mayor Jeromy Farkas, an opponent of independence, cautioned that no result will permanently settle the matter because separatists see it as a long-term project. Keith Wilson, another separatist leader, said at least a quarter of Albertans feel the province needs better treatment. Watch the party meeting: the next test of separatism will be held inside the premier's own party, where the electorate is smaller and the ballots are cheaper.
