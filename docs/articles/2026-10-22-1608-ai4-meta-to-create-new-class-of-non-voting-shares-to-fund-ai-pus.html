Meta Invents New Class of Shares to Pay for AI, Keep Control

The company will issue non-voting stock from 2027 to fund AI, acquisitions and employee pay, while capital spending guidance for this year tops out at $145 billion.

The wire said: Meta to create a new class of shares to fund AI push · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 22, 2026 16:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Meta reported third-quarter earnings on Wednesday and said it will create a new class of non-voting shares, with trading beginning in 2027. The money is meant for AI initiatives, possible acquisitions and employee compensation.

Why it matters

Investors who buy the new shares would be funding Meta's AI buildout without getting a vote on how it is run. Anyone who holds Meta stock should expect more dilution-style tradeoffs as AI spending climbs.

Meta said Wednesday, as part of its third-quarter earnings report, that it will issue a new class of non-voting shares to help pay for its AI ambitions. According to The Verge, the stock will also go toward potential acquisitions and employee compensation, and trading is set to begin in 2027. In short, Meta would like your money for the future of artificial intelligence, and would like your opinion on it considerably less.

Translation: non-voting stock is ownership with the steering wheel removed. Buyers get a claim on the company's profits but no say in who runs it or what it builds. For a company that has already decided where it is going and just needs fuel, this is a tidy arrangement.

Meta's press release was candid about the motive. It said the scale of investment needed to compete, sustain growth and expand infrastructure amid the AI transformation is "significant." Chief financial officer Susan Li told analysts the shares will "expand our funding tools" and give flexibility to invest behind what she called unprecedented momentum driven by AI. Translation: the current funding tools are apparently running out of room to hold the bills.

The bills are large. The Verge reported that Meta now expects capital expenditures, meaning money spent on data centers, chips and other physical things, of $135 billion to $145 billion this year. That is up from a previous range of $130 billion to $145 billion. Raising the bottom of a range by $5 billion is the corporate version of admitting the lower number was always decorative.

The money goes to building and promoting sprawling data centers, poaching top talent from rivals such as OpenAI, and building models meant to rival those of other tech giants. Employee compensation is on the new share list for a reason: in this market, talent is paid in stock, and stock has to come from somewhere.

The sales pitch has some evidence behind it. Meta launched an AI agent called Muse in September, which can clean out an email inbox, book restaurant reservations and plan trips. The Muse app shot to the top of app stores and reached 10 million US downloads this week, and Meta also rolled out a model called Muse Wonder.

There have been snags. The Verge reported that a tech YouTuber said the agent gave his address to a stranger on Facebook Marketplace, and that Meta paused Muse's conversation-sharing feature after researchers found chats publicly available on the web. An assistant that books your dinner and also publishes your diary is a useful product, just not the intended one.

What to watch: 2027 is a while away, and the pitch to investors will be tested by whether the spending turns into revenue before then. Until the shares arrive, Meta is asking the market to trust it on AI, which is easier when trust is the only thing the buyers are being asked to provide.
