Brazil's Losing Candidate Flees to Miami, Declares Himself President, Then Edits the Letter

Flávio Bolsonaro lost to Lula by 4.4 million votes, left by private jet, and spent 58 minutes as 'true president-elect' before being downgraded.

The wire said: Flávio Bolsonaro calls himself the ‘leader of Brazil’ after fleeing to Miami · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 30, 2026 17:17 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva beat the far-right senator Flávio Bolsonaro in Brazil's presidential runoff, a result accepted by Bolsonaro's own party and all of the country's institutions. Bolsonaro then left for the United States via Argentina and Ecuador.

Why it matters

The episode tests whether the Brazilian right can separate itself from a family accused of undermining elections, and how far Washington will go in tolerating a claim its own diplomats called 'definitive' for Lula. It also leaves Brazil's opposition without a clear leader just as Lula faces a hostile congress.

Flávio Bolsonaro lost Brazil's presidential runoff to Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva by 4.4 million votes, then left the country on a private jet and landed in Miami on Wednesday morning, according to The Guardian. From Florida he published a letter calling himself the 'true president' of Brazil. Few outcomes in democratic politics are as difficult to enforce from a Gulfstream.

The route was scenic. Bolsonaro vanished for more than 24 hours after the vote, then entered Argentina by land on Tuesday night, boarded a jet that had been waiting at Puerto Iguazú, and flew to Ecuador, reportedly to avoid Brazilian airspace. Translation: he took the long way around the country he says he leads. His wife and two teenage daughters came along, and he arrived in Miami wearing a bulletproof vest, which is a lot of protection for a man whose main threat was a letter.

The letter itself had a short career. The first version, which The Guardian says was reportedly produced with the help of ChatGPT, called him 'the true president-elect.' Fifty-eight minutes later a corrected edition downgraded him to 'leader of Brazil,' reportedly after Trump administration officials, keen to avoid a diplomatic split with Brazil's actual government, upbraided him. It also swapped out an outdated coat of arms that referred to the 'United States of Brazil,' a name the country dropped in 1967. Translation: even the rebranding needed a rebranding.

Reaction at home was uniform. Brazil's three main newspapers called the declaration 'coup-mongering.' The conservative Estado de São Paulo said it liberated the democratic right from a family that had held it hostage. Even the televangelist Silas Malafaia, close to Bolsonaro's jailed father, was quoted by O Globo calling the Bolsonaro children 'amateur morons.' When your allies in the pulpit audit your performance this harshly, the problem is not messaging.

The substance behind the farce is less funny. Bolsonaro is under investigation in at least two criminal cases, including one over an alleged money-laundering scheme worth about $905m involving betting companies. Police had asked the supreme court to retain his passport, citing an interview in which his brother Eduardo, living in self-imposed exile in the US, said Flávio should not concede and should head to America. O Globo reported the escape was planned for weeks, with Eduardo as its main organiser. Federal police now face criticism for failing to stop him.

Washington's position is a study in careful vagueness. Donald Trump said the family were 'fine people' and that he would approve their move to the US, then said he would not comment on Bolsonaro's claim and added, 'I won't get involved.' He also called the election 'very strange' and, per the Guardian, has not publicly recognised the result, though a US cable obtained by Folha called Lula's win 'surprising but definitive.' Translation: the visa is official, the opinion is not.

Lula, speaking at his 81st birthday event, called the flight 'shameful.' Whether Bolsonarismo dies with this episode is disputed. Lula's foreign policy adviser Celso Amorim says it was defeated for good, while others warn the movement remains powerful even if the family's leadership is in doubt. Far-right allies hold seven of the nine Amazon-region governorships, and a Guardian environmental analysis notes congress is dominated by agribusiness and mining interests.

What to watch: whether Brazilian conservatives follow Estado de São Paulo's advice and rally behind São Paulo governor Tarcísio de Freitas, and whether the supreme court acts on the passport request that arrived, in effect, one jet too late. The January inauguration meme, held at Cinderella Castle, remains unconfirmed.
