ECB Holds Rates at 2.5%, Then Lagarde Announces She Would Like to Speak Freely

The central bank paused at 2.5% despite 3.8% inflation, and its president said she will leave in February 2027 to gain the freedom to say things.

The wire said: European Central Bank keeps key interest rate on hold at 2.5% · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 31, 2026 02:52 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The European Central Bank raised rates in June for the first time since 2023 and again in September, as energy prices rose following the outbreak of the U.S.-Israeli war with Iran in February. On Thursday it held its deposit rate at 2.5%, and Christine Lagarde announced her early exit.

Why it matters

Euro zone borrowers face higher financing costs while the ECB weighs another hike in December, and the next president will inherit a French bond market under strain. Whoever gets the job will decide how far the bank goes to defend a currency shared by 21 countries.

The European Central Bank voted on Thursday to leave its key interest rate, the deposit facility, at 2.5%, as markets expected, according to CNBC. Euro zone inflation reached 3.8% last month, a three-year high. The bank has therefore responded to a three-year inflation high by doing nothing, which in central banking counts as a firm statement.

The Governing Council said rising energy prices threaten to push inflation up and growth down, while underlying inflation and wages have so far been broadly stable. Energy price inflation hit 18.8%. Translation: the things that are expensive are expensive for obvious reasons, and the ECB would like to see whether they make everything else expensive too.

Christine Lagarde said the council debated a hike and then decided unanimously to hold. She said the bank is not seeing "second round effects", which means companies and workers have not yet responded to pricier energy by raising prices and demanding higher pay. That is the behavior central banks fear most, and they prefer to watch for it rather than cause it.

Lagarde pointed to two developments since the last meeting: higher energy costs and a sharp rise in long-term financing costs, with the average 10-year sovereign yield up about 35 basis points. Translation: a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so 35 of them is a third of a percent, which is a lot when governments owe trillions. She also blamed part of the rise on bond issuance by hyperscalers, the large tech companies building AI infrastructure, who are competing with governments for the same lenders.

Money markets, according to LSEG data, priced about a 67% chance of a hike in December. Deutsche Bank's Mark Wall expects one, to 2.75%, and calls it probably the end of the cycle. UBS's Dean Turner called Thursday's decision a "hawkish pause". Translation: the bank is stopping to catch its breath, not to put its feet up.

Then came the larger news. Lagarde will step down on Feb. 7 next year, nearly nine months before her term ends in October 2027. She said she wants the freedom of speech to engage on matters that are not monetary policy, because ECB presidents must stay neutral. She said she cannot remain comfortable in that neutrality at present. She did not name the matters, which is consistent with the neutrality she is leaving to escape.

The timing has consequences. Her exit falls before the French presidential election, where CNBC reports polling suggests Marine Le Pen is the frontrunner. Aberdeen's Felix Feather noted the new French president will have no role in choosing the next ECB head. Appointments are made by the European Council after consulting the European Parliament and euro area states, so the independent central bank is chosen by politicians, as is traditional.

The reported leading candidates are Spain's Pablo Hernández de Cos and the Netherlands' Klaas Knot. Analysts cited by CNBC see Knot as more hawkish, meaning more inclined to raise rates, and De Cos as more continuity-minded. Berenberg's Holger Schmieding said the ECB has no mandate and no appetite to fund French fiscal follies, which is a notable thing to say about a country whose bond spread over Germany is at its highest since the early 2010s sovereign debt crisis.

Chief economist Philip Lane and board member Isabel Schnabel are also due to leave next year. The bank is therefore preparing to replace much of its leadership while inflation is high and France is borrowing at rising costs. Lagarde, for her part, got a standing ovation. Whether anyone listens to what she says next, as she put it, is another story.
