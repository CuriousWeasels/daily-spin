Serbia's Longtime Ruler Concedes, Having Somehow Lost an Election He Called Himself

Aleksandar Vučić admitted defeat to a student-led movement in snap elections, with record turnout and a canopy collapse that killed 16 at the root of it all.

The wire said: Elation across Serbia after veteran ruler Vučić concedes to student-led protest movement · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 26, 2026 01:55 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Serbia has seen nearly two years of protests since a station canopy collapsed in Novi Sad in 2024, killing 16 people. Aleksandar Vučić called snap parliamentary elections in response, and the student-led Student List ran against his Serbian Progressive Party.

Why it matters

A veteran leader conceding to a movement with no obvious leaders is rare, and it could change who runs Serbia's institutions, its media and its balance between EU candidacy and refusal to sanction Russia. Final results are still pending, so the shape of the next government is not yet settled.

Serbian President Aleksandar Vučić conceded defeat late on Sunday in snap parliamentary elections, telling supporters at his party headquarters in Belgrade that "it is clear that we have lost these elections." The BBC reported that he added that final results were not yet in, which is a careful way to admit something while leaving room for arithmetic.

The winner, or at least the party currently being congratulated, is the Student List, a movement that emerged from protests after the collapse of a train station canopy in Novi Sad killed 16 people. The protesters saw the disaster as emblematic of corruption and negligence. Vučić and his Serbian Progressive Party, known as SNS, have repeatedly denied corruption.

Vučić called the vote himself after two years of demonstrations. Translation: when a government cannot make the protests stop, it sometimes asks voters to settle the matter, on the theory that they will settle it in its favor. He had served two terms as president and could not run for a third, so he was leading his party in an attempt to return to the post of prime minister, which he left in 2017. The job swap is a familiar maneuver for leaders who are term-limited but not idea-limited.

Turnout appears to have helped the other side. Preliminary figures from Serbia's election commission showed 64.9 percent of voters had cast ballots by 18:00 local time, two hours before polls closed, which the BBC described as a record. Commentators had suggested high turnout would favor the student movement. Long queues formed in Serbian cities and also abroad, in Ljubljana, Munich, Los Angeles, Chicago and Paris, where voters waited in heavy rain. Few political rallies have ever been so well attended by people holding umbrellas.

The Student List is an unusual contender. The BBC noted it has no obvious leaders and that its members are not used to politics. It fielded candidates for the 250-seat parliament from across society, including doctors, actors, academics and sports personalities. Its manifesto promised to purge officials who broke rules cracking down on protesters, stop party loyalists from leading public enterprises, and free the public broadcaster from political control. That is a platform that sounds modest until you list how many people currently hold those jobs.

Vučić had campaigned on promises of higher pensions and public sector salaries and more investment in state healthcare. He also backs Serbia's EU candidacy while refusing to impose sanctions on Russia over its invasion of Ukraine. Translation: he has tried to sit on both sides of the same fence, a position that is comfortable until someone asks which side the fence is on.

Celebrations broke out in Belgrade and Novi Sad, with dancing, chanting, honking cars and fireworks. "Serbia is free," one student told the BBC. "It's a miracle." A voter named Selena Lebovic told Reuters, "We couldn't believe that it would be so easy," a sentence that tends to be spoken shortly before the hard part begins.

The hard part is coalition math, final counts and governing without the experience that comes from having run anything before. Al Jazeera said the movement claimed a major victory, but the final seat tally will determine whether a victory in the streets becomes a majority in the chamber. Watch for the official results, and for how smoothly an incumbent party hands over a state it has run for years.
