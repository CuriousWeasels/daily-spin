Apple Finally Lets You Touch the MacBook Pro, Provided You Can Afford It

The new OLED MacBook Pro adds a touchscreen Steve Jobs opposed, and a fully loaded 16-inch model reaches $14,549.

The wire said: Apple announces touchscreen MacBook Pro with OLED display · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 29, 2026 03:01 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Apple announced its first major MacBook Pro redesign in more than five years, adding an OLED display and, for the first time on a Mac, a touchscreen. Prices start at $2,899 for the 14-inch and $3,399 for the 16-inch.

Why it matters

Buyers who want the new display get a clear upgrade, but the price of memory and storage options now does most of the work in determining what a Pro laptop costs. Anyone planning to future-proof for local AI work should check the options list before getting excited.

Apple on Tuesday announced a redesigned MacBook Pro with an OLED display and a touchscreen, according to Ars Technica, ending a debate that has run for well over a decade. Steve Jobs was famously opposed to touchscreen laptops. The company has now decided that the idea merits reconsideration, which is how large companies say they were wrong without saying it.

The display is the main event. Ars Technica reported that it reaches 2,000 nits of peak brightness and a 10,000,000:1 contrast ratio, and that Apple is calling it the PROLED XDR. Translation: a nit is a unit of brightness, and contrast ratio is the gap between the brightest white and the darkest black the screen can show. Apple says that gap is large. Apple referred to burn-in protection, the tendency of OLED screens to retain ghost images, but did not say how it works.

The touchscreen is less revolutionary than it sounds. Ars Technica's Samuel Axon, who had about five minutes with the machine on a crowded showroom floor, said it is not a convertible, does not fold into a tablet and does not support the Apple Pencil. It is a normal laptop on which you may now reach up and drag things. He tried swiping between desktop spaces, pinching maps and dragging files, and concluded there is not much you could not already do with trackpad gestures.

Other changes are modest. The 14-inch model weighs 3 pounds, about half a pound less than before, and Axon noticed the difference right away. The camera notch has been replaced by what Apple calls a Dynamic Island, borrowed from the iPhone, and the Apple logo on the back glows again. The keyboard, touchpad and ports are unchanged. In other words, the laptop was redesigned mainly in the places where the invoice is.

The invoice is where the story gets interesting. The Verge reported that the base 14-inch costs $2,899 and the base 16-inch $3,399, and that maxing out every option reaches $14,249 and $14,549 respectively. That is $4,400 more than a fully specced version of the previous model, which MacRumors put at $10,149.

Most of the gap comes from storage. The new 16TB option adds $7,000, which The Verge attributed to the ongoing memory shortage. Translation: the chips that hold your files have become scarce, and scarcity is a thing Apple can monetize. The top configuration also has an M5 Max chip with an 18-core CPU and 40-core GPU, $150 nano-texture glass and 128GB of memory that adds $2,000. Stopping at 8TB, a $3,000 option, brings the 16-inch to $10,549, which The Verge noted is $400 more than its predecessor.

Axon offered a more realistic data point: a 16-inch with an M5 Max, 64GB of RAM and 2TB of storage costs $5,799, and doubling the RAM to 128GB adds $1,600. Few people need that much memory, he wrote, though some may want it for local AI workflows. That is the sales pitch in its purest form: you may not need it today, but you will certainly be billed for it today.

The touchscreen finally arrived after years of debate. Whether anyone will use it for anything the trackpad could not do is a question for the full reviews, and for the person who just paid $14,549 to find out.
