New Jersey Prosecutor Arrested in AI Child Abuse Imagery Case, Police Say

Bucks County detectives say a deputy attorney general used AI to sexualize photos of real minors, and a tip to a national clearinghouse led them to his door.

The wire said: “thegentlemanpervert” producing AI CSAM is a NJ prosecutor, say cops · Ars Technica

Catch up

US law enforcement has promised since at least 2024 to treat AI-generated child sexual abuse material as the crime it is, with the Justice Department making one of its earliest arrests that year. On Thursday, Bucks County Detectives announced charges against Brian Richard Fitzgerald, a New Jersey deputy attorney general.

Why it matters

The case shows that AI-made abuse imagery is prosecuted like any other, and that the usual trail of platform tips, account records and IP addresses still leads to real people. It also shows that the people tasked with enforcing the law are not exempt from it.

Bucks County Detectives announced on Thursday that they had arrested Brian Richard Fitzgerald, a New Jersey deputy attorney general, on felony charges related to AI-generated child sexual abuse material, according to a press release cited by Ars Technica. Police say Fitzgerald is 51. The charges are allegations, and he is presumed innocent until proven otherwise in court.

Authorities allege that Fitzgerald took photos of real minors from social media, used AI to turn them into sexualized images, and then distributed those images online. This is the detail that matters most. The harm does not depend on a camera ever having been pointed at a child in an abusive setting. It starts with an ordinary photo of a real child, which is why prosecutors do not treat the output as harmless fiction.

The legal position has been stated plainly for some time. In 2024, after the Department of Justice charged a Wisconsin man with spreading thousands of realistic images of prepubescent minors across Telegram and Instagram, it told law enforcement that "CSAM generated by AI is still CSAM." Translation: calling something synthetic does not turn it into a legal gray area, and the software used to make it is not a defense.

The investigation, as described by police, was a conventional one. The National Center for Missing and Exploited Children, a US clearinghouse that receives reports of this material from platforms and the public, got a tip in July that a Discord user had shared an illicit image. Detectives then used what the release calls digital forensics and subscriber records, tied to the Discord account and associated Internet protocol addresses, to trace the user to Fitzgerald's home.

Translation: an IP address is the numerical label your internet connection carries around, and the company that assigned it knows which household it was assigned to. Anonymity online is often a matter of the username only. Police say the username in this case was "thegentlemanpervert_2160_07833," a name that did not help the case for anonymity or for taste.

After a search on Thursday, police say they recovered prompts and scripts used to create AI-generated abuse material with an AI program. They also say they found Discord chat logs in which Fitzgerald allegedly admitted making multiple illicit images with artificial intelligence and sharing them with other users. Ars Technica's report does not say which AI program was involved, and the sources provided do not describe the next steps in the case.

The practical lesson is for everyone who builds, hosts or regulates these tools. Detection relied on a platform report, a clearinghouse and old-fashioned records requests, not on any special AI-detecting machinery. For now, the most reliable safeguard remains a person noticing something and reporting it. What to watch is the court process, and whether the case prompts questions about how the tools involved are policed.
