China Grows 4.5%, Mostly by Selling Things to People Who Are Not Chinese

Third-quarter growth beat forecasts on record exports, while the property slump, weak shoppers and falling factory margins stayed home.

The wire said: China's third-quarter economic growth quickens to 4.5% as exports surge, beating estimates · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 19, 2026 17:49 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

China's economy grew 4.5% in the third quarter from a year earlier, up from a three-year low of 4.3% in the second quarter. Beijing's official target for the year is a range of 4.5% to 5%.

Why it matters

An economy that depends on overseas buyers is exposed to trading partners who are already uneasy about the flood of Chinese goods. Weak domestic demand also means less Chinese appetite for everyone else's products.

China's economy expanded 4.5% in the third quarter compared with a year earlier, CNBC reported, edging past the 4.4% that economists polled by Reuters had expected. The figure is better than the 4.3% of the previous quarter and lands at the bottom edge of Beijing's target. It is the sort of result that counts as a triumph when the target is a range and you have just reached it.

The engine was exports. China's monthly trade surplus hit a record $133.5 billion, which is what happens when a country sells far more than it buys. Translation: trade surplus means the rest of the world handed over more money for Chinese goods than China handed back for theirs. Tianchen Xu of the Economist Intelligence Unit called the external sector "ever-strong," and also credited better government investment and a weak comparison base. Translation: weak base means last year was bad enough that beating it is easy.

The domestic economy was less enthusiastic. Retail sales rose 1.1% in September, which beat a forecast of 0.6% and is still the kind of growth you have to squint at. Industrial output grew 5.5%, exactly as predicted. Urban unemployment was 5.2%.

Then there is property. Fixed asset investment in urban areas fell 7.2% over the first nine months, and real estate development investment fell 20.6%, a deeper drop than the 19.9% through August. Sheana Yue of Oxford Economics rejected the idea of green shoots, saying the data gives little sign that the sector is bottoming out. For households that keep much of their wealth in housing, this is less a statistic than a slowly shrinking savings account.

Prices tell a similar story. Producer prices rose 5% in September, the fastest pace in over four years, while consumer inflation stayed below 1%. Translation: factories are paying more for inputs but cannot charge customers more, so the difference comes out of their profits. Yue said firms may have to absorb the cost shock through lower margins.

Cornell's Eswar Prasad warned that decent headline growth could make the government "more sanguine," meaning it may skip stimulus and the reforms that should accompany it. Economists mostly expect the People's Bank of China to leave benchmark lending rates unchanged this year, so fiscal measures will do the heavy lifting. Those include targeted rate cuts and mortgage subsidies announced in late September, and 550 billion yuan of unused debt quota to ease local government strains.

Beijing now has a growth number that looks fine and an economy that looks lopsided. The numbers give officials an excuse to wait, and the trading partners absorbing the export surge an excuse to get cross. Watch which reaction arrives first.
