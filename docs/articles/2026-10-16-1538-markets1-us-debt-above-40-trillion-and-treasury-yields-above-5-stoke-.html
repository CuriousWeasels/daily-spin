Treasury Responds to IMF Debt Warning by Auditing the IMF's Old Homework

A new Treasury counselor says the Fund's forecasts are 'messy' as the U.S. runs a roughly $2 trillion deficit and the 10-year yield sits at 5.19%.

The wire said: U.S. Treasury dismisses IMF debt warning and 'messy' forecasting · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 16, 2026 15:38 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

IMF chief Kristalina Georgieva warned in Bangkok that strong U.S. growth alone will not make mounting federal debt sustainable, answering Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's claim that America can 'grow our way out.' David Zervos, newly appointed counselor to Bessent, replied on Thursday that the IMF's forecasting record deserves a grain of salt.

Why it matters

Higher long-term yields feed directly into mortgage rates, now around 7.5%, and into the interest bill on federal debt. How Washington answers the warnings affects borrowing costs for households and the government alike.

The U.S. Treasury has answered a warning from the International Monetary Fund about American debt by questioning the International Monetary Fund's forecasting. David Zervos, a former Jefferies market strategist who is now counselor to the Treasury Secretary, told CNBC by email that it is 'good to remember how messy their forecasts have been in the past.' It is a classic rebuttal: the smoke detector has gone off before when there was no fire, and has also missed some fires.

Zervos supplied a list. According to CNBC, he said the IMF's outlook in 2008 had world growth at 3% before a global contraction, that it was positive on Southeast Asia before the 1997 collapse, and that it was gloomy on Russia in 2022 when Russia barely dipped. He also cited Argentina, which was in effect back in default by 2020 after a $57 billion bailout. Translation: the referee has made bad calls, so the foul count should be disregarded.

The argument has a structure worth noticing. It attacks the messenger's record, not the arithmetic. And the arithmetic, per CNBC's analysis, is unflattering. The deficit for fiscal 2026 is headed for about $2 trillion, or 6.0 to 6.2% of GDP, based on the Congressional Budget Office's $1.993 trillion estimate. Bessent has argued for a deficit below 4% of GDP by the end of Trump's term, and CNBC's analysis says there is almost no way that happens.

Then there is the growth plan. Bessent said in Dallas last month that the country must 'grow our way out' of its debt, with AI as a key engine. The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget calculates it would take a decade of 7.2% growth to balance the budget through growth alone. Second-quarter GDP was just revised up to 2.2% annualized. Translation: the plan requires growth more than three times the current pace, sustained for ten years.

Meanwhile the bond market is voting in real time. The 10-year Treasury yield is 5.19%, up 123 basis points (hundredths of a percentage point) since the end of February, when the war with Iran began and oil and diesel prices jumped. Brent crude is at $126.55. Investors also want extra pay for holding long bonds because the government keeps issuing them, while companies borrow hundreds of billions to build AI infrastructure and compete for the same money.

Households feel this through mortgages. The average 30-year rate is about 7.5%, and Redfin said pending home sales fell 4% in September, the largest monthly drop in three years. The CBO estimates that if rates run a half-point above its forecast, ten-year deficits grow by $1.9 trillion; a 1.5-point overshoot adds $6 trillion. The CBO expects the 10-year to average 4.3%, against 5.3% today.

Politics offers little relief. CNBC reports Republicans are widely forecast to lose at least the House in the Nov. 3 midterms, Trump has pledged $5,000 checks to adult citizens if Republicans sweep, and Rep. Hakeem Jeffries wants to reverse $1 trillion in healthcare cuts. The U.S. is not alone: France is debating its 2027 budget with borrowing costs at a near 25-year high, and global debt topped $365 trillion in the first half of the year, according to the Institute of International Finance.

CNBC's analysis does offer one calming note: expensive borrowing is not the same as a crisis, and higher yields also attract buyers. The IMF may well have been wrong before. The test now is whether the Treasury's own forecast, which is that growth will fix it, ends up in the next list of messy ones.
