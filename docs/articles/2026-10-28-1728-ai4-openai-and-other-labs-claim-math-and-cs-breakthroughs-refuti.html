OpenAI Solves Three Famous Problems, Then Argues With Scientists About Who Gets to Read the Answers

OpenAI and Anthropic say their models broke a core assumption about computational difficulty, and researchers are as irritated by the release plan as they are impressed by the math.

The wire said: Frontier AI labs say their models refuted the Exponential Time Hypothesis. What does that mean? · TechCrunch AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 28, 2026 17:28 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

In early October, OpenAI dumped hundreds of AI-generated preprints on mathematicians, who are still working through them. This week it announced results in mathematics, computer science and physics, each with a different release plan.

Why it matters

If the claim about the Exponential Time Hypothesis holds up, many papers built on it will need rechecking, and the fight over who gets early access will shape how science handles AI-generated discoveries. The cryptography question, which affects everyone's online security, was reportedly checked first.

OpenAI announced on Monday that its models produced results on three long-unsolved problems, according to The Verge and TechCrunch. One of them appears to refute the Exponential Time Hypothesis, or ETH, a long-standing assumption in computer science. Anthropic then released its own refutation of ETH, after one of its mathematicians, Levent Alpöge, suggested OpenAI had relied on pre-existing work. The race to be first to disprove a thing now includes a race to be first to have been second.

Some background. ETH was published in 1999 by Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi, and it says that for a certain class of tricky logic puzzles, even the best algorithm needs exponentially more time as the puzzle grows. Translation: doubling the size of the problem does far worse than doubling the work, and it keeps getting worse. The hypothesis is evidence for P not equaling NP, the famous question of whether any problem that is easy to check is also easy to solve.

MIT professor Ryan Williams offered the Sudoku version. A finished grid is quick to verify, but finding the answer takes effort, and cryptography depends on that gap. According to TechCrunch, the labs claim algorithms that run in sub-exponential time, meaning faster than previously believed possible. Williams said people should put a little more weight on P = NP being plausible, but also doubted that OpenAI's algorithm is the best possible one.

The practical worry is cryptography. Williams said he asked OpenAI science lead Dan Roberts whether any crypto systems were now insecure. The answer, per Williams, was that the company checked and was reasonably confident nothing breaks. Translation: someone asked whether the internet's locks still work, and the reply was reasonably confident. Readers may take what comfort they can from the adverb.

The other two results are a purported resolution of the Kakeya conjecture in all dimensions, and a physics result tied to quantum chromodynamics and Yang-Mills theory. Brown mathematician Brendan Hassett told The Verge the Kakeya claim looks well supported, though he had not checked the Lean formalization. Translation: Lean is software that verifies proofs mechanically, so a human does not have to simply trust anyone's confidence. OpenAI says its work does not address the unsolved mass gap in Yang-Mills.

Then came the release plan, and here the mood soured. Mathematics got a GitHub dump. Computer science got a working group, with Williams, Impagliazzo and Paturi, and a request that OpenAI fund a conference. Physics got nothing yet, as OpenAI said it would defer to outside experts on how to release it. OpenAI calls these experts shepherds, a word that implies there is a flock.

Researchers told The Verge they were unimpressed. Physicist Andreas Karch called the handpicked group a disaster and said large labs should not be picking winners and losers. Constantin Kogler of the Institute for Advanced Study said it makes no sense for only a few distinguished scientists to look first. The Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence, which OpenAI had pledged to consult, warned against this practice a day earlier. It then noted that, by OpenAI's own account, the Lean formalization is finished, so there is no scientific reason to wait.

To be fair, nobody has found a perfect method. Stanford mathematician Jared Duker Lichtman said that for any approach someone will be upset, and the task is finding the least offensive option. Kogler even welcomed three results over hundreds. Williams, who earlier called a previous proof dump a DDOS attack on mathematicians, agreed to collaborate anyway, partly because the ideas matter too much to ignore.

What to watch: whether the physics proof is published, whether the ETH refutation survives scrutiny at the conference Williams wants, and how many papers must be revisited if it does. Scientists have apparently found that the only thing harder than solving a Millennium Prize problem is agreeing on the distribution list.
