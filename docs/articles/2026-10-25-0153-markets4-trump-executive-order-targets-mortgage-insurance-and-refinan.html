Trump Order Targets Mortgage Insurance and Refinance Fees, Twelve Days Before Voters Weigh In

The executive order would cancel mortgage insurance automatically on some future loans and make a title-insurance waiver permanent, while the 30-year rate sits at 7.32%.

Catch up

President Trump signed an order titled "Decreasing Housing Costs for the American People," directing federal agencies to cancel mortgage insurance proactively on eligible future loans and to make a cheaper refinancing pilot permanent. It follows earlier orders aimed at keeping Wall Street firms from buying single-family homes.

Why it matters

Housing costs are driven mostly by home prices and mortgage rates, which this order does not touch. The savings are real but limited to closing costs and insurance premiums, and only for some borrowers.

President Trump has signed an executive order meant to lower the cost of owning a home, CNBC reported, less than two weeks before the Nov. 3 midterm elections. The order tells federal agencies to cancel mortgage insurance automatically on certain future loans and to expand cheaper refinancing. The timing is, in the strictest sense, a coincidence the White House has not been asked to rule out.

The problem it addresses is real. According to the Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances, the monthly payment on a 30-year fixed mortgage for a median-priced home took roughly 33% of the median family's income in 2025, up from 26% in 2022. Translation: a third of the paycheck now goes to the house before anyone has bought a couch.

Start with mortgage insurance. Conventional loans usually require private mortgage insurance, or PMI, when the down payment is under 20%. Translation: if you can't prove you're unlikely to default, you pay a company to bet on whether you will. The order directs the Federal Housing Finance Agency to create a pilot program that cancels it "proactively" once equity reaches a set threshold, but only for mortgages originated after the pilot launches. Existing borrowers are invited to admire it from outside.

Borrowers can already shed PMI. Fannie Mae says you can ask your servicer once your balance reaches 80% of the original value, and servicers may drop it automatically at 78%. The change, then, is mostly that the cancellation would happen without anyone having to ask. The cost of not asking is not trivial: PMI typically runs 0.2% to 2% of the loan per year, and Freddie Mac's calculator put it at $351 a month on a $600,000 home with 10% down at 7.3%.

The insurance industry reacted warmly. Seth Appleton, president of U.S. Mortgage Insurers, said the sector is "proud to already be delivering on that goal." It is an elegant response: applaud the policy and note that it was already happening, a stance available only to those whose product is the subject of the order.

The second piece makes permanent an FHFA pilot that waives the lender's title insurance policy on refinances and allows what the White House calls cost-friendly alternatives. The pilot was scheduled to run through November 2027 and was limited to refinances with a loan-to-value ratio below 80%, in a handful of states, according to former FHFA director Sandra Thompson. She estimated savings of $500 to $1,500 in closing costs. An Urban Institute report found that title-related fees are generally the largest share of closing costs.

Pulte, the current FHFA director, wrote on X that "millions of Americans will save thousands and thousands of dollars." Keith Gumbinger of HSH was more cautious, saying it is hard to know the consumer impact without more details. He also said the order is not necessarily "a game-changer," since housing costs are dictated by home prices and mortgage rates, though "every little bit certainly helps."

Those two things are not improving. Freddie Mac put the 30-year rate at 7.32% as of Oct. 23, and the 10-year Treasury yield, the usual benchmark for mortgage pricing, was 4.96% on Oct. 22. Refinance demand was down 59% in mid-October from a year earlier. Cheaper refinancing is a fine thing to offer, though fewer people have a reason to refinance at 7.3%.

The thing to watch is the fine print. The pilot programs have yet to be designed, and the thresholds and eligibility rules will determine whether this saves borrowers thousands or merely a pleasant paragraph on a closing statement.
