DoorDash Poised to Buy Instacart, Consolidating Humanity's Ability to Outsource Groceries

CNBC's sources say a roughly $17 billion deal could be announced Thursday, giving DoorDash a bigger grocery footprint and regulators something to read.

The wire said: DoorDash's deal to buy Instacart coming as early as Thursday morning, sources tell CNBC · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 29, 2026 03:01 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The Information reported last week that Instacart was exploring a sale and had talked with Uber and DoorDash. By Monday, several outlets said DoorDash was in advanced talks at a valuation of about $17 billion.

Why it matters

If the deal closes, two of the biggest names in app-based delivery would become one, which could affect fees, grocer bargaining power and prices for anyone who pays someone else to carry their milk. Antitrust reviewers will decide how much of that happens.

DoorDash's bid to buy Instacart could be announced as soon as Thursday morning, according to sources who spoke to CNBC. Instacart shares rose about 4% in extended trading on Wednesday, while DoorDash fell less than 1%. Translation: investors think the seller is getting a good price and the buyer is merely getting a lot of lettuce.

The reported price is about $17 billion, a figure several outlets attached to the talks on Monday. Neither company has confirmed it. Instacart declined to comment, and DoorDash had not responded to CNBC at the time of publication, which is the traditional way of saying a deal is real but not yet legally discussable.

The logic is straightforward. DoorDash began in restaurant delivery and has spent years adding retailers, including Sephora, Macy's, Staples and Anthropologie, plus a growing list of regional grocers after a partnership with Kroger last year. Instacart is the company that already does the grocery part at scale. Buying it is faster than building it, and considerably faster than asking customers to stop wanting avocados.

Instacart's side of the story is less sunny. It went public in 2023 after riding the pandemic surge in grocery delivery, but CNBC reports it has struggled to hold that momentum. In December it agreed to pay $60 million to settle Federal Trade Commission allegations that it misled users with false advertising and used what the agency called unlawful subscription enrollment practices. Translation: the FTC said some customers signed up for things more easily than they could understand them.

Then there is the competition. Amazon, after slowing its brick-and-mortar grocery effort, has pushed harder into online groceries and uses its fulfillment network for faster delivery. Walmart has also moved into same- and next-day service. Both threaten Instacart's model of having a person shop for you within the hour, which is convenient but expensive to staff. A sale to a larger partner looks less like a victory lap and more like a lifeboat with a very good valuation.

The remaining question is regulators. Analysts have said the combination could draw antitrust scrutiny, given overlapping markets. Wedbush analysts wrote that third-party delivery platforms remain a minority of total grocery consumption, but that viewing the market more narrowly, or from the grocers' perspective as enterprise customers, can make approvals more challenging. Translation: the merging companies will say they compete with Walmart and Amazon, and the regulators may ask whether that is the right comparison.

Markets, for what it is worth, are not panicking over any of it. The S&P 500 tracker SPY stood at 772.31 on Wednesday, and the VIX, Wall Street's fear gauge, was 17.52 on Tuesday. Brent crude at $123.10 has apparently already supplied the week's anxiety quota, and consumers paying for delivery fees may notice the cost of getting groceries is not the only thing rising.

Watch Thursday morning for an announcement and then the long wait for review. The deal will be judged on whether groceries are a delivery market or just a very large market with deliveries in it.
