Chinese Hackers Use AI Agents to Rummage Through 2,250 Organizations at Once

Microsoft says state-aligned attackers stole credentials from ConnectWise's cloud backup service and tried nearly 3,000 sign-ins a minute.

The wire said: Chinese hackers spied on thousands of companies after breaching IT giant ConnectWise · TechCrunch AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 23, 2026 02:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

ConnectWise makes IT management and remote access software, and its cloud backup service connects to customers' Microsoft-hosted environments. Microsoft researchers say China state-aligned hackers broke into that backup service, TechCrunch reported.

Why it matters

When a vendor that holds the keys to thousands of companies is breached, those companies are exposed whether or not their own security was any good. It also suggests AI tools are letting attackers work faster than defenders can notice.

Chinese state-aligned hackers broke into ConnectWise's cloud backup service and used stolen credentials to access around 2,250 organizations, according to Microsoft security researchers cited by TechCrunch. ConnectWise sells IT management and remote access software, which is to say it is the company other companies trust with the master keys. This is the sort of business where a bad day is rarely confined to one customer.

The mechanism is simple. The hackers took application credentials, the digital passes that let ConnectWise's backup service talk to its customers' Microsoft-hosted environments. Translation: instead of picking the lock on each company's front door, they stole the delivery driver's key ring. With those credentials, according to Microsoft, they could log in as if they were authorized users and read emails and other files.

The speed is where the story turns. Microsoft said the attackers used AI agents, meaning software that carries out tasks on its own, to try almost 3,000 sign-ins per minute. Microsoft said that pace leaves defenders "little time to detect and respond." Human security teams, who are famously well rested and fully staffed, are presumably thrilled.

The software involved was ARTEX, an open source AI framework that works with AI models to stress-test cyber defenses. Translation: it is a tool built to check whether security holds up, which is also a fine way to find out that it does not. According to TechCrunch, the same software was used in a recent compromise of South Korea's financial systems, many of which had weak security and lacked multi-factor authentication. That is the extra login step most people know as the text message code they resent.

The sources do not say what the hackers took beyond access to emails and files, or how many of the 2,250 organizations lost data. They also do not say how the hackers got into ConnectWise in the first place.

ConnectWise said it has paused access to its cloud backup system and is investigating. As of Thursday, it said it was working on restoring the service. Customers who rely on the backup to protect their data are, for the moment, in the novel position of having neither the backup nor much certainty about who else has read it.

TechCrunch framed this as another entry in a bad week for cybersecurity. The practical lesson for companies is unglamorous: turn on multi-factor authentication, and ask your vendors what happens to the keys they hold. The lesson for the vendors is that they are now the target, and the target has been reading the same AI product announcements as everyone else.
