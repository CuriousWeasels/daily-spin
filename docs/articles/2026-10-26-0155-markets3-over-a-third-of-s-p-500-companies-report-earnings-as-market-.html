Third of S&P 500 to Report Earnings While Investors Practice Looking Calm

More than a third of the index's companies report this week, with the Fed also meeting, after tech stocks took a beating and futures nudged up.

The wire said: More than a third of S&P 500 companies are about to report earnings as market confidence cracks · MarketWatch

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 26, 2026 01:55 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

U.S. tech stocks suffered heavy losses in the previous session, and futures edged higher ahead of a week packed with corporate earnings and a Federal Reserve meeting. MarketWatch reported that confidence in the market has been cracking.

Why it matters

Earnings and Fed decisions shape the value of retirement accounts and the cost of borrowing. A week that stacks both together leaves little room for good news to arrive alone.

More than a third of the companies in the S&P 500 are scheduled to report quarterly results this week, according to MarketWatch, and they will do so in a market where confidence has started to crack. Investing.com reported that U.S. stock futures edged higher after bruising losses in technology shares, which is how markets say they are fine after a bad day while checking their pockets.

Translation: futures are contracts that trade before the regular stock market opens, so they serve as an early guess at how the day will start. A small rise after a large drop is less a recovery than a pulse check. MarketWatch quoted one strategist as saying that with cautious to negative sentiment, it could be a tricky week.

Earnings season is the stretch when companies disclose what they actually earned, and then spend the following hours explaining why it was either better than expected or not as bad as it looks. The expectations are the real test. A company can report a profit and still see its shares fall if analysts wanted a bigger one, a ritual that confuses relatives at family dinners.

The Federal Reserve is also on the calendar, according to Investing.com. The latest official data show the fed funds rate at 3.88%, while the 10-year Treasury yield sits at 4.96% and the 2-year at 4.65%. Translation: the market is charging more to lend to the government for ten years than the Fed charges banks overnight, which suggests investors do not expect borrowing costs to fall quickly.

The backdrop is not soothing. Consumer price inflation was 3.74% year over year in the September reading, unemployment was 4.2%, and Brent crude was at 115.22 as of October 20. Expensive oil feeds into everything from shipping to groceries, which makes the Fed's job harder and the earnings calls more interesting.

The market's nerves are visible but not panicked. The VIX, a gauge of expected stock swings often called the fear index, stood at 19.03 on October 22. That is a number suggesting investors are uneasy rather than hiding under desks. The Nasdaq closed at 25,955.34 on October 23 and the SPY, a fund tracking the S&P 500, at 759.97.

The week's arrangement is simple: companies will say how they did, the Fed will say what it plans to do about the cost of money, and investors will decide which of the two to be upset about. Watch the technology reports first, since that is where the bruises are.
