Treasury Yields Top 5% as Washington Plans to Fix Deficit Sometime After Everyone Retires

A $1.992 trillion deficit and a 10-year yield above 5% are squeezing mortgages, while economists expect the Fed to do nothing this week and something later.

The wire said: Analysis: Why a $2 trillion deficit and 5% Treasury yields are becoming America's biggest economic challenge · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 28, 2026 02:42 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The U.S. ran a deficit of $1.992 trillion in fiscal 2026, and the 10-year Treasury yield has climbed about 114 basis points since the Iran war began on Feb. 28. The Fed meets this week, and CNBC's survey says it will hold rates steady.

Why it matters

The 10-year yield sets the tone for mortgage rates, now around 7.5%, so government borrowing costs show up in the price of a house. The sources also suggest the political gridlock after the midterms makes a fiscal fix unlikely for years.

The U.S. government borrowed $1.992 trillion more than it collected in fiscal 2026, up 12% from last year, according to Treasury data cited by CNBC. That equals roughly 6.2% of gross domestic product. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has argued for a deficit below 4% of GDP by the end of President Trump's term, which is a target CNBC says there is almost no way of hitting.

Meanwhile, the 10-year Treasury yield sat at 5.1% on Friday by CNBC's account, and 5.0% in the latest official data. Translation: the 10-year yield is the interest rate the government pays to borrow for a decade, and the rest of the economy borrows at a markup to it. It has risen 114 basis points, or 1.14 percentage points, since the end of February.

The causes are plentiful. The Iran war pushed up oil and diesel prices, which leads bond traders to demand more yield in case central banks raise rates. Investors also want extra pay for holding long-term bonds because they expect more of them to keep arriving. Companies are issuing hundreds of billions in debt to build AI infrastructure, so the Treasury is competing for the same pool of money as every data center with a bond underwriter.

The ordinary-person version: the average 30-year mortgage is about 7.5%, and Redfin said pending home sales fell 4% in September, the biggest monthly drop in three years. Translation: the government's borrowing habit has become your down payment's problem.

The math gets worse from here. The Congressional Budget Office estimates that rates a half-point above its forecast would add $1.9 trillion to deficits over ten years, and 1.5 points above would add $6 trillion. The CBO expects the 10-year to average 4.3% over that period, versus 5.1% today.

Bessent's proposed solution is growth. "We have to grow our way out of it," he said in Dallas last month, pointing to artificial intelligence. The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget calculates that growth alone would need a decade of 7.2% annual expansion. Second-quarter GDP was recently revised up to 2.2% annualized, so the gap is a mere five percentage points a year, sustained for ten years.

The Fed offers no relief this week. In CNBC's survey of 30 fund managers, strategists and economists, 93% expect a pause, and 76% expect a hike in December. Respondents are split on whether that is wise. Richard Bernstein of Janus Henderson called the Fed's hesitancy to raise rates "unfathomable." Mark Zandi of Moody's Analytics said this isn't the time for more hikes, citing falling real wages and a shaky AI-fueled stock market.

Politics does not offer much either. Republicans are widely forecast to lose at least the House, and the survey puts Democratic control of both chambers as the most likely outcome. Rep. Hakeem Jeffries, the likely next speaker, wants to reverse $1 trillion in healthcare cuts, while Trump pledged $5,000 checks to adults if Republicans swept the election. These positions are not close, in the way Earth and Neptune are not close.

CNBC notes that expensive borrowing is not the same as a crisis, and higher yields are also drawing buyers to Treasuries. Total debt reached $40 trillion this year, and nobody knows precisely what level is unsustainable. The country is therefore running an experiment to find out, with the results due at an unspecified time.
