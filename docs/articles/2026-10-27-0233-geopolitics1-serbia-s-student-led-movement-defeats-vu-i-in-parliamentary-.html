Serbia's Students Win Election, Proving Homework Can Outlast a Decade of Incumbency

A student-led protest movement took 52.7% of the vote to Aleksandar Vučić's 39.2%, and the long-serving leader conceded within hours.

The wire said: 'The smell of freedom': Serbia's student movement celebrates election triumph · NPR World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 27, 2026 02:33 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Serbia has been convulsed by protests since a canopy collapsed at Novi Sad railway station and killed 16 people, a disaster blamed on corruption and negligence. Vučić called snap parliamentary elections after roughly two years of demonstrations led by students.

Why it matters

A government that ruled since 2012 has lost power at the ballot box, which shifts Serbia's balance between Brussels and Moscow and shows that protest movements can turn into electoral majorities. What the new government actually does with that mandate will determine whether the change is lasting.

Serbia's long-standing leader Aleksandar Vučić admitted defeat on Monday after a student-led movement won the country's snap parliamentary election. "It is clear we have lost these elections," he said, according to the BBC. It is a sentence that, after more than a decade in power, few observers had expected him to finish.

The numbers were not close. With 97% of polling stations counted, the election commission said the students had 2.25 million votes and Vučić's Serbian Progressive Party (SNS) 1.67 million. Preliminary figures put the shares at 52.7% and 39.2%. Translation: when a ruling party's own preliminary count shows it more than 13 points behind, the usual options of recounting, disputing or reinterpreting become somewhat theoretical.

The movement began less than two years ago over the collapse of a canopy at the Novi Sad station, which killed 16 people. It was blamed on corruption and negligence, charges Vučić and the SNS have repeatedly denied. The protests that followed turned into the snap election that Vučić himself called, a bit like scheduling your own exam after the class has stopped attending lectures.

Vučić could not run for a third term as president, so he was leading the SNS list in the hope of returning as prime minister, a post he left in 2017. Translation: a term limit on one office is less binding when there is another office next door. He had campaigned on higher pensions, higher public salaries and more money for state healthcare, all of which are easier to promise than to deliver while also being in the way of your own scandal.

The winning Student List has no obvious leaders and fielded candidates for the 250-seat parliament from across society, including doctors, actors, academics and sports personalities. The BBC reported that its manifesto promises to purge officials who broke rules in cracking down on protesters, stop party loyalists from running public enterprises and free the public broadcaster from political control. Nebojša Vladisavljević, a University of Belgrade political scientist, called the result "an avalanche" and said the movement had "absolute legitimacy" to govern.

Crowds celebrated in Belgrade, Novi Sad and other cities, with dancing, fireworks and honking cars. Al Jazeera reported thousands in Belgrade for a second night. "Serbia is free," one student told the BBC. Long queues had also formed at polling stations abroad, in cities including Ljubljana, Munich, Los Angeles, Chicago and a rainy Paris.

The foreign reactions were revealing. The Kremlin's Dmitry Peskov praised Vučić's contribution to relations with Russia and hoped the new force would see cooperation with Moscow as in Serbia's interest, adding that "it takes two to tango." Vučić had backed EU candidacy while refusing to impose sanctions on Russia over the invasion of Ukraine, a position that let him dance with both partners at once. European Commissioner Marta Kos said "the door to the EU is open." Hungary's Péter Magyar, whose party ended 16 years of Viktor Orbán's rule in April, welcomed the result too.

The next question is who becomes prime minister, since the movement is not known for having anyone in particular in charge. Presidential elections are due in December, and parliament speaker Ana Brnabić is currently filling the role in a caretaker capacity. The students have won the election; now they must discover that governing is the part of the course with no syllabus.
