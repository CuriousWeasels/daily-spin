Eight States Sue OpenAI, Each Filing Separately, to Ask Whether ChatGPT Is Safe for Children

Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, West Virginia, Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho and Montana allege the company misled customers about safety and put profits first; OpenAI says it has built strong protections.

By The Daily Spin Desk · Oct 15, 2026 13:06 UTC · 2 min read
Catch upOpenAI already faces lawsuits from families of teens and adults who allege ChatGPT played a role in their loved ones' deaths. Eight states have now filed their own complaints, as reported by The Verge and earlier by Reuters.
Why it mattersIf the states win, OpenAI could face civil penalties, court-ordered changes to how it describes ChatGPT's safety, and deletion of data collected from minors. Every other chatbot maker will be watching what a judge decides a safety promise has to mean.

Eight US states have sued OpenAI, accusing it of harming children and misleading customers about the safety of ChatGPT, according to The Verge. The suits were filed separately by Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, West Virginia, Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho and Montana. It is a rare moment of bipartisan coordination in which eight attorneys general independently arrived at the same defendant.

The legal theory is consumer protection. Translation: the states say it is illegal to sell a product while describing it as safer than it is. The complaints allege OpenAI presented ChatGPT as a "safe, youth-appropriate tool that puts safety first" when it was, in the states' words, "dangerous to vulnerable users." The complaints cite harms including degraded critical thinking, encouraged paranoia and delusions, dangerous medical advice and unhealthy relationships.

The states also say OpenAI collected data from minors and did too little to keep underage users away. The complaints lean on the family lawsuits already filed over deaths, and on recent rogue AI hacking incidents, which the states offer as evidence of what they call "recklessness." The Verge's report does not detail those incidents, so readers will have to wait for the filings to learn how a hacking episode fits into a children's safety case.

Arizona's complaint goes after the company's history. It says OpenAI "devolved from its original mission as a public-first nonprofit" into a cautionary tale driven by a profit-at-all-costs mindset. This is the legal system's way of pointing out that the mission statement and the business model have been drifting apart for some time.

OpenAI, in an emailed statement to The Verge from spokesperson Drew Pusateri, said it has built "industry-leading age-specific protections" into ChatGPT. It added that no system can be perfect and cited parental controls, break reminders and age-appropriate model protections. The company also said it wants to work with states on policies that apply to the whole industry, "not just one company." That is a polite way of asking why it is the one in the courtroom.

What the states want is concrete: civil penalties, an order to stop misleading the public about ChatGPT's safety, and, in some states, deletion of unlawfully collected personal data. The last item is the most interesting, since deleting data is far easier to demand than to verify.

Watch for whether more states join, and whether OpenAI settles or argues that the guardrails it already has are the answer. Either way, the phrase "safety first" has become a statement that someone may now have to prove in court.
