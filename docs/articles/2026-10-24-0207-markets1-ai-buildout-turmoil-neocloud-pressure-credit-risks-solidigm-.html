AI Buildout Has Rocky Week as Neoclouds Slide, Default Insurance Soars, and an IPO Is Quietly Withdrawn

CoreWeave's default-protection costs passed 11%, Meta's free cash flow turned negative, and Solidigm shelved its IPO, while Nvidia's CEO says demand is off the charts.

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 24, 2026 02:07 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Tech giants and a new class of 'neocloud' rental firms have spent historic sums on AI data centers, betting that demand will repay the cost. This week, investors started asking more loudly when that repayment arrives.

Why it matters

The AI buildout now props up a large share of the stock market and is increasingly funded by debt, so stress in its weakest links can spread to index funds and retirement accounts. The credit market is where doubts show up first, before the earnings reports do.

The Nasdaq fell again on Friday and ended the week down 0.7%, according to CNBC. The index has not had an across-the-board sell-off. It has instead had a series of unpleasant discoveries, each occurring in a different part of the AI supply chain.

Start with the neoclouds, companies that rent out AI computing power to customers who would rather not build their own data centers. Translation: they are landlords for chips, and they borrowed heavily to buy the buildings. CoreWeave, Nebius and IREN each fell sharply this week, leaving each down well over 30% for October, CNBC reported.

CoreWeave's credit default swaps, which are insurance contracts against a company failing to repay its debt, widened past 11%. Translation: the price of insuring its bonds has reached what markets generally treat as a distress level. Yields on its 2031 and 2032 unsecured notes topped 15%, which is what lenders charge when they would like to be paid for worrying.

The unease spread abroad. South Korea's Elice Group dropped 30% on its market debut, and Australia's NextDC fell 39.5% after cutting its annual earnings forecast. Gartner analysts, quoted by CNBC, predict that 80% of neocloud companies will either cease operations or be absorbed by 2030. That is a forecast for an industry still being described as the future.

Then there is Solidigm. SK Hynix said Thursday it was discontinuing its review of an IPO for the storage unit, which it bought from Intel in 2021, citing market conditions and share price trends. An IPO is a company's way of asking the public to share the bill for its ambitions. The public, it turns out, checked the weather first.

Even the giants flinched. Meta fell 7% Thursday after reporting negative free cash flow of more than $2 billion for the third quarter, while lifting the floor of its full-year capital spending to $135 billion from $130 billion. Free cash flow is the money left after the bills, and negative means the bills won. Meta also disclosed about $825 billion in leases and contracts over coming years, over $450 billion of them non-cancellable take-or-pay deals. Translation: you pay whether or not you use it.

Meta's five-year CDS hit a record above 124 basis points, with Nvidia at 126 and Oracle at 343, CNBC said. A basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point, so these are small numbers that mean more than they sound like. All the mega-caps keep investment-grade credit ratings. Oracle's stock is down more than a third this year and it faces downgrade risk.

MarketWatch reported that investors poured a record sum into a leveraged semiconductor ETF last week and then got walloped. Leveraged funds multiply daily moves, which works wonderfully in one direction. Meanwhile, Investing.com reported that Amazon's CEO says cloud capacity is sold out through 2027, which is the bullish case in a single sentence.

Nvidia's Jensen Huang, the industry's best-known evangelist, told CNBC that demand for neoclouds is 'off the charts' and that demand for capital exceeds its supply, a problem he expects to be solved. Roundhill's Dave Mazza argued investors are throwing out the baby with the bathwater. Both can be right: demand can be huge and some borrowers can still fail to survive long enough to serve it.

Alphabet, which fell 6% this week, reports earnings next week alongside Microsoft and Amazon. Those reports will say how much more they plan to spend. The market's question is whether anyone will say how much they expect to earn.
