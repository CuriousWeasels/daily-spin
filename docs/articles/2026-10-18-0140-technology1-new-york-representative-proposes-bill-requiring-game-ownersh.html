Congressman Proposes That Buying a Video Game Should Involve Owning It

Rep. Ritchie Torres's Power to the Players Act would direct the FTC to write rules ensuring game buyers own what they pay for, citing NBA 2K25, which goes dark on December 31.

The wire said: US Rep proposes bill to give video game buyers "ownership," not "a license" · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 18, 2026 01:40 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Most digital games are sold as revocable licenses, so publishers can shut down servers and leave paying customers with an icon that does nothing. The Crew's 2024 shutdown sparked the Stop Killing Games movement, and Sony has drawn protests over plans to wind down physical releases.

Why it matters

If the bill passes and the FTC writes strong rules, a game purchase could come with a guarantee that it keeps working after the publisher loses interest. If not, buyers keep paying purchase prices for what courts and lawyers describe as a rental.

Rep. Ritchie Torres (D-NY) has introduced the Power to the Players Act, which would direct the Federal Trade Commission to write rules so that buying a video game gives the consumer ownership rather than a license to play it, according to Ars Technica. Torres told Kotaku his position is "if you pay for it, you own it." Translation: he is proposing that the word "buy" mean what it meant at the register in 1996.

The bill's example is NBA 2K25, which launched on September 6, 2025, and is scheduled to become unplayable on December 31 because of server shutdowns. That is a shelf life of under four months, which compares unfavorably with the yogurt in most refrigerators. Tom's Hardware adds that the game is being pulled from digital storefronts, so new players cannot buy it either, a small mercy that spares them the experience.

Torres's statement calls such a product "a rental disguised as a sale." The distinction matters because of how the industry sells games. Under a license, the publisher keeps ownership and grants you permission to play, which it can revoke. Tom's Hardware reports that Sony's lawyers recently argued in a class action that clicking "purchase" would not lead a consumer to think they were acquiring a durable right to use a game. Translation: the button says one thing, the contract says another, and the contract has better lawyers.

According to the bill's release, quoted by Tom's Hardware, games with online functionality would also have to provide an offline mode before their servers are shut down. The same report says concrete definitions have not been released. It is not yet clear whether the right to transfer a game, which Torres mentions, would apply to digital purchases or mainly to physical copies. The details of what "ownership" means will decide whether this is a consumer protection law or a very optimistic press release.

The bill follows a Pennsylvania state proposal. State senator John Kane introduced the Protect Our Games Act, which would require publishers to offer an offline mode, community servers, or a 25 percent minimum refund, according to Tom's Hardware. The pattern is familiar: when a company will not preserve a product voluntarily, legislators propose a menu of ways to make it.

The backdrop is a growing fight over game preservation. Ubisoft's 2024 shutdown of The Crew, an online-only racing game whose single-player mode also needed Ubisoft's servers, made the problem concrete and fed the Stop Killing Games movement. Ars Technica notes that Sony says digital downloads account for 82 percent of full-game unit sales on PlayStation 4 and 5. Physical discs are a minority format, but preservation advocates argue they are the one that still works when the servers do not.

Readers of the Tom's Hardware piece were split. Some argued that games once shipped with tools for private servers, so the requirement is not unreasonable. Others warned that publishers would respond with thinner single-player games, more free-to-play titles and more microtransactions. Both outcomes are plausible, and the second has the advantage of being something publishers can do without Congress's help.

The bill must still pass Congress and then survive the FTC rulemaking process, neither of which is known for speed. NBA 2K25, meanwhile, has a deadline already on the calendar.
