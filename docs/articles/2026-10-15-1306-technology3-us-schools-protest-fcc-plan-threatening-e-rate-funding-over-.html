Schools and Libraries Ask FCC Not to Unplug the Internet They Use

Comments pour in against a proposal to limit or sunset E-Rate, the roughly $2 billion-a-year program that helps pay for connectivity at schools and libraries.

The wire said: Schools across US protest FCC plan that could disconnect kids from Internet (Ars Technica)

By The Daily Spin Desk · Technology Oct 15, 2026 13:06 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

E-Rate, created under the 1996 telecommunications law, discounts internet service and equipment for schools and libraries. In June, FCC Chairman Brendan Carr led a 2-1 vote to seek comment on whether it should be limited or sunset.

Why it matters

E-Rate discounts help pay for the connections that schools and libraries use daily, and some report they would face higher local costs or cut services without them. The fight also tests whether the FCC can end a program Congress mandated.

Schools, libraries and the industry that sells them broadband told the Federal Communications Commission this week that they would prefer to keep E-Rate, according to Ars Technica. The program typically distributes over $2 billion a year to help connect students and library visitors. The agency's own proposal asks whether it should be "limited or sunset to reflect today's extensive connectivity rates." The commenters, it turns out, have thoughts.

Carr has criticized school districts for having "experimented with a massive increase in screen time for students" and argued E-Rate has grown beyond its original purpose. Translation: a program built to get schools online is being questioned because schools are now online, which is the sort of reasoning that could also retire the fire department after a quiet year.

The proposal could cut the discounts schools and libraries receive, add restrictions on how the money is used, or, because sunsetting is on the table, end the program entirely. The Carr FCC already trimmed E-Rate last year by ending funding for lending Wi-Fi hotspots and for Wi-Fi on school buses. The National Association of Elementary School Principals urged the commission to reject proposals that would "eliminate, substantially restrict, or impose new conditions" on the program.

There is also a legal wrinkle. The American Library Association told the FCC it lacks authority to terminate E-Rate, since Congress mandated it and the agency merely administers it. Ars notes Carr could argue that the program's original mission is complete, which would supply the legal basis. His proposal points to the "substantial expansion of broadband access" over three decades and asks whether continued funding fits Congress's objective. Translation: if the program worked, it can be declared finished, and the better it worked, the stronger the argument.

The library association also objected to the screen-time angle, saying the FCC lacks authority and expertise to regulate screen time or determine educational outcomes. It cited US law that leaves decisions about inappropriate content to local school boards and libraries. Democratic Commissioner Anna Gomez put it more briefly: "The FCC is not the nation's parent."

The examples were concrete. Paul Lapka, principal of Rock Valley Christian School in Iowa, said annual internet costs are about $3,120 and E-Rate support about $1,560, so losing it would mean higher local costs and delayed upgrades. The Texas Library Association said only 35 percent of Texas public libraries receive E-Rate discounts, versus 65 percent nationally, and only 55 percent meet the commission's own speed benchmarks. Its conclusion was that this signals unmet need, not a need that has passed.

Opposition also came from an unexpected corner. America's Communications Association, a cable industry lobby, argued that schools and libraries must keep paying for service, equipment and upgrades. Ars noted that ending E-Rate would stop new purchases of equipment and broadband, which would upset providers. It is rare to see schoolchildren and cable companies on the same side of a billing dispute.

E-Rate and other Universal Service Fund programs are paid for by fees on phone companies, which usually pass the cost to consumers on monthly bills. The program has a $5.2 billion cap, though payouts depend on demand and application approvals. The first comment deadline has passed, and reply comments come next. After that, the commission will decide whether the public was merely consulted or actually heard.
