Anthropic Tells Wall Street It Is Profitable, Then Lists $850 Billion in Bills

The AI lab's IPO filing shows revenue up fourteenfold to $16.3 billion in six months, and commitments that assume the growth never stops.

The wire said: Anthropic is asking Wall Street to believe in AGI · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 30, 2026 17:17 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Anthropic has filed an S-1, the document a company must publish before listing on a stock exchange, to go public on the Nasdaq. The Verge reports it could start trading as soon as next month at a valuation of $1.5 trillion to $2 trillion.

Why it matters

If you own tech stocks, index funds, or a pension with either, the filing is a bet that AI revenue will outrun contracts that last up to two decades. If it doesn't, someone else gets the invoice.

Anthropic has filed to go public, and The Verge says the filing offers the first detailed look at the finances of a leading frontier AI lab. The headline numbers are strong: revenue of $16.3 billion in the first half of 2026, up fourteen times from the same period a year earlier. The company also reported about $1.62 billion in adjusted income, after stripping out an $80 billion loss that The Verge describes as largely an accounting matter. Translation: the company lost a great deal of money on paper, and it would like you to focus on the part where it made money in reality.

Joey Brookhart, an analyst at SemiAnalysis, told The Verge's Decoder podcast that the filing showed a company that had scaled efficiently, with more than $70 billion in annualized recurring revenue. Translation: that is the current monthly pace multiplied by twelve, a figure that makes any month look like a full year. He said he found little to nitpick, and noted that semiconductor and compute stocks rallied after it appeared.

The Verge's separate analysis is less cheerful, and the cause is the fine print. Anthropic lists $265.6 billion in non-cancelable purchase commitments, $321.4 billion in compute leases that have not yet started, and roughly $245 billion in newer agreements. Another $125 billion to $150 billion in deals are still unfinalized. The Verge's tally is at least $850 billion over 20 years, nearing a trillion once pending deals are counted, though the author says she may have missed something. Two spots in the filing, on pages 64 and 137, leave the relevant amounts blank, which is a bold way to handle a number that large.

Translation of the business model: Anthropic has signed long leases on computing capacity it must pay for whether or not customers show up. PitchBook analyst Harrison Rolfes estimates payments at about $90 billion a year from 2027 to 2030. He writes that growth is not optional and that the contracts assume it. A fixed bill that large is a mortgage on the future, and the future has not yet agreed to the terms.

There is a case for optimism. The Verge notes Anthropic has stayed focused on enterprise customers and coding rather than image generators or consumer novelties, and about three-quarters of its customers spend at least $100,000 a year. Net revenue retention, a measure of how much existing customers spend over time, is 900 percent. Its newest model reportedly cut customer costs by 40 percent, which Anthropic says encourages more usage and protects margins.

The risks are just as concrete. Brookhart says the industry has become much more competitive: OpenAI was probably the majority of net new revenue in the third quarter, after Anthropic took 90 percent in the first half. Open-source inference spending reportedly grew from about $1 billion to more than $10 billion. Futurum Group's Brendan Burke warns the filing freezes in time a period when demand outran compute and the company could charge more for what it had. Brookhart adds that coding alone will not triple from here, so the growth must come from broader white-collar work, including replacing labor, not just assisting it.

The Verge's reading is that this pressure explains why Anthropic must keep spending on compute and model development, which already cost $9.7 billion in the first half against $2.3 billion for serving customers. Investors are being asked to believe the models will keep improving fast enough to cover the bills. Prospective shareholders can read the full document and decide whether they are buying a software company or a very large leveraged wager that the machines keep getting better.
