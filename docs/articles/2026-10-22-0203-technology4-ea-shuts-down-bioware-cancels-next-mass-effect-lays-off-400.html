EA Closes BioWare, Citing Desire to Align Talent With Opportunity

The studio behind Mass Effect and Dragon Age shuts after three decades, minutes after a Bloomberg report on 400 job cuts across EA.

The wire said: RIP BioWare: EA shuts down storied RPG studio after over three decades · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 22, 2026 02:03 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

EA bought BioWare in 2007 and went private last year in a $55 billion deal led by Silver Lake, Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund and Jared Kushner's Affinity Partners. On Wednesday, BioWare announced it was closing, and early work on the next Mass Effect was ending.

Why it matters

The closure shows what can happen to creative studios when a heavily indebted owner goes looking for predictable profit. Players of story-driven single-player games should note which kind of game the money is chasing.

Electronic Arts is closing BioWare, the studio behind Mass Effect, Dragon Age, Neverwinter Nights and Star Wars: Knights of the Old Republic, according to Ars Technica. BioWare announced the news on social media Wednesday afternoon, saying that after more than three decades "BioWare is closing" and early development on the next Mass Effect project is ending. The studio was founded in Edmonton, Alberta, in 1995, which means it lasted roughly as long as a mortgage, and several of its games.

The timing had a certain efficiency. The announcement went up just minutes after Bloomberg reported that EA was cutting 400 jobs company-wide, including 50 at BioWare, citing a person familiar with the company's plans. Translation: the leak arrived, and the press release followed it, like a ship's captain confirming the iceberg after the passengers have already seen it.

EA executives, in a message to staff reported by Bloomberg, said: "Across our portfolio, we're making deliberate choices to align talent and investments where we see the greatest opportunities." Translation: talent is a thing that can be aligned, and the alignment in this case pointed away from the building. The memo also invoked "long-term growth," a phrase that here covers a closed studio.

History offers some contrast. When EA bought BioWare in 2007, then-CEO John Riccitiello called it one of "the most respected studios in the industry" and said it would contribute to the company's strategic growth initiatives. He also said the deal would drive long-term value for shareholders. Both sides of the marriage have, in a sense, kept their vows: the shareholders no longer exist, and neither does the studio.

That first point matters. EA is no longer publicly traded after the $55 billion take-private deal last year, led by Silver Lake, Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund and Kushner's Affinity Partners. Ars Technica noted that analysts at the time expected "layoffs, studio closures, and [selling] of IP" as the heavily indebted company leaned toward live services, annual sports franchises and microtransactions. Translation: when a company borrows heavily to be bought, the debt has to be paid from somewhere, and a role-playing game that takes years to make is a slow place to find the money.

The sources do not say what happens to the Mass Effect franchise itself, only that early development on the next project is ending. Nor do they say what becomes of the remaining BioWare staff beyond the 50 cuts Bloomberg reported. Fans hoping for clarity may have to wait for another message about deliberate choices.

Watch for whether EA says anything about who now holds the rights to Mass Effect and Dragon Age. In an industry where annual sports titles are the safe bet, those properties are now either valuable assets or very expensive memories.
