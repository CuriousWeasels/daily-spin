China Drafts Export Rules for AI Models, With a Fast Lane for Giving Them Away

A draft list covers 23 AI technologies, restricts models above 10^26 FLOPs, and approves open-source releases the moment the paperwork is filed.

The wire said: China plans to tighten export controls on open-weight models and 23 key AI technologies · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 18, 2026 01:40 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

China's commerce and science ministries have published a draft update to the country's catalog of banned and restricted export technologies, adding 23 AI areas including large models, agents, compute clusters and data processing. Until final rules take effect, companies must register affected exports through the commerce ministry's portal.

Why it matters

Companies in the US and Europe that build on Chinese open-weight models now face the possibility that future releases could be restricted. Firms and governments hoping to stay independent by hosting open models locally may find the supply is controlled by two capitals.

China's commerce and science ministries have released a draft rule tightening export controls on artificial intelligence, according to The Decoder. It covers 23 key technologies across large models, agents, compute clusters and data processing. The most severe category is AI tools that can write or alter malware on their own to attack critical infrastructure, the military or government agencies, which face a total export ban. Translation: some things are not for sale, and the list of things is now written down.

Large models are caught in two ways. The first is data: a model trained on China's "important data," as defined by the Data Security Law and related rules, that offers services based on it. Lawfully public data is exempt. Up to 0.1 percent is also allowed if it was cleaned beforehand, never shows up in outputs, and does not substantially boost the model's R&D capacity for core technology in that field. In other words, a small amount is tolerated, provided it is invisible and unhelpful.

The second trigger is size and fame. Controls apply to the core technologies behind a model, meaning weights, checkpoints, source code, architecture and training or inference methods, once it reaches 10^26 FLOPs of training compute, at least 2 trillion parameters, or a spot in the global top 10. Translation: FLOPs are the arithmetic operations used in training, so this is a rule that measures how much homework the model did. The top-10 test is stranger. Export officials would judge it from public rankings by major domestic and foreign evaluators or industry platforms, so a leaderboard could become a customs document.

Beijing is not trying to throttle its own open-source boom, so it adds a fast lane. Publishing restricted AI technology and weights as open source qualifies for automatic licensing, as do Chinese firms running their own data centers abroad, taping out chips overseas, researching with foreign partners or helping set international standards. Eligible exports are cleared the moment the paperwork is filed. Technology that is already public through open-source releases or research papers is exempt entirely. The regime, then, restricts exports mainly in the sense that it requires you to tell the ministry about them.

Access methods matter too. Using restricted models through API calls, apps, websites, SaaS tools, or lightweight versions on local devices stays outside the controls. Installing them on local servers abroad requires a standard export license, though companies can use the streamlined process if the software runs on their own overseas cloud. The Decoder also reports that Beijing pledges to ease exports for capacity building and joint rulemaking in the World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organization, a bloc China launched to counter Western AI bodies.

Dean W. Ball, head of strategic futures at OpenAI, wrote that the move does not mean China is shutting down open-weight models, but gives the government "optionality" to shut down future ones at will. He called the framework a "mirror image" of US model-weight export controls. The Decoder expects US companies relying on open-weight AI to lean more on domestic models. It sees Europe in a tougher spot, since its sovereignty strategy of running open models in its own clouds assumes the best ones stay available, and they come from the US or China.

The rule is still a draft, so the open question is how the thresholds and the top-10 test are applied once it is final. Until then, the practical advice for everyone is to keep the registration portal bookmarked.
