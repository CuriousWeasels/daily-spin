Apple, After 12 Years of Telling Others to Build Smart Homes, Builds Its Own

The Apple Home Collection arrives with a smart display, a $200-to-$400 price range, and a quiet threat to the people who write recipes.

The wire said: Why Apple is finally making its own smart home devices · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 17, 2026 14:30 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Apple launched the Home Collection, a lineup of cameras, thermostats, sensors, a lock and a doorbell codeveloped with LG Electronics and Schneider Electric, plus HomeView, its first smart display. The Verge examined both the hardware strategy and what HomeView's recipe feature means for food writers.

Why it matters

Buyers get a pre-approved smart home starter kit with simpler setup, but at a price and with camera lock-in. Meanwhile, the people who write recipes face another tool that delivers their work while skipping most of the ad revenue.

Apple has spent 12 years, since HomeKit launched in 2014, telling other companies to build smart home devices for its platform. This week it built some itself. The Verge reported that the Apple Home Collection includes cameras, thermostats, sensors, a smart lock and a video doorbell, made with LG Electronics and Schneider Electric, alongside HomeView, a smart display. Apple has therefore concluded that the best way to support an open ecosystem is to also sell the nicest part of it.

The reasoning, according to Kaiann Drance, Apple's vice president of product marketing for home, is timing. Matter, the interoperable smart home standard Apple helped found, supplied the ecosystem. AI supplied a more conversational Siri. Translation: Apple waited until the pieces existed, then announced that it had been waiting. The Verge notes that Amazon and Google already had in-house brands, Ring and Nest, while Apple had nothing of its own to point customers toward.

The pitch is that Apple Home has been a buffet and the new collection is the chef's choice. Setup is a tap of your iPhone, with no separate account or app. Every device supports Thread, a low-power mesh network that keeps gadgets connected if Wi-Fi drops, and Apple says everything is end-to-end encrypted. The Verge says the lock, thermostats and sensors are Matter-compatible, but the cameras use Apple's HomeKit Secure Video instead, which it called the biggest lock-in.

There is no feature, in theory, that only the Home Collection can do. The Verge compares it to a Google Pixel phone showing developers what Android can do: Apple's products simply come with everything switched on. Drance said the broader Matter ecosystem is staying. The products cost $200 to $400, and camera history requires an iCloud Plus plan, so the buffet remains open, but the chef's choice comes with a cover charge.

Parks Associates research cited by The Verge says the biggest barrier to smart device sales is that people see no value in them, followed by privacy, complexity and cost. Apple's answer addresses the middle two and politely declines the last. The target customer is the existing Apple owner, whom Parks Associates' Elizabeth Parks described as very loyal. If you already own a Ring doorbell or Google Nest Hub, The Verge says this probably isn't for you.

HomeView also pulls up recipes with step-by-step, hands-free instructions, which a second Verge piece says strips out the ads and affiliate links many recipe writers depend on. Eater's Bettina Makalintal said such tools break the unspoken social contract with recipe developers and audiences. The long personal introductions people complain about exist partly because, as Makalintal explained, writers padded text to rank in Google searches. Translation: the industry wrote the essays because the search engine demanded them, and is now being faulted for them by the same search engine.

Apple's approach does guarantee one visit to the original page, since the recipe must first be opened on an iPhone or through Safari on the HomeView. That is worth less than repeated ad views from someone scrolling while they cook. Makalintal ranks the threat below Google's AI summaries, whose opaque sourcing she says is erasing labor. Big apps like Allrecipes or NYT Cooking can present content on their own terms through casting, while independent writers mostly cannot, and the Verge author says Apple hadn't answered how paywalled recipes will be handled.

The remaining options for writers are ever-shrinking Google traffic, sponsored content or paywalled newsletters, which Makalintal says are causing subscription fatigue. Jaya Saxena, a founder of the food site Ravenous, argues that a tested recipe takes time and effort. The likely conclusion is that everyone, including Apple, will have to get used to paying for recipes again. In the meantime, the French toast will be very easy to follow.
