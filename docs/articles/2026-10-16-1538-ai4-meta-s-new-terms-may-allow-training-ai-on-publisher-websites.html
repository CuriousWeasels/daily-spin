Meta Updates Terms So Its AI Can Read Your Website, Which You Are Welcome to Pay to Link To

New commercial terms effective October 30th let Meta crawl sites tied to business accounts and train AI on them, while Meta says nothing has changed.

The wire said: Meta’s new terms may allow it to train AI on news sites for free · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 16, 2026 15:38 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Meta is revising the commercial terms governing businesses that advertise or promote content on Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp and Messenger. Nieman Lab reported that the language appears to authorize broad scraping of linked websites, including those of news publishers.

Why it matters

If the terms work as some legal experts read them, publishers could lose leverage in AI licensing talks, since a company that can crawl for free has little reason to pay. Any business that relies on Meta for customers faces a choice between accepting the terms and losing access.

Meta will, starting October 30th, require business users to authorize it to crawl the websites associated with their products or services, according to a report from Nieman Lab as relayed by The Verge. The terms say Meta may use what it finds to "develop, improve, research," and "train" its products. Translation: if you advertise with Meta, your website may become a reading list for its AI.

The language is broad. It covers automated software such as scrapers, bots, spiders and crawlers, and it lets Meta create test credentials and periodically pull updates. It also reaches pages made available through users' use of Meta's products, which is a category with few obvious edges.

Meta disagrees with the alarming reading. Spokesperson Daniel Roberts said the company is merely "consolidating" its commercial and self-serve ad terms, and that "there is no change to how Meta uses web crawlers." He added that content owners can still use robots.txt flags to state their preferences. Translation: robots.txt is the small text file at a website's door that tells crawlers whether they are welcome. It works on the honor system, a system that has had a busy few years.

Legal experts told Nieman Lab the concern is precedence. Meta could, they said, argue its commercial terms supersede other blocking instructions, including robots.txt, and might even bypass a licensing agreement. Cornell law professor James Grimmelmann said the terms clearly represent permission to train AI on any content from publishers' sites and possibly to deploy it in competition with them.

Andy Stone, Meta's vice president of communications, wrote on X that the update is meant to pull accurate product information from third-party sites for ad and content recommendations, not to scrape news sites. That is a plausible purpose. It is also a purpose the contract, as quoted, does not say it is limited to.

The context makes publishers jumpy. Meta has followed OpenAI in signing AI licensing deals with CNN, Fox News, USA Today and People Inc. Licensing deals only make sense if the alternative is not free. Meanwhile, Meta launched a Meta One subscription last month that requires businesses and creators to pay to include links in posts and reels. In Nieman Lab's words, Meta has begun charging users to post links to the same content it will be permitted to crawl for free.

Businesses that decline the terms, the document says, may lose access to Meta's products for commercial purposes. For a company that gets customers through Instagram, that is less a negotiation than a form with a single button.

Watch for whether publishers test the terms by blocking Meta's crawlers, and whether Meta's developer documentation ends up carrying more legal weight than its contract. One of them will have to win.
