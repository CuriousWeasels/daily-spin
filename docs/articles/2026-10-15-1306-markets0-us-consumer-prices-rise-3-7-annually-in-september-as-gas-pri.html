Inflation Hits 3.7% as Gasoline Does Half the Work and Everyone Else Helps

September consumer prices rose 0.6% on the month as a war-driven energy surge pushed the Fed toward another rate hike and the midterms into view.

The wire said: Consumer prices rose 3.7% annually in September as gas prices skyrocket · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 15, 2026 13:06 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Inflation has run above the Federal Reserve's 2% goal since March 2021, and it spiked again this year after the U.S. and Israel struck Iran and a war followed. The Fed raised rates in September for the first time in more than three years.

Why it matters

Higher gas prices hit budgets immediately, while another Fed hike would raise borrowing costs for loans and credit cards. Prices are unlikely to fall back, so the realistic hope is that wages eventually outpace them.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Wednesday that the consumer price index rose a seasonally adjusted 0.6% in September, putting annual inflation at 3.7%, up from 3.4% in August. Economists surveyed by Dow Jones had expected 0.6% and 3.6%, so the number was mostly as forecast, which is a fairly efficient way to be unwelcome.

Translation: the consumer price index tracks what a broad basket of goods and services costs, and the annual rate tells you how much more that basket costs than it did a year ago. The Fed wants that figure near 2%. It has not been there for roughly 65 consecutive months, according to a CNBC analysis, which is the longest such streak of this century.

Gasoline did the heavy lifting. Prices rose 9.1% in a single month and accounted for about half of the headline gain, the BLS said. Over the past year gas is up 34.2% and fuel oil is up 74.2%. Brent crude, the global oil benchmark, traded around $105 a barrel on Tuesday after rising about 42% in the third quarter, according to CNBC.

Core CPI, which strips out food and energy because they bounce around, rose 0.3% on the month and 2.6% on the year, a bit above the 0.2% and 2.5% forecast. Translation: even after ignoring the part of the economy that is currently on fire, the rest is warming up. Shelter rose 0.4%, used vehicles 0.7%, and tariff-sensitive apparel 0.3%. Food was flat. Carson Group's Sonu Varghese said the only surprise is that investors keep being surprised, and that the full impact of higher diesel prices and the AI boom has yet to show up.

The Fed's problem is one of interpretation. Energy shocks are normally treated as temporary, and central bankers often look through them. But officials worry that the longer the war lasts, the more likely higher prices seep into everything else and into what people expect. Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack wrote last week that consumers are starting to accept high inflation as a fact of life, and she called that concerning.

The expectations point matters because it can become self-fulfilling. Businesses that expect rising costs raise prices early, and workers who expect them ask for bigger raises, which raises costs further. The New York Fed's survey showed the one-year inflation outlook rising to 3.9%, the highest in more than three years.

Markets responded as markets do. Stock futures fell and bond yields rose sharply. Futures traders put the odds of an October rate hike at about 54%, according to CME's FedWatch tool, though many Fed officials have said there is no need to rush. Traders see better than 96% odds of a hike by year-end. The Fed's rate is currently 3.88%, per official data.

The politics are unavoidable. This was the last CPI report before the November midterms, and University of Michigan data show confidence has fallen this year, more sharply among Republicans and independents than Democrats. Economists say shoppers should not expect prices to fall, since deflation would bring its own trouble. The best on offer is inflation that cools below wage growth. Producer prices, a wholesale gauge, arrive Thursday, so there is still time for more news.
