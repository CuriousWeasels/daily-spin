Fed Expected to Hold Rates Days Before Election, Citing Purely Coincidental Calendar

After a September hike, the central bank is expected to pause in October as inflation hits 3.7% and oil climbs, leaving borrowers to carry the bill.

The wire said: Federal Reserve is likely to hold interest rates steady in October. Here's what that means for consumers · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 26, 2026 17:43 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate by 25 basis points to a range of 3.75% to 4% last month, its first hike since 2023, as inflation picked back up. Oil prices have surged because of the U.S. war with Iran, adding to price pressures.

Why it matters

A hold means credit card, mortgage and auto loan rates stay high rather than rising further, while savers keep decent yields. Anyone hoping for relief should note that no one in the sources is forecasting any.

The Federal Reserve is expected to leave interest rates unchanged at its meeting this week, according to CNBC, even though inflation is moving the wrong way. The consumer price index rose again in September, lifting the annual rate to 3.7%, and the Fed's target is 2%. Translation: prices are rising roughly twice as fast as the central bank says it wants, and it is preparing to do nothing about it, for now.

Markets agree on the pause. Fed funds futures, which are bets on where rates are headed, priced in less than a 10% chance of another quarter-point hike this week, per the CME Group's FedWatch tool. Nearly 75% of traders expect at least one hike by year-end. That is a cheerfully specific forecast: no hike now, probably a hike soon.

The timing is awkward. The meeting lands just days before the midterm elections, and Heather Long, chief economist at Navy Federal Credit Union, told CNBC that "given that it's right before an election," the Fed won't hike in October. The Fed insists it is independent of politics, which is a claim that works best when its decisions and the election calendar do not line up so neatly. CME's Mark Shore noted that the four times the Fed raised rates near a midterm, in 1978, 1982, 2018 and 2022, were all tied to rising inflation. So hiking before an election is possible, but only when things are bad enough.

Things are, arguably, getting there. Oil is the main culprit: CNBC reported that Brent crude hit a five-month high on Friday before slipping, and the latest figure we have puts it at 115.22. Higher energy costs flow into the producer price index, which measures what businesses pay, and eventually into what everyone else pays. Bank of America economist Aditya Bhave warned that if the Fed holds, markets could question its inflation-fighting credibility. Translation: if you keep promising to be tough and then wait for a better moment, traders start to notice.

For households, the mechanics matter more than the drama. The Fed's benchmark rate is what banks charge one another for overnight loans, and it feeds into credit cards, which mostly carry variable rates. A hold means card APRs stay put, at an average of 23.96% on new offers, according to LendingTree. Staying put at nearly 24% is the financial equivalent of being told the storm has stopped getting worse.

Mortgages follow a different script. They track Treasury yields, and the 10-year yield has been near 5%, which keeps the average 30-year fixed rate close to 7.4%, per Mortgage News Daily. Moody's Mark Zandi said longer-term rates have risen more than short-term ones and are weighing heavily on refinancing, homebuying and vehicle sales. In other words, the Fed can decline to hike, but the bond market will happily do some of the work for it.

Car buyers are coping by borrowing more and stretching repayment terms, according to Edmunds, a strategy that solves a monthly payment problem by making it last longer. Savers are the rare winners, since deposit yields track the Fed's rate and remain relatively attractive. Long said the outlook means "really high borrowing costs for the foreseeable future."

Watch what the Fed says about December. A pause before an election is easy to explain. A pause after it, with inflation at 3.7% and oil this high, will be harder.
