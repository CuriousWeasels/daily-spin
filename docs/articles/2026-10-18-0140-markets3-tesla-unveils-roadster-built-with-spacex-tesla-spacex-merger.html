Tesla Shows Hovering Roadster, Traders Bet Elon Will Merge His Companies

A car nine years late and a rocket company worth $2 trillion have pushed prediction-market odds of a Tesla-SpaceX merger above 50% for early 2027.

The wire said: Tesla unveils long-delayed Roadster developed with SpaceX, fueling merger speculation · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 18, 2026 01:40 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Tesla unveiled its long-delayed next-generation Roadster on Thursday at a SpaceX test site in Texas, almost a decade after it began taking deposits. Both companies are controlled by Elon Musk, and merger speculation has swirled since SpaceX went public in June.

Why it matters

A Tesla-SpaceX combination would merge two of the 12 U.S. companies worth at least $1 trillion, affecting index funds and shareholders who own either one. The car itself is unlikely to matter financially, but the corporate plumbing behind it could.

Tesla showed off its next-generation Roadster on Thursday, a little under a decade after it started collecting $50,000 deposits for the car. According to CNBC, the white prototype raced up a curved ramp, clung to it upside down, vaulted off, flipped in mid-air and hovered before landing. Tesla's own timer put zero to 60 mph at under a second. Production, Musk said, begins in 2028, which in Roadster time is practically tomorrow.

The hovering relies on SpaceX technology, including thrusters, pressure vessels and powerful fans. Translation: the car is partly a rocket, which raises the question of what exactly the customers' deposits bought. Musk conceded that "This is not necessarily a safety-optimized car," and CNBC reported it was unclear whether the vehicle would be street-legal. No price was announced.

The delivery record is its own genre. Tesla has missed every targeted date since 2017, including a demo Musk hoped to hold before the end of 2025. Sam Altman, who got his refund, wrote last year that "7.5 years has felt like a long time to wait." Others stayed in line. One reservation holder told CNBC that his $250,000 Founder's Edition deposit amounted to an "interest-free loan" to Tesla, and after the show called what he saw "off the charts." Translation: the loan is still outstanding, but the demo was good.

The Roadster is not the main event. CNBC noted it is unlikely to have a material financial impact, as a high-end, low-volume car. The real audience was the market, which has been guessing whether Tesla and SpaceX will merge. Tesla is valued at about $1.4 trillion and SpaceX at about $2 trillion. The companies already pay each other, share some personnel, and answer to the same man, so a merger would mostly be a formality in the sense that a wedding is a formality for a couple who already share a mortgage.

On Wednesday, Barclays analyst Dan Levy wrote that a deal could come before the end of 2026, and before Tesla's annual meeting, due by Dec. 6. He suggested it might be pitched as AI vertical integration, tying Tesla's Robotaxi and Optimus to SpaceX's Grok and Macrohard, with the Terafab chip project as a possible driver. Translation: vertical integration means owning every step of the process, which is easier when you already own most of the steps. Levy said the true value would be there rather than in financial synergies. SpaceX COO Gwynne Shotwell told CNBC before the IPO that a merger "might make Elon's life a little easier."

Traders took the hint. On a Kalshi contract asking when the two will merge, odds of a deal before March jumped to 56% by Friday morning from 29%, according to CNBC. CNBC disclosed that it has a commercial relationship with Kalshi, including a minority investment. Also Friday, Evercore ISI recommended buying SpaceX ahead of its early-November earnings and raised its price target by 20% to $275.

Next up is Tesla's third-quarter report, due after the bell on Wednesday. Investors may learn more about the overlap Musk has described on earnings calls. They may also learn whether the Roadster date, like its predecessors, has room to move.
