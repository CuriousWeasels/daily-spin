Man Says Namecheap Disabled All His Domains; Internet Rediscovers Landlord Problem

A Reddit sysadmin's post about a registrar suspending every domain he owns drew 438 points and 204 comments on Hacker News, though the reason is not in the post's headline.

Catch up

A user on Reddit's r/sysadmin community posted that Namecheap, a major domain registrar, had seized and disabled all of his domains. The post was shared on Hacker News, where it gathered 438 points and 204 comments.

Why it matters

Anyone whose email, website or business runs on a domain is one registrar decision away from going dark, and the appeals process is rarely as fast as the suspension. The story is a reminder to know who controls your domain and how you would reach them in a bad week.

A Reddit user posted in r/sysadmin that Namecheap had seized and disabled all of his domains. The Hacker News listing, which links to that thread, shows 438 points and 204 comments. The only claim available from the material provided is the headline itself, which is the poster's own account and not a finding by anyone else.

That limitation matters, so here is what is not known: why the domains were disabled, whether Namecheap has responded, and whether the action has been reversed. Namecheap's side of the story was not part of the material provided. Readers should treat the post as an allegation with a very motivated author.

What can be said is how the machinery generally works. A registrar is the company that records who controls a domain name in the global directory. Translation: you do not own your domain so much as rent it, and the landlord keeps a key. Registrars typically reserve the right in their terms of service to suspend domains over abuse complaints, payment problems, suspected fraud or legal orders, and they usually do so first and sort out the paperwork afterward.

This is sensible when the domain is hosting malware and less sensible when it is hosting your payroll portal. Because a suspension typically stops websites and email at the same moment, a single decision can take an entire small operation offline. The poster's choice of forum is telling: when the support queue is the only door, people knock on the internet's window instead.

The reaction on Hacker News, 204 comments deep, suggests the topic touches a nerve among people who run infrastructure for a living. The usual advice in such threads is to spread domains across providers, keep contact details current and have a backup way to receive email. Translation: do not keep all your eggs in one registrar.

Watch for whether Namecheap says anything publicly, and whether the poster gets the domains back. Until then, the only confirmed outage is the poster's faith in the phrase 'your domains'.
