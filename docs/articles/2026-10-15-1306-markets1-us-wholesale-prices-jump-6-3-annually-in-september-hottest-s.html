Wholesale Prices Jump 6.3%, Giving Fed Fresh Reason to Look Busy

September's producer price index beat forecasts by a wide margin, and traders now put the odds of a rate hike this month at nearly 34%.

By The Daily Spin Desk · Oct 15, 2026 13:06 UTC · 3 min read
Catch upThe U.S. is at war with Iran, and the conflict has curtailed oil supply and pushed energy prices sharply higher. Inflation reports this week have shown that pressure spreading through the economy ahead of the midterm elections.
Why it mattersProducer prices tend to feed into what consumers eventually pay, and a hotter reading raises the chance the Fed increases borrowing costs rather than lowering them. That would mean pricier mortgages, car loans and credit cards on top of already elevated prices.

The producer price index, which measures what businesses receive for their goods and services, rose 1.3% in September and 6.3% from a year earlier, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Thursday, according to CNBC. Economists polled by Dow Jones had expected 0.6% and 5.5%. The monthly gain was the largest since March 2022 and the annual gain the largest since December 2022, which is the economy's way of saying it remembers that period and would like to revisit it.

Translation: the producer price index is inflation measured before the price tag reaches you. It tracks what companies charge each other, which is why it is often treated as a preview of what you will be charged later, with the cheerful implication that the trailer is worse than the film.

Energy did much of the damage. CNBC reported that the Middle East conflict curtailed oil supply and sent prices surging. A narrower measure excluding food, energy and trade still rose 0.5% on the month and 5% on the year. So removing the obvious culprit lowers the number, but leaves a figure no one would describe as relaxed.

The producer data followed Wednesday's consumer price index, which showed a 0.6% monthly rise and 3.7% annual increase, against forecasts of 0.6% and 3.6%. Core CPI, which strips out food and energy on the theory that people can live without eating and driving, rose 0.3% on the month and 2.6% on the year. Economists had expected 0.2% and 2.5%. These are small misses, but every one of them pointed up.

Also on Wednesday, the Fed's Beige Book, a collection of anecdotes about the economy from around the country, contained the most mentions of inflation-related terms on record, according to Bespoke Investment Group. The central bank has, in effect, asked the nation how things are going and received one very consistent answer.

Retail sales provided a mixed signal. They rose 0.8% in September, above the 0.4% economists expected, though slower than the gain of more than 1% in August. Whether that counts as resilient consumers or consumers paying more for the same things is a question the data does not settle.

The market reaction is where the rate story sits. According to CME's FedWatch tool, traders now see a nearly 34% chance the Fed raises rates at its meeting later this month, up from under 18% a week earlier. Investing.com reported Nasdaq 100 futures held onto losses after the producer report. Official data cited there put the fed funds rate at 3.88%, the 2-year Treasury yield at 4.79% and the 10-year at 5.21%, meaning bond investors are already demanding more than the Fed currently charges.

Translation: when long-term government borrowing costs run well above the central bank's policy rate, the market is signaling it expects rates to go up or inflation to stay put, and is charging accordingly. Unemployment was 4.2%, so the labor market gives the Fed no obvious excuse to look away.

Politics adds seasoning. CNBC noted these reports are among the last looks at inflation before the midterm election. A rate hike a few weeks before voters go to the polls is the kind of decision central bankers insist is unrelated to the calendar, and the calendar insists it has no opinion either.

Odds of 34% are still odds of a no, so the meeting is not a foregone conclusion. But a week ago the figure was under 18%, and the next data point will have to work hard to move it back.
