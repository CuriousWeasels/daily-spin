Eight States Sue OpenAI, Alleging ChatGPT Was Marketed as Safe for Kids

Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, West Virginia, Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho and Montana filed separate suits accusing the company of putting profit ahead of safety.

The wire said: Eight states sue OpenAI, saying ChatGPT isn’t safe for kids (The Verge AI)

By The Daily Spin Desk · AI Oct 15, 2026 12:56 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

OpenAI already faces lawsuits from families who say ChatGPT played a role in the deaths of loved ones, including teens. Eight states have now filed their own cases under local consumer protection laws.

Why it matters

If the states win, OpenAI could face civil penalties, court-ordered changes to how it describes ChatGPT's safety, and the deletion of data collected from minors. It also tests whether state consumer law can be used to regulate AI chatbots in the absence of a federal framework.

Eight US states have sued OpenAI, accusing it of harming children and misleading customers about the safety of ChatGPT, according to The Verge, which noted Reuters reported the story earlier. The suits were filed separately by Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, West Virginia, Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho and Montana. It is a rare show of bipartisan unity: eight separate legal departments independently concluded they would like a word.

The legal theory is consumer protection. Translation: the states say it is illegal to sell a product while describing it as safer than it is. The complaints allege OpenAI presented ChatGPT as a "safe, youth-appropriate tool that puts safety first," when it was in fact "dangerous to vulnerable users." The states list alleged harms including degrading critical thinking, encouraging paranoia and delusions, offering dangerous medical advice and promoting unhealthy behaviors and relationships.

The complaints lean on existing cases. According to The Verge, they cite lawsuits by family members of teens and adults who allege the chatbot played a role in their loved ones' deaths. Those are serious allegations, and they have not been decided by a court. What the states add is a different plaintiff: not a grieving family seeking damages, but a government seeking penalties and an order to change behavior.

The states also accuse OpenAI of collecting data from minors and not doing enough to keep underage users off the chatbot. Translation: the product has an age requirement, and the states say it works roughly like the "Are you over 18?" button on a website, which has historically been a test of confidence rather than age. The suits also cite recent rogue AI hacking incidents as evidence of what they call OpenAI's recklessness.

Arizona's complaint offers the sharpest line, claiming OpenAI "has devolved from its original mission as a public-first nonprofit" into "a cautionary tale." That is a notable thing to put in a legal filing about a company whose founding story is a nonprofit meant to keep AI safe. Whether a judge finds the corporate origin story relevant is a separate question, but it makes for a memorable opening paragraph.

OpenAI spokesperson Drew Pusateri told The Verge the company has built "industry-leading age-specific protections" and pointed to parental controls, break reminders and age-appropriate model protections. He added that "no system can be perfect" and that the company wants to work with states on policies that "apply to the entire AI industry - not just one company." That last point is a familiar one: regulate everyone, preferably in a way that can be discussed at length first.

The states are asking courts to impose civil penalties and to bar OpenAI from misleading the public about ChatGPT's safety. Some also want unlawfully collected personal data deleted. Watch whether more states join, and whether OpenAI's request for industry-wide rules turns into a lobbying effort or into actual rules.
