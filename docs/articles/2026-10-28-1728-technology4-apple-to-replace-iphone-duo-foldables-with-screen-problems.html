Apple Admits Some $2,000 Folding iPhones Developed Screen Lines, Offers Replacements

Days after launch, Apple says an inner-display issue affects 'a small number' of Duos, and declines to say how small.

The wire said: Apple to replace iPhone Duos affected by screen problem · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 28, 2026 17:28 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Apple launched the iPhone Duo, its first foldable and most expensive iPhone, last week at a starting price of £1,999 in the UK. Social media photos soon showed lines on the inner screen, and Apple has now acknowledged a problem.

Why it matters

Anyone considering a £1,999 phone should know that the first generation of a new hardware category tends to be where the engineering lessons are learned, and the buyers are the ones who pay tuition. Apple's warranty answer matters before 28 more markets get the phone on 30 October.

Apple has acknowledged that some iPhone Duo handsets have developed screen problems just days after going on sale, according to the BBC. The company said it was aware of 'an issue impacting the inner display on a small number' of phones and told affected customers to contact it for a replacement. Apple did not say how many devices are affected, which is the part of 'a small number' that most needs a number.

The Duo is Apple's first foldable and the first major iPhone redesign in almost two decades. It starts at £1,999 ($2,668) in the UK and rises to £3,199 for the best-equipped models, making it the most expensive iPhone Apple has sold. Crowds queued at Apple stores around the world last week to buy it.

Photos on social media show lines across the inner display. Some owners also posted images of cracks along the central crease, and others reported a crunching noise when opening the phone. One user, Justin Lokos, posted a picture of a faulty unit and later one of a replacement, thanking an Apple store in New York.

A note of caution is in order. The BBC reported that some of the images of faulty devices may have been created using AI. Translation: in 2025-era internet terms, a photo of a broken phone is now a claim rather than evidence, which is awkward for both critics and defenders.

Francisco Jeronimo, an analyst at IDC, said the caveat means people should be careful about drawing conclusions about the whole production run. He also said the early reports show 'that even a company with Apple's engineering resources and supply chain discipline faces a steep learning curve in foldables'. That is a polite way of saying that folding glass is hard, even for a company worth trillions.

The hinge is the culprit in the general theory. The Duo's 7.6-inch inner display is held together by a central hinge, a piece of engineering that has caused similar flaws in rivals' folding phones. Samsung's first foldable, the Galaxy Fold, ran into broken-screen reports from reviewers in 2019. Apple spent years developing the technology while Android users enjoyed a broad selection of foldables, and its reward is that it now gets to rediscover the same hinge.

Ars Technica's review was more favourable on the hardware. Reviewer Andrew Cunningham said Apple has wrangled the crease 'so close to invisibility' that he rarely thought about it. His complaints were elsewhere: he called the Duo technically impressive but not always enjoyable, said it works well as a small iPad, and could not name a way he prefers it to a regular iPhone. He also noted it lacks Face ID and a customizable Action Button.

The business stakes are larger than one batch of screens. Jeronimo said that because Apple pitched the Duo as a 'premium alternative' to existing foldables, how it handles this problem will shape buyer confidence in the whole category. He added that Apple's loyal customers and retail network, which can swap devices on the spot, are an advantage, but that it needs to clarify its warranty position quickly.

New chief executive John Ternus, who took over from Tim Cook in September, said last month that the Duo would 'redefine the experience of using a foldable phone'. Early evidence suggests it has redefined the experience of visiting an Apple Store in the first week. What to watch is whether Apple publishes a number, and what its warranty says, before the next 28 markets open on 30 October.
