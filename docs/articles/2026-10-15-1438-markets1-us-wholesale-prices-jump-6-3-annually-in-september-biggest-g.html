Wholesale Prices Jump 6.3%, Fed Funds Traders Discover Rate Hikes Exist

September's producer price index rose 1.3% on the month, more than double forecasts, as the Iran war's energy shock spread into diesel, goods and the Fed's calendar.

The wire said: Wholesale prices jumped 6.3% annually in September, biggest gain since 2022 · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 15, 2026 14:38 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The U.S. is in a war with Iran that has curtailed Middle East oil supply and pushed energy prices sharply higher. This week brought a run of inflation reports, including consumer prices on Wednesday and producer prices on Thursday.

Why it matters

Higher wholesale costs tend to reach store shelves, and a hotter inflation picture raises the odds the Fed lifts borrowing costs, which affects mortgages, credit cards and business loans. The data also lands just before the midterm elections.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Thursday that its producer price index rose 1.3% in September and 6.3% from a year earlier, according to CNBC. Economists polled by Dow Jones had forecast 0.6% and 5.5%. In forecasting terms, this is what is known as a miss, and in household terms it is what is known as the bill.

Translation: the producer price index measures what businesses receive for goods and services at the wholesale level, before a retailer adds its own markup and a sad little sign about supply chains. When wholesale prices rise, the cost tends to be passed along, with the speed of a rumor and the generosity of a toll booth.

Energy did much of the work, as the conflict with Iran curtailed oil supply. Brent crude stood at 125.44 as of Oct. 6, per the latest official data. The more interesting detail is the spread: the BLS said almost half of September's rise in processed goods for immediate demand was tied to diesel prices. Diesel moves trucks, trucks move everything, and so a war far away ends up in the price of nearly anything that sits on a pallet.

Core PPI, which strips out food and energy because they are considered volatile, still rose 0.7% on the month and 5% over 12 months. Translation: removing the part of inflation officials like to call temporary leaves a part that is not behaving like a guest who plans to leave.

Wednesday's consumer price index showed prices up 0.6% on the month and 3.7% on the year, slightly above the 3.6% forecast. Core CPI rose 0.3% against an expected 0.2%. Wednesday's Beige Book, the Fed's anecdotal tour of the economy, contained the most inflation-related terms on record, according to Bespoke Investment Group. The central bank's own reading material is now essentially a complaint thread.

Consumers are holding up, but with visible effort. Retail sales rose 0.8% in September, beating the 0.4% forecast, yet excluding autos and gas stations the gain was about 0.25%, down from roughly 1.1% a month earlier. CNBC read this as a sign that shoppers are cutting back elsewhere to pay for fuel. Headline spending looks healthy partly because gasoline costs more, which is a bit like praising a restaurant for its rising bill.

The Fed's response is the part markets are now pricing in. CME's FedWatch tool put the chance of a rate hike on Oct. 28 at nearly 34%, up from under 18% a week earlier, and about 90% of traders expect at least one hike by year-end. Fed funds stand at 3.88%, while the 10-year Treasury yield is 5.21%, so bond investors have already moved ahead of the committee. Chris Zaccarelli of Northlight Asset Management said the Fed will have 'no choice' but to raise rates again this year.

Stocks, for their part, appear unbothered. The S&P 500 closed at 7718.94 as of Oct. 14, and the VIX, Wall Street's fear gauge, sat at 16.87. Investors may know something, or may simply be waiting for the next data release to explain it. The next one arrives just before the midterms, which will determine who holds power in Washington, and who gets to explain prices to voters.
