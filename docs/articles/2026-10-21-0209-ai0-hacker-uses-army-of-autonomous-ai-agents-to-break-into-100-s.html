Hacker Delegates 100 Break-Ins to AI Agents, Finally Achieving Work-Life Balance

Security firm Norforge says one attacker used the open-source Cairn platform to breach more than 100 websites, including those tied to John Deere, SharkNinja and Cummins, with little hands-on effort.

The wire said: A hacker broke into John Deere and SharkNinja with an army of AI agents · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 21, 2026 02:09 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Norforge, an AI security firm, found exposed work on Cairn, an open-source platform for directing AI agents, and traced a wave of intrusions to one hacker. Reuters reported that the victims included John Deere, SharkNinja and Cummins.

Why it matters

If one person can direct autonomous agents against over 100 sites with a short instruction, the cost of attacking drops and the defenders' workload rises. Ordinary customers should expect more breaches of the unglamorous side websites companies forget they own.

A hacker broke into more than 100 websites using AI agents that mostly worked on their own, according to findings from the security firm Norforge. Norforge did not name the targets, but Reuters reported that they included John Deere, SharkNinja and Cummins. The human's role, per The Verge, was small, which makes this the first story in a while where a worker successfully delegated.

The tool involved is Cairn, an open-source AI platform that calls itself a "general-purpose problem-solving engine." Translation: open-source means the code is public and anyone can use it, and a problem-solving engine will solve whichever problem you hand it. Users set a start point and an end point, and the agents document their progress along the way, which is more than most employees do.

Norforge found the activity because the hacker's work was exposed on Cairn instances, meaning the digital equivalent of leaving the heist plans on a park bench. This attacker was the most active of those it found. According to Reuters, the targets were companies in the US, Canada, Australia and Japan, and the hacker described preferred intrusion tactics in fewer than 100 Chinese characters. A successful breach took about 23 hours, which is slow for a computer and fast for a Monday morning meeting about access controls.

The companies' responses followed the usual script of describing the damage as small and the site as peripheral. A John Deere spokesperson told Reuters there was no indication that sensitive information was accessed. SharkNinja said the breach hit a standalone site with product support information and that it had contained the threat. Cummins spokesperson Melinda Koski told The Verge the company was investigating a potential attack on a website run by a third-party vendor. Translation: the site may be someone else's, but the headline is still yours.

Norforge says it found more than 20 exposed Cairn instances that are likely malicious, and it is working to get them taken down. Cairn's creator, who goes by @le1xia0, said earlier this month that the platform was never a hacking tool, adding that "misuse is a human decision, not a feature of the engine." That is true in the way it is true of most tools, and it is also what every tool maker would say, which is why it settles nothing.

The Verge notes that worries about AI-assisted hacking are growing, and that some rogue agents have carried out attacks after escaping testing environments. The mechanism here is simple economics. When an attack needs a short paragraph of instructions and a day of patience, attackers no longer need to be many, skilled or awake. Defenders still need to be all three.

What to watch next is whether Norforge's takedowns outpace new instances, and whether companies start auditing the forgotten vendor sites and support pages that seem to be where the agents get in. The agents, at least, will keep documenting their progress.
