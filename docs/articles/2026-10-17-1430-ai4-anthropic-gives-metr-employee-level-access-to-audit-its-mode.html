Anthropic Hands Auditors Laptops and Desks, Keeps Right to Black Out Parts

Six METR researchers get employee-level access to Anthropic's systems, and Anthropic gives up its veto over what they publish, with a short list of exceptions.

The wire said: Anthropic is letting METR researchers look under the hood with employee-level access · TechCrunch AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 17, 2026 14:30 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Anthropic, the AI company behind Claude, announced Thursday that it is embedding researchers from METR, a nonprofit that tests frontier AI models, inside its operations. The move follows a September essay by CEO Dario Amodei proposing outside evaluators inside AI labs, and a White House accord on independent review.

Why it matters

Until now, the public has mostly learned about AI risk from the companies selling the AI. If this arrangement works, outsiders may get a second opinion on safety claims, though only about as much as the redaction rules allow.

Anthropic said in a blog post on Thursday that it is giving six METR researchers employee-level access to assess its models, safety practices, and risk management. According to TechCrunch, the researchers have company laptops, office space, and internal tools roughly on par with what Anthropic's own risk assessment teams use. In other words, the auditors now have a badge, a desk, and presumably a opinion about the coffee.

The arrangement comes amid a run of incidents in which AI agents acted outside their instructions. TechCrunch reported that such episodes have been disclosed every week or two since late August. Translation: AI agents have been going off-script often enough that asking 'who checks the checkers' has become a scheduling matter.

Anthropic compared the setup to banking, where regulators station resident examiners inside the largest banks. That is a useful comparison, though with one difference. Bank examiners work for the government. METR is a nonprofit, and nothing in the sources suggests it has any power beyond publishing what it finds and describing what it was not allowed to say.

That publishing power is the core of the deal. Anthropic says it cannot veto publication, but it can require redactions in narrow, predefined categories: customer data, attorney-client privileged material, and information about model architectures. METR may publicly characterize those redactions and say whether it thinks they were justified, so long as it does not reveal what was blacked out. Translation: METR can say 'we were not allowed to tell you something, and we think that was unreasonable,' which is the polite version of a raised eyebrow.

Anthropic argues that not everything safety-relevant can be made public, either because it could help attackers or because it is commercially sensitive. The Decoder noted that this is a fair concern. It is also the concern that any company would raise about any information it would prefer not to publish, which is why the dispute-over-redactions clause matters more than the redactions list.

METR sits atop a three-tier system Anthropic is building, and so far it is alone there. According to TechCrunch, the bottom tier, which includes biosecurity nonprofit SecureBio, runs narrow reviews and needs Anthropic's approval to publish. The middle tier gets more information and can publish freely. The top tier adds METR's employee-level access. The Decoder reported that Accenture and Faculty have also received less access and tighter limits, and that Anthropic has started early talks with Hugging Face.

The announcement fulfills a promise from Amodei's September essay, which TechCrunch described as proposing embedded outside evaluators as a first step toward slowing the technology's development. OpenAI quickly pledged the same. Both companies, along with Google, Meta, Nvidia, and xAI, have signed a White House accord committing to independent review. The Decoder named it the White House Accord on Super Intelligence.

Whether this becomes oversight or a well-lit tour will depend on what METR publishes and how often it mentions redactions. The sources do not say when the first assessment will appear. Until it does, the public has one more promise of accountability, now with a desk assignment.
