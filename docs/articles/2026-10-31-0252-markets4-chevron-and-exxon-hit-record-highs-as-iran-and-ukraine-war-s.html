Exxon and Chevron Post Record Highs as the World Runs Short of Diesel

Both oil majors beat estimates on third-quarter profits of $15.4 billion and $9.9 billion, as wars in Iran and Ukraine squeeze refining capacity.

The wire said: Chevron and Exxon hit all-time highs as refining profits soar in reaction to Iran and Ukraine wars · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 31, 2026 02:52 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Fuel supplies have been disrupted since March, when the U.S. and Israel attacked Iran and Tehran began striking ships in the Strait of Hormuz. Attacks by Ukraine, Iran and Houthi militants on refineries in Russia and the Middle East have cut global refining capacity further, CNBC reported.

Why it matters

Diesel moves freight, farm equipment and construction, so a 70% jump in its price shows up in the cost of nearly everything else. The same shortage that strains household budgets is what is producing the record corporate profits.

ExxonMobil and Chevron reported sharply higher third-quarter profits on Friday, and both stocks touched all-time highs, CNBC reported. Exxon's profit doubled to $15.4 billion from $7.5 billion a year earlier. Chevron's net income rose nearly 200% to $9.9 billion. Both companies beat Wall Street estimates, which is a feat when the quarter's main event is a global fuel emergency.

The cause is simple. Refineries in Russia and the Middle East have been hit by Ukrainian, Iranian and Houthi attacks, removing millions of barrels per day of capacity. Importers have turned to U.S. Gulf Coast refineries, which belong in large part to companies like these two. Exxon's Gulf Coast plants ran at 100% capacity. Chevron's U.S. refineries ran at 98% and processed just over 1 million barrels a day, a record.

Translation: when nobody else can make diesel, the people who still can get to name the price. Chevron's refining profit rose more than 300% to $4.9 billion. Exxon's refining arm earned about $7.2 billion in the quarter, narrowly beating its production business at about $7.1 billion. In other words, the part of the company that turns oil into fuel is now worth more than the part that finds the oil.

Exxon CEO Darren Woods told CNBC that diesel is the key crunch point because demand is very inelastic. Translation: people buy it at any price because trucks cannot run on optimism. AAA put the U.S. national average at $6.32 a gallon on Friday, up more than 70% from a year ago, after a record high in September.

Chevron CEO Mike Wirth said the system is under stress and the environment is fairly fragile. This is an unusual thing for the owner of a record-setting refinery to say, but it is also the business case. Fragility is what the quarterly results are made of.

Production did not share in the fun. Chevron's oil and gas output slipped to 3.9 million barrels a day from about 4 million, partly because of the conflict in the Middle East. Exxon has produced about 4.5 million barrels a day this year, down more than 2%. The profit comes from scarcity, not from pumping more.

The politics are awkward. CNBC noted that President Donald Trump said in August that the two companies were making too much money based on a shortage. Friday's numbers suggest the companies heard him and made even more.

The shipping picture explains why this may last. A second CNBC report said tankers are still moving crude through Hormuz despite a record number of attacks in October. At least 30 vessels were hit this month, according to the International Maritime Organization, and Brent traded above $117 on Friday. Ships are using a shuttle system, with transponders off, that pushed supertanker rates above $900,000 a day in early October. It works only because of U.S. air cover, and no one is sure how long Washington will stay engaged after Tuesday's midterms.

Watch whether the shuttle system holds and whether Congress changes the war's direction. If it does, diesel could get cheaper. Refiners would then have to find a new way to explain the record year.
