GM Beats Earnings, Announces $20 Billion Buyback, Is Punished by Shareholders Anyway

The automaker topped third-quarter estimates and narrowed its 2026 outlook, but a guidance midpoint a few cents shy of forecasts sent the stock down sharply.

The wire said: Here’s why GM’s stock is having its worst day in over a year · MarketWatch

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 20, 2026 15:53 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

General Motors reported third-quarter results on Tuesday, beating Wall Street estimates on revenue and adjusted earnings. It also narrowed its full-year guidance and announced a new $20 billion share repurchase program.

Why it matters

GM's results show how rising costs for memory chips, logistics and tariffs are squeezing even profitable carmakers, while prices stay flat. For investors, it is a reminder that a stock trades on expectations, not on whether the company did well.

General Motors reported third-quarter adjusted earnings of $3.70 per share against the $3.63 analysts expected, and revenue of $50.02 billion against $48.7 billion expected, according to CNBC, citing LSEG data. The company also narrowed its 2026 guidance and announced a new $20 billion stock buyback. Its shares fell anyway, roughly 5% during trading according to CNBC's report, and more than 7% in Jim Cramer's morning list. MarketWatch called it the stock's worst day in over a year.

The reason is the guidance midpoint. GM now expects adjusted earnings of $12.75 to $13.75 per share, a midpoint of $13.25. Analysts had penciled in $13.41. Translation: guidance is the range of profit the company says it expects for the year, and investors treat its middle as a promise. Missing the middle by 16 cents is, in market terms, a character flaw.

The company did make some of its numbers look better. Its adjusted automotive free cash flow forecast rose to between $11.5 billion and $12.5 billion, up from $9.5 billion to $11.5 billion in July. Translation: free cash flow is the money left after the bills, which is the money that pays for buybacks. The buyback follows a completed $6 billion program announced in January, and GM gave no deadline for spending the new $20 billion.

The trouble is the cost side. GM expects about $2 billion in commodity inflation, the high end of its earlier range, driven partly by logistics and DRAM chips, the memory chips that every industry now wants at once. Tariff costs are expected at the low end of a $2.5 billion to $3.5 billion range, which is the good news. Warranty costs are at the high end, between $1 billion and $1.5 billion.

Chief financial officer Paul Jacobson told CNBC that vehicle pricing is relatively flat while costs rise, and that GM will judge consumers' health next year before deciding whether to raise prices more than usual. Translation: the company is waiting to see how much customers will tolerate before sending them the bill. He also said the fourth quarter will be down sequentially, meaning lower than the third. GM's slides, per Cramer, say the path forward is more challenging, though the company expects a strong 2027 if demand holds and costs ease.

There was some tidying up. GM said the costs of its pullback from all-electric vehicles are substantially completed, totaling $11.2 billion in charges since the second half of last year, including $4.6 billion paid in cash of an expected $7.4 billion. Net income attributable to stockholders was $3.3 billion, up roughly $2 billion from a year earlier. North America produced $3.6 billion of the $4.1 billion in adjusted EBIT, which is profit before interest and taxes, so the rest of the world contributed the remaining $500 million.

Analysts, CNBC reported, called the quarter solid but worried that higher costs and pricing pressure could limit earnings growth next year. It is a familiar market arrangement: a company can do well, but it is graded against what everyone assumed it would do. The next test is whether 2027 arrives as strongly as the slides suggest, and whether memory chips become cheaper by then.
