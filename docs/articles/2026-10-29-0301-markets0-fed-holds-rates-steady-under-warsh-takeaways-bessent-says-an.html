Fed Holds Rates, Cuts Number of Meetings Where It Can Change Them

The Federal Reserve held rates at 3.75% to 4% on a 10-2 vote, while Chairman Warsh announced that in 2027 two of eight meetings will be 'strategy' sessions with no rate decision.

The wire said: Here are five key takeaways from the Fed rate decision and Warsh news conference · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 29, 2026 03:01 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The Fed raised borrowing costs last month for the first time in three years, as the U.S. war with Iran cut global crude supply and pushed inflation further from the 2% target. Kevin Warsh, who succeeded Jerome Powell, is now chairman.

Why it matters

Fewer rate-setting meetings and two dissents calling for a hike suggest borrowing costs are more likely to rise than fall, with futures traders expecting a December increase. Mortgage, loan and credit-card rates are unlikely to get relief soon.

The Federal Reserve voted 10-2 on Wednesday to keep its benchmark rate in a range of 3.75% to 4%, according to the central bank's statement. Markets had expected this, which is the Fed's preferred way to avoid disappointing anyone: do what everyone predicted and describe it as patience. The statement said that gathering more information will help the committee return inflation to 2%, and added: "The Committee will deliver price stability." Translation: a promise, in the same paragraph that says inflation remains elevated.

The two dissenters wanted a hike. Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack both preferred raising the rate by a quarter of a percentage point, per the Fed. CNBC noted that governors rarely dissent and that Cook had never done so since joining in 2022. Seema Shah of Principal Asset Management read the votes as a tightening bias and said her firm expects a hike in December and another in early 2027.

The larger surprise was procedural. CNBC reported that Warsh said the committee will still meet eight times in 2027, but one meeting will have no rate vote and no post-meeting statement. The following year there will be two of these 'strategy' meetings, leaving six chances to move rates outside of emergencies. Translation: the Fed is keeping its meeting calendar and removing the part where it does anything. Rates can still change, but there will be fewer scheduled moments for it, which markets will have to adjust to.

The press conference itself has also been rearranged. CNBC said it has been trimmed to about half an hour, reporters get one question each instead of the old two-for-one, and seating went from loosely by outlet size to alphabetical last month and reverse alphabetical this month. The stated effect is to keep microphone carriers on their toes. The unstated effect is that the chairman now controls the room more tightly than the interest rate.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, speaking to CNBC, said further hikes would not 'necessarily' be a mistake. This is notable because President Trump has repeatedly called for lower rates and pressured Powell over the issue, according to CNBC. Bessent said both he and Trump think Warsh is doing well, and asked the Fed to keep an 'open mind' about the energy supply shock being temporary. The data are less patient: Brent crude was 123.10 on Tuesday, CPI inflation was 3.74% year over year in the latest reading, and the 10-year Treasury yielded 4.99%.

Bessent also played down worries about debt tied to artificial intelligence. Elon Musk had warned that more than 200 'neocloud' firms, companies providing cloud infrastructure for AI, face 'guppy-level death and consolidation,' CNBC reported. Bessent called neoclouds a small part of the ecosystem and said financing and deals are still getting done. Translation: the Treasury is monitoring it, which is what officials say when they want you to know they are watching and also not worried.

Third-quarter GDP data are due Thursday. Bessent said he expects them to be strong, then added that he had not seen them yet. That is a forecast made with great confidence and no information, a method the Fed is currently trying to improve on, one fewer rate-setting meeting at a time.
