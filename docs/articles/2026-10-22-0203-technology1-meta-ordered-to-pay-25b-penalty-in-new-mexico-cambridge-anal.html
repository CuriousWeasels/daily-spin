New Mexico Judge Fines Meta $25 Billion, Discounting Math Down From $219.5 Billion

A court multiplied 43 million violations by $5,000, then rounded the bill down to a figure Meta still calls impossible to live with.

The wire said: Meta ordered to pay $25B penalty in New Mexico Cambridge Analytica case · Hacker News

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 22, 2026 02:03 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

New Mexico sued Meta in 2021 over how Facebook handled user data after the Cambridge Analytica scandal, and a Santa Fe jury found the company liable in September. A judge was left to set the penalty.

Why it matters

The ruling shows how state consumer-protection laws can turn per-violation fines into figures large enough to matter even to Meta, and it sets up an appeal over how far states can go. Meta says it will fight, and the 15% annual interest makes waiting expensive.

A New Mexico court has ordered Meta to pay $25 billion in penalties over its handling of user data, according to a court filing reported on Wednesday. The case grew out of the Cambridge Analytica scandal, which began in 2018 and involved a political consulting firm improperly accessing the information of 87 million users. Nearly a decade later, the invoice has arrived.

The arithmetic is simple, which is the unsettling part. A jury found more than 43 million violations of the state's unfair practices act, and each can carry a civil penalty of up to $5,000. Multiplied out, the state was awarded $219.5 billion, which the judge then reduced to $25 billion. Translation: the court did the math, looked at the result, and chose a smaller number that is still larger than the gross domestic product of many small countries.

Judge Francis Mathew was not gentle about the facts. He wrote that Facebook "did not conduct the comprehensive investigation that it promised" after the scandal broke. He cited pressure from senior executives to keep costs down, a lack of enforcement resources, inadequate investigative techniques, and a desire not to create precedent for future investigations, and he called the internal review "willfully ineffective." It is rare for a corporate review to be so thoroughly designed around not finding things.

The judge also said the maximum $5,000 per violation was warranted by the number of willful violations, the duration of the conduct, and the involvement of senior leadership. He added that interest accrues at 15% a year until the fine is paid. Translation: delay is available, but it is not free, and the rate is higher than most credit cards would dare to advertise in a friendly font.

New Mexico Attorney General Raúl Torrez said powerful technology companies have operated as though size put them beyond the reach of the law. Meta chief legal officer C.J. Mahoney said the company is confident it will prevail on appeal. He argued the ruling makes it "all but impossible to do business" in the state and that the lawsuit seeks to impose a tax on speech. Meta, in other words, is characterizing a penalty for allegedly misleading consumers as a speech policy, a framing the judge's findings about misstatements may complicate.

This is not New Mexico's first round with the company. A jury ruled in March that Meta should pay $375 million in a child safety case, and a judge later ordered $567 million into an abatement fund after finding a public nuisance. Meta said it disagreed and planned to appeal that ruling too. Separately, Meta settled a class action over Cambridge Analytica for $725 million in 2022, and in August reached a nearly $17 billion settlement with a coalition of state attorneys general. New Mexico was not part of that deal, which is how it ended up with its own trial and its own very large number.

The next stop is the appeals process, where Meta will argue the penalty is excessive or unlawful. Watch whether higher courts trim the figure, and whether other states that took the settlement now wonder if they left money on the table.
