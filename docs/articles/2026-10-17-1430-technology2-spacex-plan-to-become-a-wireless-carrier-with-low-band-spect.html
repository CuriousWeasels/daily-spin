SpaceX Buys Old Walkie-Talkie Airwaves, Prepares to Become Your Phone Company

After roughly $20 billion in EchoStar spectrum last year and $8 billion more for Nextel-era airwaves, the rocket company has decided satellites alone cannot do the job.

The wire said: SpaceX’s ambitious, expensive gamble to replace AT&T, T-Mobile, and Verizon · The Verge

Catch up

SpaceX recently agreed to buy 800 MHz spectrum, the old Nextel push-to-talk band, adding to nearly $20 billion in EchoStar spectrum it agreed to buy last year. The Verge's Decoder podcast discussed the deal with LightShed Partners analyst Walter Piecyk.

Why it matters

If SpaceX really builds a mobile network, the three big carriers could face a competitor with deep pockets and no obligation to follow their pricing habits. Whether customers see lower bills depends on whether the company can afford the towers.

SpaceX has agreed to buy a large block of low-band spectrum to expand Starlink, according to The Verge. Walter Piecyk, a partner and analyst at LightShed Partners, put the price at $8 billion for 800 MHz spectrum, on top of nearly $20 billion SpaceX already spent on separate EchoStar airwaves. A company that built its reputation on avoiding the ground is now shopping heavily for the ground.

Translation: spectrum is the government-licensed slice of radio frequencies that carries your calls and data. Low-band spectrum is the valuable kind because, as Piecyk explained, it travels farther and penetrates buildings better. Satellites work well when you have a dish on your roof. A phone inside a house, with a small battery and a small antenna, is a harder customer.

The spectrum has a history that The Verge's host described as the Nextel service, the old push-to-talk walkie-talkie network that Sprint had to divest as part of its sale to T-Mobile. Piecyk said the government wanted a viable fourth competitor in the market, so T-Mobile and Sprint had to give up low-band holdings. The airwaves then moved through EchoStar and later Grain Management, and sat there until Starlink showed up. According to the interviewer, nobody else wanted them.

Piecyk's argument is that SpaceX learned as it went. It bought EchoStar spectrum, got positive investor feedback, and went back for more. He said the FCC approval of the earlier deal, if you read the details, shows the company cannot do this by satellite alone and needs a terrestrial network. He said talk of femtocells instead of macrocells was a diversion. Translation: a femtocell is a small, local signal booster, and a macrocell is a tower. SpaceX would like to avoid building towers, a preference that has now met the physics of radio.

There is also a business reason. Piecyk said the major carriers are not going to give SpaceX a wholesale agreement, meaning they will not rent out their networks to a future rival. That leaves SpaceX two options: beg, or own its own coverage. It has chosen the one that costs billions. The Verge also noted the FCC approved deployment of 15,000 next-gen Starlink satellites.

The Verge's host was skeptical in the right places. The telecom industry, he said, has a vast graveyard of pivots, bad ideas and failed acquisitions, because announcing a plan is cheap and building a network is not. Piecyk described the market as a trillion-dollar enterprise in the US alone, which explains the ambition and the price tag in the same breath.

What happens next is mostly construction: towers, handsets, and the question of whether customers will switch from carriers they dislike to a carrier run by a rocket company. The customers are known to dislike their carriers, a rare competitive advantage that requires no launch window.
