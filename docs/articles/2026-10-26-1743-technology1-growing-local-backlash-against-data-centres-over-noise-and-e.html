Data Centres Unite Republicans and Democrats, Mostly Against Data Centres

With about 12,250 sites worldwide and 16 US states weighing bans or pauses, the buildings that run the internet have become a campaign issue.

The wire said: Big, noisy and coming to a town near you - why the data centre backlash is growing · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 26, 2026 17:43 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Data centres are the warehouse-sized buildings that house the chips and servers behind the internet and AI services such as ChatGPT and Claude. They are multiplying quickly, and the BBC reports a public backlash is growing around the world.

Why it matters

The buildout shapes local electricity prices, water supplies, noise levels and job prospects, and it is now entering US midterm politics. How the fight resolves will help decide where the AI boom physically lands.

Data centres have become politically unpopular in the United States ahead of the 3 November midterm elections, according to the BBC. Both Republicans and Democrats are coming out against their development, which in the current climate counts as a minor miracle of bipartisanship. President Donald Trump has dismissed the pushback as a "sick conspiracy", the BBC reported, which puts him at odds with a coalition that includes some of his own party.

The industry is growing quickly. Data Center Map, a company that tracks both operational and planned sites, counts about 12,250 worldwide, up from roughly 8,000 operational sites in 2021. The US leads with 4,767. Translation: the country that invented the phrase "not in my backyard" is also the one with the most backyards under construction.

The buildings are also getting larger. The biggest operational site is widely reported to be a China Telecom facility in Inner Mongolia covering about one million square metres, or roughly 140 football pitches. Larger sites are under construction, built exclusively for AI. One of them, Colossus 2, will hold hundreds of thousands of Nvidia chips and help train xAI's chatbot Grok.

That site will use 41 methane gas turbines for power. Activists say this could significantly increase local air pollution, while local regulators say the turbines will meet air quality standards. Both statements can be true at once, which is the usual way a standard works: it defines how much pollution counts as acceptable.

The complaints are practical ones. Data centres worldwide are thought to consume more electricity than all of France. They can use large amounts of water for cooling, which some experts say could cause shortages, and heavy demand on local grids worries people who fear higher energy bills. Neighbours also mention the noise and the amount of land the buildings occupy. Some 16 US states have proposed laws to ban or temporarily block new facilities, and opposition has surfaced in Mexico, France, Indonesia and the UK.

Supporters respond that data centres matter for national security and AI development, and that they bring investment, tax revenue and construction jobs. A recent Brookings report, cited by the BBC, found they do create local jobs, though fewer than industry advocates claim. Hyperscale sites, run by giants such as Amazon and Microsoft, help local employment more. Colocation sites, which lease space to tenants who are not present locally, help less. Translation: a building full of computers is a poor substitute for a building full of employees.

None of this has slowed the spending. The tech industry is expected to spend a combined $765bn on AI infrastructure in 2026, with the total passing $1tn next year, according to the BBC. The proposed escape routes include putting data centres in space or on the Moon, where solar energy is plentiful, or shifting more AI processing onto the chips already in phones and laptops. The industry's answer to local opposition is thus to look for a location with no local residents, which is at least consistent.
