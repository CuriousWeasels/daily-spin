Thieves Spoof GPS to Steal $100 Million in Data Center Gear, Proving AI Boom Has Fans

Since June, criminals have made off with more than $100 million in data center freight, NBC News reported, using hacked broker accounts, fake drivers and falsified tracking data.

The wire said: Highway thieves are spoofing GPS to steal millions in data center-bound cargo · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 27, 2026 02:33 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The AI buildout has created a steady stream of trucks carrying expensive servers, cables and power equipment to data centers. According to NBC News, as relayed by Ars Technica, thieves have worked out that this makes for an attractive target.

Why it matters

Stolen freight often carries other goods bound for ordinary customers, so the losses are not confined to the AI industry. Higher insurance and security costs tend to end up in prices, and the trucking system's reliance on trust is clearly easier to exploit than expected.

Thieves have stolen more than $100 million in data center freight since June, according to NBC News, which spoke to industry leaders, consultants and investigators advising law enforcement. Ars Technica summarized the findings. The crime is not a smash-and-grab. It is mostly a paperwork operation with a truck at the end.

The method begins on shipment-listing websites, where freight brokers post loads and trucking companies bid to carry them. Thieves first hack a broker's account and post fake shipments. Translation: they create a job that does not exist to attract workers who do. When a real trucking company responds, the thieves send a malicious link.

Once someone clicks, the thieves install software that takes control of the victim's account. They then use it to bid on real shipments, which is a sort of identity theft with a loading dock. Sometimes a legitimate driver is hired to pick up the cargo without knowing anything is wrong. Other times, according to the report, fake driver's licenses are used to get accomplices hired.

Either way, the goods end up with a driver working with the thieves, who resells the equipment. In one of the largest thefts, tracking coordinates were spoofed. Translation: the truck's GPS reported a location that was not its actual location, so the monitoring system calmly confirmed that everything was fine while the cargo went elsewhere.

The targets include servers, fiber-optic cables, computer memory chips, copper wire and bitcoin mining machines, NBC News reported. This is a shopping list that would please anyone who has read about the AI industry's spending plans. Two stolen loads found in Illinois held an estimated $300,000 in copper wire spools and about $1 million in custom-made modular power equipment, according to the Cook County Sheriff's Office.

The harm may spread beyond tech companies. In a Los Angeles County Sheriff's Department case, data center cooling equipment bound for Deepcool AI was recovered alongside nicotine pouches, televisions, shoes, printers, speakers and skin care products. That means the trailer was not a dedicated AI vault but a shared truck. Experts worry, per the report, that ordinary Americans may be broadly affected.

Some of the stolen goods have been recovered. In two of the largest thefts NBC News found, however, none of the equipment turned up, and off-duty police officers hired to escort the shipments were targeted in hit-and-runs. The report does not detail those incidents further, and neither will we. The lesson for the industry is that a security escort is only as useful as the assumption that nobody will go after the escort.

What to watch next: whether brokers tighten account verification on listing sites, and whether anyone starts treating a GPS dot as a claim rather than a fact. For now, the world's most sophisticated computing infrastructure depends on a click from a dispatcher.
