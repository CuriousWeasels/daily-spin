IMF Lifts Growth Forecast Thanks to AI Boom, Then Lists Everything That Could Go Wrong

The fund now expects 3.1% global growth in 2026, while warning that the buffers holding up the world economy are 'rapidly narrowing.'

The wire said: IMF sees milder global slowdown as AI boom offsets war hit — warns resilience is nearing limits · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 16, 2026 15:38 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The IMF released its latest forecast at its annual meetings in Bangkok, with a Middle East war still disrupting energy supplies through the Strait of Hormuz. Separately, Britain reported modest August growth ahead of a closely watched budget.

Why it matters

Growth that depends on AI spending and emergency oil reserves is growth with an expiry date, and the bill for both tends to arrive as higher borrowing costs, energy prices and taxes.

The International Monetary Fund has raised its 2026 global growth forecast to 3.1% from the 3% it projected in July, according to CNBC. That is still below last year's 3.5%. The fund kept its 2027 forecast at 3.4% and its 2026 inflation outlook at 4.7%. In other words, the news is good in the sense that a smaller bruise is good.

The credit goes mostly to artificial intelligence. The IMF said AI-related investment gave the economy a 'demand impulse,' which helped the first half of the year hold up despite the war and trade tensions. Translation: companies are spending enormous sums on chips, data centers and power plants, and spending counts as growth whether or not anyone has yet figured out what the machines will earn.

The fund appears aware of this. It called the boom 'a large, front-loaded demand shock' arriving before any broad productivity gains have materialized. It also said calm financial markets may partly reflect 'AI-related exuberance.' Translation: investors are relaxed, and the IMF would like it noted that this is not the same as being right.

The second pillar of resilience is oil. The fund now expects the Strait of Hormuz to reopen in March next year and return to its pre-war state by October. In July it had assumed mid-July and March 2027, so the reopening has been rescheduled in the way that airport departure times are rescheduled. Gulf crude exports averaged 88% of pre-conflict levels in September. Meanwhile global oil reserves are being drawn down by about 2.7 million barrels a day, close to levels that could trigger sharp price moves. Brent crude stood at $126.55 on October 14, which suggests the market has noticed.

The growth is also unevenly distributed. Thirty-two economies now account for 95% of global growth, down from 54 in April. Taiwan is forecast to grow 10.7% and South Korea 3.3% on semiconductor demand, and India 7.0%. The euro zone is expected to manage 1.1%, and the Middle East and Central Asia region to contract 0.3%, with Bahrain, Iraq, Kuwait and Qatar facing double-digit declines. The IMF's summary was that growth is being 'reallocated,' a word that sounds gentler than what it means for the countries on the losing end.

The fund also laid out the downside scenario. If AI productivity gains fail to show up, U.S. equities could fall 20% and global growth could end up 1.1% below baseline by 2028. The S&P 500 closed at 7,744.06 on October 15, so there is a considerable distance to fall. The IMF put the chance of global inflation above 5% in 2027 at about 35%, up from 30% in April.

Governments are not well placed to cushion any of this. The IMF said advanced-economy benchmark yields have reached multidecade highs, and that advanced-economy public debt is projected to reach 115% of GDP by 2031 from 108% in 2025. The U.S. 10-year Treasury yield was 5.19% on October 14. The fund urged governments to keep household support 'temporary, targeted, and nondistortionary,' which is the fiscal equivalent of telling guests the buffet is closing, but politely.

Britain offered a small illustration. The economy grew 0.1% in August, beating the 0.1% contraction economists expected, the Office for National Statistics said, as production rose 0.8% while services were flat and construction fell 0.2%. Annual growth of 2.1% was the highest since August 2022. The pound rose 0.4%, and the 10-year gilt yield fell 9 basis points to 5.3364%. Translation: a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, and the government's borrowing cost is still over 5%.

Chancellor John Healey delivers his first budget on October 28, and analysts say weaker growth squeezes his 'fiscal headroom,' meaning the room to spend or cut taxes without upsetting bond markets. Joe Nellis of MHA warned that raising taxes risks weakening spending, while unfunded commitments could push borrowing costs higher. Deutsche Bank's Sanjay Raja noted that GDP growth hit 1.8% in eight months with 95,000 fewer jobs, if one believes the HMRC data, and asked who had said productivity growth was dead. Healey will need a better answer than that on October 28.
