NASA Moves Moon Meeting Point Closer to Home, Keeps Dates Unchanged

At a lengthy Friday briefing, NASA laid out revised Artemis lander plans, with Orion docking to Starship in high-Earth orbit and a 2028 landing still on the calendar.

The wire said: NASA updates lander plans for Moon landings, finally solves long-festering Orion issue · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 25, 2026 15:06 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

NASA's Artemis program aims to return astronauts to the lunar surface, relying on commercial landers from SpaceX and Blue Origin. Earlier this year NASA canceled the Lunar Gateway, a planned lunar-orbit station, which reshaped how the pieces meet up.

Why it matters

Taxpayers are funding a race to put people back on the Moon, and the architecture determines how many things must go right before launch. Simpler docking arrangements may help the schedule, but the dates are still promises rather than hardware.

NASA used a lengthy news conference on Friday to describe in detail how it plans to return astronauts to the Moon as soon as 2028, according to Ars Technica. Much of the content had already leaked into the public record, but this was the first time the agency laid it all out and admitted the architecture had changed. Translation: the plan is now official, which is different from the plan being finished.

The headline change involves SpaceX. Under the revised approach, the Orion crew capsule will dock with the Starship lunar lander in high-Earth orbit, then travel to the Moon together. Previously the meeting was to happen in a near-rectilinear halo orbit around the Moon, a path that demands more energy and brings, in Ars Technica's words, other difficulties. Translation: it is easier to hold a meeting near home than to ask everyone to drive to a remote venue first.

Ars Technica reports that removing that requirement was the biggest request SpaceX engineers brought to NASA in early 2026. When NASA canceled the Lunar Gateway earlier this year, the problem effectively disappeared. Cancelling a space station as a way of making a rocket company's life simpler is an unusual form of customer service, but it is a form.

Blue Origin's version calls for four launches of its New Glenn rocket and no spacecraft-to-spacecraft refuelings. That omission matters, since moving propellant between vehicles in orbit is the kind of thing that works beautifully in diagrams. The plan also leaves open the possibility that the Blue Moon lander could rendezvous with Orion in low lunar orbit.

The schedule is unchanged. NASA still expects Artemis III, a demonstration that involves docking with both Blue Moon and Starship near Earth, by summer 2027. Artemis IV, the actual landing, remains on track for 2028. Note that the first mission in this sequence no longer lands anyone, which is one way to keep the landing date intact.

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said, "This is an achievable path we are on right now." He added that the agency has the mandate, resources and stakeholder support to execute. Ars Technica credited his team for taking a hard look at every element of the program, then asked the question every budget office eventually asks: can they hold to those dates?

The answer will come from hardware rather than briefings. Watch for whether the 2027 docking demonstration happens in summer, or whether summer, as a unit of time, proves flexible.
