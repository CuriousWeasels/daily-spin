AI Stocks Lose $1 Trillion in Four Days, Blame Interest Rates, China and a Newspaper Column

Twenty of the world's most valuable AI and chip stocks shed more than $1 trillion this week as rising borrowing costs met a 2029 oversupply forecast.

The wire said: Nvidia, AMD, Micron fall as AI stock sell-off deepens (CNBC Markets)

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & Economy Oct 15, 2026 12:56 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

AI and semiconductor stocks have surged on the data-center building boom, with memory chips in especially short supply. This week, hotter U.S. inflation data, climbing Treasury yields, and new worries about Chinese memory competition triggered a global sell-off.

Why it matters

The AI buildout is financed largely with borrowed money, so higher rates make every data center more expensive. Investors with index funds or retirement accounts heavy in tech are feeling the cost of that arithmetic.

Twenty of the most valuable AI and semiconductor stocks have lost more than $1 trillion in market value this week, CNBC reported, and the slide continued Thursday. In premarket trading Nvidia fell 1.8%, AMD 3.1% and Micron 4.2%. In Asia, SK Hynix dropped more than 10%, Samsung Electronics 8.73% and Advantest almost 14%. A trillion dollars is a large sum to misplace in four days, though markets manage it with no apparent embarrassment.

The proximate cause was Wednesday's inflation data. CNBC reported that a hotter-than-expected reading raised fears the Federal Reserve could raise rates further, and the Nasdaq Composite fell 1.88% to 26,692.87. The latest official figures show CPI inflation at 4.02% year over year, the 10-year Treasury yield at 5.21% and the fed funds rate at 3.88%. Translation: the government can now borrow for ten years at a higher rate than the Fed charges banks overnight, which is the bond market's way of expressing an opinion.

Yields matter because AI infrastructure is expensive. Broadcom's Hock Tan said on Monday that financing could be a bigger challenge to AI expansion than shortages of chips or electricity, according to CNBC, and Intel's chief executive also warned of financing headwinds. This is notable, since both companies sell things that the buildout needs. Warnings from the people selling the shovels tend to carry weight.

Memory chips took the worst of it. Over five days SK Hynix fell 26%, Samsung 22% and Micron about 11%. The trigger was ChangXin Memory Technologies, or CXMT, which announced a server memory product on a new architecture that could pack more data into less space. Translation: a Chinese competitor might make the same thing for less, which is a sentence that has ruined many an incumbent's week.

Analysts were skeptical. Ben Barringer of Quilter Cheviot noted that with a new architecture you can make chips, but a 50% yield means putting half of them in the bin. Rosenblatt's Kevin Cassidy said he doesn't think it will swing the supply-demand equation at all. Translation: yield is the share of chips that actually work, and announcing a breakthrough is cheaper than manufacturing one.

Then came a South Korean newspaper column. According to a CNBC translation, Maeil Business Newspaper quoted two experts expecting a flood of new supply to turn memory into a red ocean in 2029, with the supercycle possibly over by 2030. Elon Musk replied on X that China will be making a lot of RAM in 2028. CNBC noted that other analysts have not echoed a forecast that far out, which did not stop traders from pricing it in on Thursday.

The more sober numbers are less dramatic. Memory factories take three to five years to build, so many analysts expect the shortage to last until 2028. Counterpoint Research sees DRAM prices falling about 23% in 2028 from 2027, though still roughly 230% above 2025. Its analyst Jeongku Choi pointed to three- to five-year long-term agreements with upfront payments and price limits that should soften any downturn. A crash that leaves prices more than triple where they started is not how crashes are usually described.

Dan Ives of Yorkville Ives called the pullback a buying opportunity, saying rates are a financing question, not a demand question. He listed what would change his mind: cuts to hyperscaler capital spending, canceled contracts or falling GPU rental prices. MarketWatch separately pointed to reported details about Anthropic's financial progress as adding to worries about the AI ecosystem's health. Watch those three signals, and the yield on the 10-year, which for now is doing most of the talking.
