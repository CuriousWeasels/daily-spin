Anthropic Books $16.8 Billion Quarter, Investors Ask Why It Isn't Bigger

Revenue jumped about 45 percent from Q2, but slowing growth and a jittery market may shave the planned November IPO valuation below $1.5 trillion.

The wire said: Anthropic's Q3 revenue hits $16.8B but growth slows ahead of planned November IPO · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 15, 2026 14:38 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Anthropic, maker of the Claude AI models, is preparing a public stock offering reportedly set for November, before Thanksgiving. It shared third-quarter figures with prospective investors at a private meeting in San Francisco.

Why it matters

Anthropic's IPO is being treated as the biggest test yet of whether public markets will pay AI-boom prices. The outcome will shape how other AI labs raise money and whether trained models can be profitable.

Anthropic told prospective investors that third-quarter revenue reached $16.8 billion, according to multiple US media reports cited by The Decoder. That is roughly 45 percent above the second quarter and more than 3.6 times the company's entire 2025 revenue. Some investors reportedly found this disappointing, which is one way to learn that a business can grow by nearly half in three months and still get a note saying 'we were hoping for more.'

The problem is the comparison. In the second quarter, demand for the developer tool Claude Code pushed revenue up more than 140 percent from the first quarter. Translation: when your last act was a rocket launch, a very fast airplane looks like a stall. The Decoder says a summer slump is one likely factor, since people work less in those months and use fewer tokens (the small chunks of text AI models are billed by). It also points to OpenAI's strong September competitor, Astra, which Anthropic only countered in late September with Opus 5.5.

There was real good news buried in the deck. The Information reported an adjusted operating margin of 11 percent for the year through Q3, and the Financial Times said it was the second straight quarter of adjusted profitability. Translation: 'adjusted' means leaving out big costs such as stock-based compensation, which is like calculating your personal budget without counting rent paid in shares. The company is also cash-flow positive, and earned $1.70 in revenue for every dollar spent on compute, the computing power that runs the models.

That ratio matters because it suggests a trained model can make money even through months of price wars. The caveats are the usual ones: steep training and staff costs, and the risk that open-weight models, whose parameters anyone can download and run, start replacing proprietary ones like Claude.

Then there is distillation, in which one company uses another's AI outputs to train its own model. Anthropic estimates 5 to 7 percent of its revenue comes from it. The company has repeatedly accused Chinese AI labs of copying its models this way, and it has now rolled out effective countermeasures. The Decoder notes this raises the question of why Anthropic lobbied so long against the practice if it could simply stop it. Anthropic told investors the revenue will likely drop and weigh on the current quarter, in exchange for making Claude harder to imitate. It is a rare business plan in which the customers were also the competitors, and the company has chosen to be right rather than paid.

The valuation arithmetic is where the nerves show. Anthropic's private valuation reached $965 billion in May, up from under $20 billion two years earlier, and some bankers floated a $2 trillion IPO. Several investors now expect advisers to lower expectations to possibly under $1.5 trillion, partly because the company did not discuss its September annualized revenue rate or a full-year outlook. In July that rate was reportedly $65 billion. Leaving out the number everyone asked about is a confident move, or at least a memorable one.

The backdrop is unhelpful: high inflation, rising borrowing costs, public sentiment toward AI that is more negative than positive, and unresolved cybersecurity risks. Even so, The Decoder expects people to use more AI, not less, as it works its way into office jobs. A company valued at nearly a trillion dollars can survive a haircut, and the next check is whether the market agrees before Thanksgiving.
