Ad Firm Accused of Hacking Al Jazeera Via Banner Ads Was Officially 'Certified'

Citizen Lab and Confiant say Israeli firm Discover-Tech used targeted ads to deliver zero-click spyware, and the industry's approved-vendor lists did not notice.

The wire said: An Israeli firm is sending spyware through online ads, researchers say · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 15, 2026 14:38 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Researchers at Citizen Lab and Confiant say Israeli ad firm Discover-Tech ran a platform that let spyware companies pick individual targets and infect them through ads. The researchers say devices at Al Jazeera were hit with spyware linked to Candiru.

Why it matters

The same ad auctions that decide which energy drink you see can, according to the researchers, also decide who gets hacked, and nothing you click is required. Regulators and ad platforms now face pressure to treat ad code as a security risk, not just a privacy one.

An Israeli advertising company called Discover-Tech helped spyware vendors infect devices by showing targeted ads, according to a joint investigation by Citizen Lab, a research unit at the University of Toronto, and the security firm Confiant. The researchers say devices at Al Jazeera were compromised, with Citizen Lab saying at least 45 devices were targeted in 2024 and 2025. Discover-Tech presents itself as a specialised advertising agency, which is technically a description of what it was doing, just not the one customers expected.

The mechanism is the interesting part. When you load a website or app, your device triggers a real-time auction, lasting under a second, in which advertisers bid for the right to show you an ad. Translation: dozens or hundreds of companies learn who you probably are, what device you hold and roughly where you are, then compete over it, hundreds of billions of times a day according to Citizen Lab. Ads can also carry code, which is the feature the researchers say was abused.

Citizen Lab says Discover-Tech ran a platform called Controlium that let its spyware partners profile and de-anonymise users, fingerprint their devices, and pick targets by IP address, mobile ad ID or browser identifier. Translation: a zero-click attack is one where the victim does nothing. The ad merely has to appear. Citizen Lab says infection takes seconds and can give access to messages, photos, emails, calls and microphones. The researchers call the technique AdFection, and say it is the first public technical confirmation of this kind of attack delivered through targeted ads.

The scale is where the story stops being about one newsroom. Citizen Lab analysed four billion Discover-Tech bidding records and found ads on almost 200,000 websites and apps over four months. It says millions of devices were directed to contact servers belonging to Candiru, which has been blacklisted by the US government and has rebranded as Integrity Labs. Confiant's related work suggests the ads also reached networks of government entities in Europe and the US, the UN, Nato, the International Criminal Court and the International Atomic Energy Agency. Not every device contacted was infected; researchers say the contact was likely for tracking and reconnaissance.

The industry's defences did not fare well. Citizen Lab says Discover-Tech appeared on certified and approved vendor lists kept by Google and IAB Europe, and served ads through networks including Google, Yandex and Beeswax. It also used the consent frameworks built for Europe's GDPR privacy law to reach EU users. Translation: the paperwork designed to prove ads are trustworthy was, in this case, part of the delivery route. 'It's disastrous that major ad vendors and organisations apparently were neither able to detect nor prevent' the operation for years, said Citizen Lab researcher Wolfie Christl.

After disclosure, Google, IAB Europe, Beeswax, Vungle (Liftoff) and Eskimi cut Discover-Tech off, according to Citizen Lab; Reuters reported that Google and Beeswax removed it and IAB Europe suspended it. Other vendors did not respond in time. Discover-Tech had been approached for comment and none was reported in the sources. Citizen Lab also describes a separate actor, which it calls DARK CARTOGRAPHER, using the same infrastructure to link advertising IDs to phone numbers, with a further report promised.

The researchers want regulators to investigate whether Discover-Tech and Candiru broke laws, and advise high-risk users to use ad blockers, disable ad tracking and regularly reset their mobile ad IDs. That is the current state of digital self-defence: a journalist is asked to clear their advertising identifier as a security measure. The next thing to watch is whether any regulator opens an inquiry, and whether the approved-vendor lists start vetting the vendors.
