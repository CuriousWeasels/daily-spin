Boeing Promises to Build Planes Faster Eventually, Investors Hear 'Eventually' and Sell 9%

Boeing says it won't significantly raise 737 Max output until late 2027 because wings are arriving slowly, even as losses narrow and defense sales climb.

The wire said: Boeing stock sinks 9% as company says it won't significantly ramp up 737 Max production until late 2027 · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 27, 2026 16:54 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Boeing has spent years trying to stabilize production of its best-selling 737 Max after a long run of safety and quality problems. On Tuesday it reported third-quarter results and updated investors on when it expects to build more planes.

Why it matters

Boeing collects most of an aircraft's price only when the customer takes delivery, so slower production means slower cash for the company and later planes for airlines. Travelers and investors should expect the recovery to be gradual rather than quick.

Boeing said on Tuesday that it does not expect to significantly ramp up production of the 737 Max until the second half of 2027, according to CNBC. CEO Kelly Ortberg said the target is 52 planes a month, up from about 47 now. The stated reason is a slower-than-expected pace of wing production, which is a fairly fundamental part of an airplane. Boeing shares fell about 9% during the call.

The delay is the story, though the quarter itself was less grim. Boeing reported a net loss of $116 million, or 26 cents a share, compared with a $5.34 billion loss a year earlier. Translation: the company lost far less money, mostly because last year's comparison included a nearly $5 billion charge on the long-delayed 777X program. Adjusted for one-time items, the loss was 34 cents a share, against the 21-cent loss analysts had expected, according to LSEG consensus estimates.

Revenue rose 4% to $24.19 billion, ahead of the $23.84 billion forecast. Free cash flow, the cash left after paying for operations and investment, rose to $464 million from $238 million. Boeing delivered 161 commercial airplanes, one more than a year earlier, which is technically growth and the sort of increase a company would describe as stability.

The weak spot was the commercial aircraft unit, the part of Boeing that makes the things people actually fly in. Its revenue fell 3% to $10.72 billion, and it posted a negative operating margin of 6.5%. Translation: for every dollar of sales it lost about six and a half cents on operations. Boeing blamed a less favorable delivery mix, higher projected costs tied to future production rate changes, and higher estimated supply chain costs, a list that covers most of the ways to lose money building aircraft.

Defense carried the quarter. Revenue in that unit jumped 15% to $7.94 billion, and the U.S. Navy picked Boeing to develop its next-generation fighter jet. On the civil side, the FAA approved the 737 Max 10 last week, following the Max 7 in August, which completes certification of the Max family. Boeing can now legally sell every version of the plane it is not yet building fast enough.

Ortberg struck a patient tone. 'We're going to do it right,' he told CNBC's Phil LeBeau, acknowledging that everybody wants the company to go faster. In a note to staff he said expectations are rising 'and they should,' and that the company has more work to do. Investors, who had apparently hoped for faster, responded by selling.

The logic is straightforward. Airlines are waiting on planes, Boeing is waiting on wings, and shareholders are waiting on cash, which arrives mostly at delivery. Next 52 planes a month is the number to watch, along with whether the wing supply improves before 2027 turns into 2028.
