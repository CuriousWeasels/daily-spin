Alphabet Falls 10% After Spending Money It Didn't Have on Things It Says Are Worth It

The Google parent posted negative free cash flow of $9.07 billion and said 2027 capital spending will exceed this year's, which investors took as a sign the bill is still growing.

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 29, 2026 03:01 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Alphabet, Google's parent, has been spending heavily on data centers and chips to build out artificial intelligence, and investors have been asking when that spending will pay off. Its third-quarter report on Wednesday gave them a fresh occasion to ask.

Why it matters

Alphabet is one of the largest stocks in major index funds, so a 10% drop reaches many retirement accounts, and its results are a test of whether AI spending is producing returns. Its borrowing and spending plans also hint at how expensive the AI race may remain.

Alphabet shares fell more than 10% after the company reported a third-quarter earnings miss and negative free cash flow, according to CNBC. Adjusted earnings per share were $2.67 against the $3.02 analysts expected, while revenue of $127.58 billion came in just under the $128.22 billion forecast. CNBC noted it was the company's first miss on both the top and bottom lines in nearly four years. Revenue still grew 25% from a year ago, which is the sort of thing that used to count as good news.

Then there is the cash. Free cash flow, meaning the money left after a company pays for its operations and its buildings and equipment, was negative $9.07 billion. Analysts had expected negative $2.70 billion. Translation: the company expected to be a little short and was instead quite short. Net income fell to $26.17 billion from $34.98 billion a year earlier.

Capital expenditures, the money spent on data centers, chips and the other physical goods of AI, totaled $51.36 billion in the quarter. That was actually below the $55.09 billion analysts expected, a rare case of an underspend being treated as a footnote. Alphabet kept its full-year forecast at between $195 billion and $205 billion, and finance chief Anat Ashkenazi said capex will "increase significantly in 2027." CNBC put the 2027 figure above $300 billion.

To help pay for this, Alphabet has borrowed. Long-term debt reached $125.1 billion, up from $21.6 billion a year earlier, an increase of about 479%, according to CNBC. Translation: a company famous for having piles of cash is now acquiring the other thing, which is debt. Investors, who like AI in theory and receipts in practice, noticed.

The business lines offered mixed comfort. Cloud revenue more than doubled to $30.7 billion against $28.7 billion expected, and Google Cloud CEO Thomas Kurian cited "extraordinary demand" from AI labs and enterprises. Jefferies analyst Brent Thill told CNBC that cloud was the only business that beat, and that the Street wanted 100% growth and got 103%. His summary of the rest was: "Everything else missed."

Search, the core business, grew 12% to $63.35 billion, short of the $64.96 billion estimate. MarketWatch said investors worry AI is eroding the company's ability to make money from search queries, while Investing.com blamed search woes for the miss. CEO Sundar Pichai countered that AI investments drove the highest query growth rate in five years. More queries are good, though only if someone pays for them, and the market appears to be waiting on that detail.

There were other bright spots. Alphabet said its Gemini app reached 1 billion users, its fastest-growing product ever. Equity holdings, including stakes in SpaceX and Anthropic, swung from a $99.03 billion gain in the second quarter to an $8.66 billion loss in the third, which is how paper wealth behaves in both directions.

The wider market had a calmer backdrop, with the VIX volatility gauge at 17.52 as of Tuesday. Alphabet will give more detail on 2027 spending on its fourth-quarter call. Until then, investors have a $300 billion figure and an open question about when the money starts coming back.
