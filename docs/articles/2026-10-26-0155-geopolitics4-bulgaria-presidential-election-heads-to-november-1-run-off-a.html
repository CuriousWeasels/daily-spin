Bulgaria Holds Presidential Election, Needs Second One After Voters Mostly Skip First

Incumbent Iliana Yotova and former caretaker premier Andrey Gyurov advance to a November 1 run-off after turnout fell far short of the 50 percent required.

The wire said: Bulgaria election heads to a run-off amid low voter turnout · Al Jazeera

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 26, 2026 01:55 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Bulgaria voted Sunday for a president from 27 candidate pairs, amid drone attacks on ships off its coast, high energy prices and inflation, and a split over Russia and Ukraine. No candidate came close to winning outright, so the top two go to a second round.

Why it matters

The presidency is mostly ceremonial, but the officeholder commands the armed forces, can veto laws and can appoint interim governments, which matters in a country that keeps needing them. The result will signal whether Bulgaria leans toward dialogue with Moscow or closer Euro-Atlantic ties.

Bulgaria's presidential election is going to a run-off on November 1, because nobody won a majority and too few people showed up to make one possible. According to Al Jazeera, exit polls gave President Iliana Yotova between 35 and 36.5 percent, ahead of former caretaker Prime Minister Andrey Gyurov at 31 to 33.5 percent. The country now gets a second chance to hold the election it just held.

The rules explain the outcome. To win in round one, a candidate needs more than 50 percent of the vote, and more than 50 percent of registered voters must turn out. Translation: the voters must attend, and then must also agree, which is two separate things to ask of a public that has been through multiple elections and coalition collapses in recent years.

Turnout did not cooperate. Al Jazeera, citing the Measure polling agency, put it at 22.2 percent as of 8pm local time, while DW reported that turnout was below 40 percent and described it as a record low. The two outlets cite different pollsters and different measures, and the figures are not directly comparable, but both land well short of 50. Official results are expected within 48 hours, according to DW.

On the ballot were 27 pairs of candidates for president and vice president, nearly half of them openly pro-Kremlin, according to DW. That is a wide menu for an electorate that mostly declined to order. Yotova, 62, is backed by Prime Minister Rumen Radev's Progressive Bulgaria and the Socialist Party. DW reports she favors dialogue with Moscow and opposes military aid to Kyiv. Al Jazeera notes she is expected to keep Bulgaria in line with Europe while seeking better relations with Russia.

Gyurov, 50, is backed by the pro-Western opposition and, as caretaker prime minister, signed a security cooperation agreement with Ukraine. He has criticized Radev's foreign policy. Far-right Vazrazhdane leader Kostadin Kostadinov was third in DW's pre-election polling at 10 to 13 percent, which means his voters now hold something every campaign wants: a decision to make.

The war was never far from the campaign. DW reported that four merchant ships were allegedly hit by drones off Bulgaria's coast this month, and that Starlink antennas were set on fire near Sofia. Radev said the attacks appeared to benefit Russia and summoned the Russian ambassador, though DW notes his government has declined to blame Moscow directly. Translation: the government has noticed who benefits, but has not yet said who did it.

At home, the issues are less dramatic and arguably more urgent. Bulgaria is the EU's poorest member, according to DW, and energy prices, inflation and corruption weighed on voters. Yotova spoke of poverty and inequality after voting, and Gyurov invited her to a debate. Interior Minister Ivan Demerdzhiev said the vote went peacefully and without the usual vote buying, Bulgarian National Television reported, a compliment that works best if you do not think about the word usual.

The next week will test whether the 22 to 40 percent that showed up can persuade the rest to come. Two candidates, two directions, one week, and a public that has so far treated the choice as optional.
