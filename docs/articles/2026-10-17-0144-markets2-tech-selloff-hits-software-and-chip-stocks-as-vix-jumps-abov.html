Tech Selloff Finds Software and Chips Finally Agreeing on Something: Down

The Nasdaq fell 2% on Friday, its worst day since June 5, and the VIX topped 22 for the first time in four months as traders bought protection for November.

The wire said: Nowhere to hide: Tech selloff hits software and chip stocks alike · MarketWatch

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 17, 2026 01:44 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Software and semiconductor stocks have mostly traded separately this year, but both dropped Friday amid worries about AI and interest rates. CoreWeave, a so-called neo-cloud provider, lost 13%, and the VIX volatility index jumped above 22.

Why it matters

If you own an index fund, you likely own plenty of these stocks, and the options market is signaling that some large investors expect more turbulence rather than less. Higher interest rates make it harder for expensive growth stocks to justify their prices.

Technology stocks fell broadly on Friday, and MarketWatch noted that software and semiconductor shares, which have not moved together much this year, dropped at the same time. According to CNBC, the Nasdaq shed 2%, its worst day since June 5, as cracks appeared in the AI trade and worries grew around CoreWeave, which fell 13%. Diversification, the idea that owning different things protects you, had a rough afternoon.

CNBC reported that the Cboe Volatility Index, or VIX, rose above 22 for the first time in four months. Translation: the VIX is Wall Street's fear gauge, a number derived from options prices that rises when investors pay more for insurance against big moves. Fear is, conveniently, something the options market can package and sell by the contract.

The more interesting action was in the November contracts. CNBC said one trader bought 9,200 November 18 calls at a 20 strike for $1.7 million, and 12,500 of the 23-strike calls for $1.1 million. Translation: a call option pays off if the underlying number ends up above a chosen level, so these traders are betting the VIX will be above roughly 23 in the third week of November. In other words, they are paying seven figures for the right to be right about anxiety.

Of the ten most popular VIX contracts bought Friday, nine were calls, and six had strikes above where the VIX settled after its early spike. Nearly $27 million in premium changed hands across those nine, about 36% of all VIX option premium traded that day. Brent Kochuba of SpotGamma, a CNBC contributor, said traders appear to be cashing in October positions and rolling them forward to hedge a bigger market downturn, noting several November call trades above $1 million.

MarketWatch's technical outlook was not cheerful either. A separate piece argued that the Nasdaq Composite's chart suggests investors should not buy the dip yet, because signs point to more pain ahead. Chart-based advice has a mixed record, but it has the advantage of being available at all hours.

The backdrop is not soothing. The latest figures show the 10-year Treasury yield at 5.1%, the 2-year at 4.77%, and the Fed funds rate at 3.88%, with September CPI inflation at 3.74% and Brent crude at $126.55. Translation: borrowing is expensive, inflation is above comfortable, and oil is not helping. Those are the conditions in which investors begin asking what exactly they are paying for.

One caution on the numbers: the data feed lists the VIX at 16.46 as of October 15, a day before Friday's move, so it predates the spike and says nothing about it. The next test, CNBC said, comes when Tesla, SpaceX and Meta report earnings. Those results will show whether the fear gauge was early or merely well paid.
