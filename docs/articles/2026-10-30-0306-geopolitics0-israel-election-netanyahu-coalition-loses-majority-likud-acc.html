Netanyahu Loses Majority, Alleges Voters Who Turned Out Were Probably Not Supposed To

After a record 19 Arab-party seats left neither bloc with a majority, the prime minister's Likud accused those parties of stealing the vote, while Israel's own election officials said they found no basis for it.

The wire said: Netanyahu attacks Israel’s Arab parties as he falls short of majority · Al Jazeera

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 30, 2026 03:06 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Israel held an election on Tuesday that ended with neither Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc nor the Jewish opposition led by Gadi Eisenkot winning a majority in the 120-seat Knesset. Arab-led parties surged in turnout and now hold a record 19 seats.

Why it matters

Whoever forms the next government will depend on a handful of seats and on whether Arab parties are treated as legitimate partners or as suspects. The dispute over the vote's integrity could shape both coalition talks and public trust in the result.

Final results confirmed that Netanyahu's bloc won 53 seats and Eisenkot's bloc won 48, according to the BBC. Translation: a majority needs 61 of the 120 seats, and nobody is close. The Joint List and Ra'am, two Arab-led lists, won a record 19 seats between them after a surge in Arab turnout, which makes them the people both sides now need to think about, whether or not they enjoy it.

Netanyahu's Likud responded by accusing the Arab-led parties of "stealing" the election, according to the BBC. On Thursday it also alleged that voters had been "bribed". The party called the matter a "political earthquake" and "an attack on democracy". Translation: the vote count had been a problem, so the vote itself became one.

The allegations rest on a request from the head of the Central Elections Committee, who asked the Shin Bet, Israel's domestic security agency, to investigate "serious suspicions" of foreign interference. Israeli media reported the committee had been given a claim that Qatar funded efforts to raise turnout in Arab communities, and the Shin Bet is examining whether the parties received illegal funding from Qatar and Turkey. Qatar's foreign ministry "categorically" denied it, calling the claims "ridiculous and dangerous" and accusing Israeli politicians of a disinformation campaign. The Joint List called the claim baseless.

The investigators themselves have been less dramatic than the politicians. The Shin Bet clarified that its inquiry did not implicate an entire community that had voted legally. Acting Central Elections Committee director general Dean Livne said there was "no basis for allegations of systematic violations of election integrity", according to the BBC. Eisenkot, who was briefed on the investigation, said it was too early to tell "if anything happened at all".

Critics read the timing as strategy, and the arithmetic supports them. Arab parties are the only route by which Eisenkot's bloc can reach power, and Eisenkot and all but one member of his bloc have previously refused to govern with them. Making those parties politically toxic would close that route. Eisenkot accused Netanyahu of running an "orchestrated and organized campaign to delegitimize the election results". Sami Abu Shehadeh of Balad, part of the Joint List, called the allegations a "racist blood libel".

The campaign did not begin on Thursday. The BBC reported that Netanyahu spent much of election day posting that high Arab turnout was an "emergency" and a "matter of life or death" for the Israeli right, and shared AI-generated images of Arab voters at polling stations to rally right-wing voters. Arab citizens make up about one fifth of Israel's population, and many have long boycotted elections on the grounds that voting legitimises a system that treats them as second-class. This time many voted to remove the most right-wing coalition in Israel's history. The warning to turn out apparently reached both audiences.

That leaves three plausible outcomes, according to the BBC: a national unity government with Netanyahu and Eisenkot, a right-wing coalition built with defectors from Eisenkot's bloc, or an Eisenkot-led government backed by the Arab parties. A Hebrew University snap poll found that the favorite among voters in every opposition party was an Eisenkot minority government with Ra'am, supported from outside by the Joint List. Voters have offered a preference. Whether the politicians treat it as information or as an interference campaign is the next thing to watch.
