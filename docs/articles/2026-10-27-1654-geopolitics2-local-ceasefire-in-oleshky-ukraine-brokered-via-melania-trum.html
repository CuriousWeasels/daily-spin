Russia and Ukraine Pause Shooting Over Oleshky, Thanks to First Lady's Mediation

A limited truce in the occupied Kherson city is meant to let aid in and civilians out on Friday, though almost no one can confirm it is holding.

Catch up

Oleshky, a Russian-occupied city on the Dnipro River in the Kherson region, has had no electricity, gas or water for three years, and residents have been eating garden weeds. Both sides now say a limited ceasefire is in effect, which the White House credits to Melania Trump.

Why it matters

Up to 6,000 people in and around Oleshky depend on a pause in fighting that nobody can independently verify, and the evacuation routes run through mined ground. How it plays out will show whether even narrow humanitarian truces can work in this war.

A localized truce began early Tuesday in Oleshky, and both Ukrainian officials and Russia's defence ministry say it is being respected, according to the BBC. It is meant to allow aid deliveries and a civilian evacuation planned for Friday. The White House said the deal was secured by US First Lady Melania Trump, which makes it one of the stranger entries in the long history of wartime diplomacy.

Oleshky sits on the front line in the Kherson region, with the Dnipro River on one side and Russian forces on the other. Before the war it had 24,000 residents; Russia seized it in the first days of the February 2022 invasion. Kyiv believes up to 6,000 people, including 200 children, remain in the city and its surroundings, with only about 1,700 in the city itself. No shops are open, and the city has had no electricity, gas or water for three years. Many residents told the BBC they have been cooking garden weeds to survive, and NPR reported that some have also been eating pickles.

The difficulty is that leaving is nearly as dangerous as staying. Routes out have been mined and are subject to drone attack. Serhii, a resident who fled about three weeks ago, told the BBC he walked 24km through minefields, passing within a metre of mines. Translation: the safest way out of town was previously a long walk in which you could not speak.

The truce is designed to fix this. Russia's defence ministry said its Dnepr group units have been doing what it called 'engineering reconnaissance' and clearing access roads of explosive and booby-trap devices. It also said it stopped firing toward Kherson, across the river, at 04:00 Moscow time. According to Ukrainian MP Davyd Arakhamia, the truce runs until 23:59 local time on 1 November.

Nobody involved is fully relaxed about it. President Zelensky warned that routes demined by Russia will lead civilians to 'head towards Russia and stay there', though he said some would reach Ukrainian-controlled territory one way or another. Residents fear that Russian forces may check phones for signs of Ukrainian support, and one person with relatives in the city fears that shooting could resume during the evacuation and Ukraine would be blamed. One resident told the BBC, 'I don't believe in Russia's humanity.'

The BBC also notes that verification is hard. Communications are patchy and many residents are afraid to speak. Details of the evacuation, including how to register and whether pets may come, were initially unclear. Ukraine's general staff asked the public not to spread rumours about routes and timings, which is a reasonable request in a place where the information environment currently consists of weeds and rumour.

The Kremlin acknowledged last month that Oleshky faced 'a critical situation' and 'problems with supplies of food and medicine', but blamed Kyiv. Pope Leo XIV earlier this month appealed to Putin to allow aid in and to Zelensky to cooperate on a ceasefire. Ukrainian rights commissioner Dmytro Lubinets said the mission is coordinated with Russia's rights commissioner, Yana Lantratova, with Mrs. Trump mediating.

Elsewhere the war goes on. Attacks on Kyiv on Monday evening hit a university and a bridge, killing one person and injuring 14, and a Ukrainian drone strike on Bryansk killed one and injured six. Friday's evacuation is the test: a truce that holds for one road is useful, but it needs to hold long enough for the people on it to get to the end.
