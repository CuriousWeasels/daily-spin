Pennsylvania Mandates AI Ad Labels, Defines Key Terms as 'Significantly,' 'Evidently,' and 'False'

Gov. Josh Shapiro signed a law requiring disclosures on AI-generated ads, though the exemptions leave plenty of room for interpretation.

The wire said: Pennsylvania will require labels on AI-generated ads · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 26, 2026 01:55 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro on Thursday signed the Artificial Intelligence Deceptive Advertising Act, making the state one of the first to require disclosures on AI-generated content. The law covers images, video, audio and text.

Why it matters

Because the label must appear wherever an ad airs in Pennsylvania, national advertisers may end up disclosing AI use well beyond the state's borders. Whether viewers actually see many labels will depend on how courts and regulators read the vague terms.

Pennsylvania will now require advertisers to say so when an ad has been made with artificial intelligence. Governor Josh Shapiro signed the Artificial Intelligence Deceptive Advertising Act on Thursday, according to The Verge. The law applies to any ad, in any format, that "has been significantly modified or generated by artificial intelligence," and the label must appear in the same format as the ad itself. A video ad needs a video disclosure, an audio ad needs an audio one, and a text ad needs text.

The scope is broad. The Verge noted that it covers TV commercials from multi-billion-dollar companies and also the ugly, crowded flyers restaurants make for their wing specials. Rarely does a single statute unite a global consumer brand and a sports bar in a shared compliance burden.

The law also follows the ad, not the advertiser. According to The Verge, the label is required regardless of where the ad was created, so viewers in other states may see disclosures on commercials that are also expected to air in Pennsylvania. Translation: a state legislature has, in effect, written a rule for national advertising, because it is cheaper for a company to label everything once than to maintain a separate edit for one commonwealth.

Then come the loopholes, which The Verge described as plentiful. First, what counts as "significantly modified" is subjective. Translation: nobody has said how much AI retouching turns a photo into an AI photo. Is it a full synthetic spokesperson, or a background that was quietly cleaned up? The statute does not say, which is convenient for anyone who would prefer not to find out.

Second, the law carves out "parody, satire, commentary or criticism," along with anything "evidently fictional." The Verge pointed out that the meaning of "evidently fictional" is unclear. Most advertising is, in a loose sense, fiction, so this exemption has plenty of room to grow.

Third, the wording suggests a disclosure is needed only when content "uses synthetic content in a manner that creates a false consumer perception" about a product or service. That leaves open what counts as false and who decides. A rule that requires a label only when the ad is already misleading is a little like a speed limit that applies only to cars that are speeding.

The office of State Senator Nick Pisciottano, who introduced the bill, did not immediately return The Verge's request for comment. That leaves the open questions to be answered the usual way: by regulators, lawyers and, eventually, a judge with a strong opinion about wing flyers.

For now, the practical effect is that Pennsylvania has established the principle that people should be told when a machine made the ad. What remains to be seen is how often the machine, or the company using it, will be required to admit it.
