Bank of England Official Notes Bond Market Now Run by Leveraged Hedge Funds, Which Is Fine Until It Isn't

Sarah Breeden tells CNBC that hedge funds' growing role in government debt makes a market shock spreading across borders more likely than before, a view the IMF shares.

The wire said: Why hedge funds could be making a bond market shock more likely · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 19, 2026 01:58 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Government bond yields hit multi-decade highs this month in the US and UK, and French yields have climbed on fiscal worries. Hedge funds now hold a record share of the Treasury market, and regulators are watching one popular strategy, the basis trade.

Why it matters

Government bond yields set the cost of mortgages, business loans and public borrowing. If leveraged traders are forced to sell all at once, a rough week in one market can become a rough year for everyone else.

Bank of England deputy governor Sarah Breeden told CNBC on Thursday that the likelihood of a broader market shock is "greater than what it was," because hedge funds have become a bigger part of the global government bond market. Breeden, who handles financial stability at the central bank and sits on its rate-setting committee, spoke on the sidelines of the IMF-World Bank meetings in Bangkok. Her message, in short: the people holding the safest assets in finance are no longer the people who used to.

Breeden said the investors in core government bond markets have changed. Insurance companies and pension funds, the so-called real money investors, are less dominant. Translation: the buyers who used to hold bonds for decades because they owe retirees money in decades are being joined by buyers who hold them until the spreadsheet says otherwise. In her words, hedge funds are "price-sensitive, leveraged, international," which is three adjectives that each make a shock easier to pass along.

The IMF said much the same in its Global Financial Stability Report on Tuesday. It put hedge funds' gross assets at about $13 trillion and acknowledged that they help markets function by supplying liquidity and efficiency. It also warned that leverage and crowded trades could turbo-charge stress in turmoil, with spillovers into areas such as banks' prime brokerage businesses. Translation: leverage is borrowed money, a crowded trade is when everyone has the same idea, and prime brokerage is the part of a bank that lends to the people having it.

The scrutiny falls heavily on the basis trade. CNBC explains that hedge funds buy cash Treasurys and short the matching futures contracts, hoping to pocket the small price gap between them. Because the gap is small, funds borrow heavily to make it worth the effort. Translation: a tiny profit, multiplied by a very large loan, is still a profit right up to the moment someone asks for the money back.

The numbers explain the attention. According to US Office of Financial Research data published on Aug. 19, hedge funds held a record 7% of the $30 trillion Treasury market at the end of 2025. Earlier this month, 10-year and 30-year Treasury yields reached their highest levels since 2002, and 10-year UK gilt yields touched multi-decade highs. The latest official data put the 10-year Treasury at 5.1% and the 2-year at 4.77%, a yield curve with the 10-year well above the 2-year, which is how bond markets look when they want to be paid for worrying.

France adds a second source of unease. CNBC reported that yields on French government bonds, known as OATs, have risen sharply in recent weeks on concerns about the country's fiscal outlook, fueling fears of a fresh euro zone debt crisis. Breeden's point is that when funds are leveraged and international, trouble in Paris can become a margin call in New York by way of London, without anyone having to change their mind about France.

For now, the broader mood is calm. The VIX, Wall Street's fear gauge, stood at 16.46 as of Oct. 15, which is not the level of an anxious market. That is the awkward part of financial stability warnings: they are most useful when nothing is happening, and least listened to for the same reason. Watch whether regulators follow the warnings with rules on leverage, or just with more reports.
