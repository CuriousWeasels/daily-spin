Apple Adds Touchscreen to MacBook Pro, Quietly Retiring Its Toaster-Refrigerator Theory

The $2,899 laptop arrives November 6th, more than a decade after Apple's leadership said touchscreen laptops were a bad idea.

The wire said: Apple announces its first touchscreen MacBook Pro · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 28, 2026 17:28 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Apple held a "Pro and tell" event Wednesday, closing a week of launches that included an OLED iPad Mini and M6-powered iMacs. The finale was a MacBook Pro with a touchscreen, a feature Apple executives had long dismissed.

Why it matters

Buyers face a confusing lineup: the touchscreen model costs $900 more than the new entry-level MacBook Pro and runs an older-generation chip. Anyone shopping for a Mac should check which chip they are actually getting before preordering.

Apple on Wednesday announced its first MacBook Pro with a touchscreen, according to The Verge. The 14-inch model starts at $2,899, the 16-inch at $3,399, and both can be preordered now ahead of a November 6th release. The company had previously treated this feature as a matter of principle, and has now treated it as a matter of a product cycle.

The history is not subtle. The Verge noted that Tim Cook said in 2012 that you can converge a toaster and a refrigerator, but the result is probably not pleasing. In 2010, Steve Jobs called touchscreen laptops "ergonomically terrible." Translation: Apple spent roughly fifteen years explaining why customers did not want the thing that Windows laptops had made mainstream well over a decade ago.

Apple is presenting the new machine as a different thing from those earlier touchscreen laptops. It says the touch sensor is built "directly into the display panel" rather than layered on top, which it claims reduces latency. Translation: the delay between your finger touching glass and the computer noticing should be shorter. Apple suggests uses such as testing iOS and iPadOS apps on the display or playing software instruments on the screen, which is a specific and rather modest list for a reversal of this size.

The hardware is otherwise ambitious. The display is what Apple calls "ProLED XDR," a tandem OLED panel with up to 2,000 nits of peak SDR brightness, a 120Hz refresh rate and a 10,000,000:1 contrast ratio. Translation: a nit is a unit of brightness, and 2,000 is a lot of them. There is also a Dynamic Island, the iPhone's cutout-turned-notification area, plus a redesigned hinge, which is useful when customers begin poking the screen. The glowing Apple logo on the lid returns, and the 14-inch model weighs 3 pounds.

Ports include three Thunderbolt 5 connections, MagSafe 3, HDMI, an SD card reader and a headphone jack. Apple claims up to 24 hours of battery life, with the usual "up to" doing a great deal of work. The machine supports three external displays, or four with an M5 Max.

The chip naming is where it gets awkward. The touchscreen model uses M5 Pro or M5 Max chips, not the new M6 found in the other MacBook Pro announced this week. That one is a 14-inch model starting at $1,999, with 12-core CPU and GPU, 16GB of memory and 1TB of storage. So Apple's newest laptop feature arrives on a chip generation that its cheaper laptop has already moved past.

The rest of the week was a conventional refresh. The iPad Mini gets an 8.5-inch OLED display, an A20 Pro chip and a $699 starting price. The M6 iMac starts at $1,499 and, Apple says, delivers up to 5x faster on-device AI performance than the M4 model. Keynote, Numbers and Pages also get an AI chatbot built with models from Apple and OpenAI.

The Verge noted that the launches are among the first under CEO John Ternus, who previously led hardware engineering. Having Ternus unveil the toaster-refrigerator is a tidy way to change a position without anyone having to admit to it. What to watch next is whether people actually touch the screen, or whether it becomes the most expensive fingerprint collector in the lineup.
