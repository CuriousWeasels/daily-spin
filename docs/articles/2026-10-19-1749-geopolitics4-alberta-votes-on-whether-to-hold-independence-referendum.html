Alberta Votes on Whether to Schedule a Vote on Whether to Leave Canada

Monday's referendum asks only whether to begin the legal process for a binding separation vote, which could come as soon as 2027, and polls suggest most Albertans would stay.

The wire said: ‘This is not poker’: Alberta goes to polls in referendum that has exposed divisions, not least among separatists · The Guardian

Catch up

Premier Danielle Smith called Monday's vote after a separatist petition gathered about 300,000 signatures, even though a court had blocked that petition for failing to consult First Nations. She says she opposes separation herself.

Why it matters

Even a vote to remain would not end the argument, and even a vote to proceed would only start a legal process. In the meantime, opponents warn that uncertainty alone could drive jobs and investment out of a province of about five million people.

Albertans go to the polls on Monday to answer a question that is not, strictly speaking, whether Alberta should leave Canada. According to the BBC, it is whether the provincial government should begin the legal process under the Canadian Constitution to hold a binding referendum on separation, which Premier Danielle Smith says could happen as soon as 2027. Translation: this is a vote to decide whether to schedule a vote, which is democracy's equivalent of a meeting to plan the meeting.

The referendum exists because a grassroots group collected about 300,000 signatures asking for a binding vote on separation, the BBC reported. A court blocked that petition, saying it failed to consult the First Nations whose land would be affected. Smith let the process go ahead anyway, and First Nations groups have called that decision "undemocratic" and "authoritarian". Smith, who opposes separation, defends it as a chance for residents to have their say. The Guardian reports she called the vote after pressure from within her own United Conservative Party, having argued for years that none was needed.

The premier has thus organised a vote on a cause she does not support, while declining to campaign for it. In June, the Guardian reports, she put the cost of separating at about C$400bn for the transition, plus C$25bn to C$50bn a year in the costs of starting a new country. Some of her supporters accused her of betraying the independence movement. Translation: she is being criticised for pricing the product honestly.

Opponents have leaned heavily on the numbers. The University of Calgary's school of public policy estimated a cost of up to C$170bn over five years and warned of high unpredictability beyond that. Opponents also compare the uncertainty to Brexit, a comparison that Prime Minister Mark Carney is unusually qualified to make, having served as governor of the Bank of England. "This is not poker," he told a rally. "A vote against Canada is a dangerous bluff." A counter-petition opposing separation drew more than 400,000 signatures, according to the BBC.

The separatist side is less unified than its slogan. The Guardian reports that Keith Wilson, an architect of the movement, published a 214-page transition plan and called most of its tasks "super easy". Bill Jones of the Wildrose Independence party said leaders are moving too fast without a police force or pension system, and that Alberta would be "a failed state for generations" if it left today. Translation: even some separatists think the plan needs more plan.

The ballot also carries nine unrelated questions, several on immigration control, and the Guardian says the result has been a small civil war over lawn signs. One campaign urges "no to the nine", some angry separatists urge "decline the 9", and Smith answers with "yes to the rest". Voters may reasonably wonder whether they are electing a province or filling out a very long customer survey.

Polls suggest most Albertans would vote to remain, the BBC reported, though Chris Scott of the pro-separatist group Let's Talk Alberta argues the campaign has already succeeded because "the conversation has started". That is a strategic position worth noting: a movement that can win by losing needs no majority at all. Election officials will count ballots by hand, with up to 48 hours allowed, and results on the separation question are expected in the early hours of Tuesday.

The vote was also overshadowed by a deadly fire at CFB Wainwright on Saturday that reached the nearby town. The Guardian reports that Canada's armed forces announced multiple deaths and injuries, and that it muted enthusiasm on both sides. Whatever the result, the underlying grievances over energy policy and federal finances will still be there on Tuesday, with a pipeline proposal and a lot of lawn signs to sort out.
