Treasury Yields Fall Below 5% as Investors Rediscover Government Debt as a Hiding Place

The 10-year yield dropped more than 17 basis points to 4.972% on Thursday, its biggest one-day decline since the March 2023 banking crisis, as AI worries and oil prices sent investors looking for cover.

The wire said: 10-year Treasury yield tumbles below 5% to lowest level in nearly a month · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 23, 2026 02:08 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Earlier this month, 10- and 30-year Treasury yields reached their highest levels in more than two decades on worries about inflation and energy prices. This week, a sell-off in tech stocks and rising oil prices pushed money back into bonds.

Why it matters

The 10-year yield is used to price mortgages, auto loans and credit card debt, so a sharp move in it eventually reaches household budgets. Whether the drop lasts depends on oil, inflation and how much the Fed feels it must do about both.

The 10-year Treasury yield fell more than 17 basis points on Thursday to 4.972%, dipping below 5% for the first time since Sept. 23, CNBC reported. Translation: a basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point, so 17 of them is a large move for an asset whose main selling point is that nothing exciting happens to it. Yields and prices move in opposite directions, so a falling yield means investors were buying bonds in volume.

The 2-year yield, which closely tracks expectations for Federal Reserve policy, fell more than 16 basis points to 4.659%. The 30-year, which CNBC describes as most sensitive to geopolitical developments, fell more than 14 basis points to 5.296%. According to CNBC and MarketWatch, it was the biggest one-day decline in the 10-year since March 10, 2023, in the middle of the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank. That comparison sounds alarming, though one strategist did not read it that way.

That strategist was PGIM's Tipp, quoted by MarketWatch calling it a "take-back trade," not an all-out fear of recession type of situation. Translation: investors sold bonds too hard earlier this month, drove yields to two-decade highs, and are now buying some back because the price looked better. CNBC reported that above 5%, yields began to look like an attractive entry point, even with inflation above 3%.

The immediate trigger was stock market stress. Investors have been asking about the billions being spent to build artificial intelligence networks. Mitch Goldberg of ClientFirst Strategy said people are "terrified" of the amount of bond issuance by AI-related companies. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang told CNBC that demand for neoclouds, specialized cloud providers built for AI workloads, is "off the charts," but that capital is scarce. Demand that is off the charts and money that is hard to find are, in finance, the two ingredients of a bond sale.

Oil supplied the second push. West Texas Intermediate futures closed up 2.39% at $99.47 a barrel, and Brent rose 4% to $113.17, after the New York Times reported, citing unnamed U.S. officials, that the U.S. and Israel are weighing resuming strikes on Iran in a "short but intensive" operation after the Nov. 3 midterm elections. Note the scheduling: even the contemplated military operation is waiting for the vote count, which is a rare case of the calendar being treated as a strategic asset.

Not everyone sees a clean story. Gregory Faranello of South Street Securities told CNBC that there is no single risk at the moment, and that geopolitics matters most. He warned that a pinched consumer complicates life for central bankers, and that if energy prices stay elevated it could force the Fed to do even more, which would not be good for bonds. In other words, the safe haven is safe provided that the thing driving people into it does not also force the Fed to hike.

Official data points to why the Fed is in a bind. The 10-year yield stood at 5.13% as of Oct. 21 and the 2-year at 4.81%, against a fed funds rate of 3.88%. Annual CPI inflation was 3.74% as of Sept. 1, and Brent was at 115.22 as of Oct. 20, a bit above Thursday's close. Unemployment was 4.2%. Those figures differ from Thursday's intraday numbers because they come from different dates.

Watch the 5% line next. MarketWatch described a stock market splitting into two camps, with CNN's Fear & Greed index signaling fear. If oil keeps climbing, bond buyers may find that the haven has a mortgage rate attached to it.
