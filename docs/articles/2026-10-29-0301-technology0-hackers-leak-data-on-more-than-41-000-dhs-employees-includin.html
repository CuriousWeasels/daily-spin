Agency That Tracks Everyone Else's Data Loses 41,000 Employees' Records Via Login Service

A hacker group called Department of Peace says it used Okta to pull DHS staff details, which a nonprofit then published as the 'Icepick' dataset.

The wire said: A hacker stole more than 40,000 DHS employee records · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 29, 2026 03:01 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

A group calling itself Department of Peace says it obtained personal and professional details of more than 41,000 Department of Homeland Security employees, including ICE and CBP staff. Transparency nonprofit Distributed Denial of Secrets is now hosting the data.

Why it matters

The leak raises safety and legal questions about masked immigration agents and about the surveillance tools the data reportedly names. It is also the latest in a run of breaches at US government bodies, which suggests the problem is not one agency's bad week.

The Department of Homeland Security has had its staff directory published online. According to The Verge, citing TechCrunch and Wired, a hacker calling itself Department of Peace took the names, job titles, phone numbers, email addresses and office locations of more than 41,000 employees. The BBC reported the same figure. The agency responsible for protecting the homeland has now, in a sense, provided a very complete map of it.

The breakdown is as follows. About 21,000 records belong to Customs and Border Protection. ICE accounts for nearly 7,000 according to The Verge, and nearly 6,600 according to the BBC, so the sources disagree slightly on that number. The rest cover other parts of DHS, including Homeland Security Investigations, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency and, per The Verge, US Citizenship and Immigration Services. Yes, the cybersecurity agency is in the cybersecurity leak.

The group says it got in through Okta, a single sign-on service. Translation: a single sign-on service is a digital front desk that checks your credentials once so you do not have to prove who you are at every door. Okta told the BBC that it is secure and that it has "no evidence our data or systems were compromised." The sources do not explain how the data was obtained, which leaves a mystery of the sort that tends to be solved in a later press release.

The data was reportedly handed to researcher maia arson crimew, who passed it to Distributed Denial of Secrets, a nonprofit that now hosts it as 'Icepick.' The BBC reported that the files also include dozens of previously unknown operation names, the agents who led them, and technology used, including products from Cellebrite and Palantir. The Verge said the data also links groups to surveillance tools such as the facial recognition app Mobile Fortify and PenLink, which analyzes phone and online data. In other words, the leak contains both the people and the shopping list.

The motive is stated plainly. "They have our information, so it's only fair that we have theirs," the hacker told TechCrunch, according to The Verge. DDoSecrets co-founder Emma Best told the BBC the aim is to replace "anonymity with accountability," and said the release is not meant to be a directory of every agent. A Department of Peace spokesperson also cited a July 17 statement by Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin about 'turning up the heat.'

The context is an agency whose officers often work masked and rarely identify themselves, even though, the BBC noted, the law requires them to. Public backlash has grown amid a large increase in arrests and deportations. The BBC reported that ICE agents have shot at more than two dozen people this year and killed at least nine, and that the Migration Policy Institute counts nearly 1 million deportations since President Trump returned to office. The same agency that guarded its staff's identities is now watching them be published, which is the sort of transparency nobody asked the agency to volunteer for.

DHS said it is "aware of a potential breach" and working to address it, and promised to update the public. The BBC called this the third recent incident involving a government agency, after the FBI breach attributed to ShinyHunters and a suspected hacking attempt that took the Federal Election Commission's site offline. Watch for whether DHS confirms the breach vector, and whether Okta's position survives contact with that explanation.
