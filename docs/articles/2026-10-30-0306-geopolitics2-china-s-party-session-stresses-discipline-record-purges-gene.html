China's Communist Party Expels Record 33 Members, Reports Record Absences, Calls Meeting a Success

The fifth plenum drew 279 Central Committee members, the lowest turnout of Xi Jinping's tenure, as the party stressed discipline ahead of a 2027 leadership reshuffle.

The wire said: Record number of purges as Xi emphasises 'party discipline' at China political gathering · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 30, 2026 03:06 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

A plenum is a closed-door meeting of the Communist Party's Central Committee, held a few times each term to set direction. Xi Jinping has made anti-corruption a defining feature of his rule since 2012, and recent years have seen senior generals and officials removed.

Why it matters

The purges are hollowing out the military's top ranks and the party's leadership just as Xi is expected to seek a fourth term, which affects how China is governed and how predictable its decisions are. Analysts also say the focus on loyalty may discourage the initiative officials are supposedly being asked to show.

China's Communist Party concluded a four-day closed-door gathering in Beijing on Thursday by expelling a record 33 members, according to the BBC. Sixteen of them come from the military. The meeting also set a record of a different kind: 279 Central Committee members attended the fifth plenum, down from 315 at the fourth plenum in October 2025 and 364 at the third in July 2024. The party has found a way to make attendance and membership shrink at the same time, which is one way of achieving unity.

The communique said the members had committed "serious disciplinary and legal violations." Translation: the party has declined to say what they did, which the BBC notes has become routine under Xi's anti-corruption campaign. The phrase is widely read as a euphemism for corruption, though the specifics are left to the imagination, a faculty the party generally prefers to keep under supervision.

The numbers tell the story. The latest expulsions bring the total removed from the Central Committee during Xi's third term to 51, compared with 35 in his second term and five in his first, the BBC reported. The anti-corruption watchdog said 680,000 people across the party apparatus were disciplined in the first nine months of this year. The current committee began its term in 2022 with more than 370 members, so the room has been getting roomier by design.

Among those removed are former Xinjiang party chief Ma Xingrui and former generals Zhang Youxia and Liu Zhenli. Zhang was vice-chairman of the Central Military Commission, which controls the armed forces. That seven-member body is now down to two: Xi as chairman and Zhang Shengmin as vice-chairman. Analysts at the Asia Society Policy Institute said the military purge is "in a category of its own" and raises questions about combat readiness, even as China unveils new hardware. Hardware, it turns out, does not need to be promoted.

Zhang Shengmin was elevated to the Politburo at the plenum, bringing that body to 22 of its 24 seats, after earlier removals had left it at 21. NPR described the move as the elevation of a military general. In a system where the person who promotes you can also be the person who expels you, a promotion is best understood as a temporary condition.

Why the emphasis on discipline? The BBC and DW both point to the Party Congress due in 2027, where Xi is widely expected to take a fourth term. Chong Ja Ian of Carnegie China argues the campaign now serves "an effort to consolidate and maintain authority" and will expand depending on how secure Xi feels rather than on actual corruption. DW notes that term limits were removed in 2018, so Xi could stay in post until 2032. He is 73.

The party has also asked officials for two things that sit awkwardly together: more loyalty and more initiative. Kerry Brown of King's College London told DW this is a genuine dilemma. Give officials freedom to solve problems and party control may weaken, which could raise corruption. Tighten the screws and officials may stop taking initiative, because the party is, in Brown's words, "very, very risk averse." Chong offered a tidy resolution: Xi wants proactivity in implementation, not in decision-making. Officials are invited to show initiative in the same way a train is invited to choose its destination.

Brown says the approach "kind of works" for now, noting that China's economy is "not great, but it's not disastrous" and the outside world is "a mess." The unresolved matter is succession. Naming a successor could undermine Xi's authority; not naming one could create a power vacuum later. Chong says solving that requires confidence and a willingness to step back, and that not everyone has it.

What to watch: who fills the emptying seats. Thomas of the Asia Society suggests provincial leadership ranks are a promising place to look for the next cohort, which tends to be young, technocratic and connected to Xi's network. Those candidates may want to check the attendance sheet first.
