Defeated Brazilian Candidate Reportedly Leaves Country, Passport Having Been Cancelled, Which Is Not How Passports Work

Flávio Bolsonaro, who lost Sunday's election to Lula with 48.21% of the vote, is reportedly bound for Florida to seek asylum while a money-laundering probe continues at home.

The wire said: Flávio Bolsonaro flees Brazil after presidential election loss, reports say · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 28, 2026 02:42 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Senator Flávio Bolsonaro lost Brazil's presidential election on Sunday to incumbent Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva. A supreme court justice cancelled his passport in a criminal case, and he missed a deadline to surrender it to federal police.

Why it matters

The right's leadership in Brazil is now unsettled, with the dominant political family scattered across jails, house arrest and foreign exile. The episode also tests how Washington treats the political and legal problems of allies of the Trump administration.

Flávio Bolsonaro, the far-right senator who lost Brazil's presidential election on Sunday, has reportedly left the country and is headed to the United States, according to The Guardian, citing reports from the news site Metrópoles and the newspaper Folha de S Paulo. Allies told Metrópoles he would land in Florida on Tuesday afternoon and request political asylum. A US state department spokesperson said he and his family would be granted visas to enter the country, so at least one bureaucracy is moving quickly.

The sequence matters. Bolsonaro lost on Sunday, and a supreme court justice cancelled his passport minutes after the result, in a case where federal police are investigating possible involvement in a money-laundering scheme worth about £680m ($905m) involving betting companies. Translation: a court told him to hand over his travel document, which is the legal equivalent of asking someone to leave their car keys at the door. He had until 11am Tuesday to comply and did not appear.

On Monday he posted a video about the cancellation in which he said: "I never thought about leaving the country." The timeline of what happened next is, charitably, contested. Infobae reported he went to Argentina to board a private jet in Puerto Iguazú, but the plane did not take off. Todo Noticias reported he crossed the border on foot and dropped the plane plan after news leaked. CNN Brasil said he left in the early hours with his wife and two daughters. Brazilian federal police are asking Argentina whether any record exists of him entering, because there is none of him leaving Brazil through a regular checkpoint. The Guardian also notes that he has held Italian citizenship since 2023 and reportedly has an Italian passport, which makes a cancelled Brazilian one a modest inconvenience.

Lula, speaking at an event for his 81st birthday, was not inclined to treat this as a private family matter. "He who owes nothing, fears nothing," the president said, adding that leaving Brazil was shameful. He also accused the Bolsonaro family of loyalty to the US over their own country. Lawmakers from Lula's Workers' party asked the supreme court to order an arrest and to place Bolsonaro on Interpol's Red Notice list. Translation: a request that other countries' police please notice him.

His campaign chief, senator Rogério Marinho, said he was surprised, because the two had discussed just the day before that Bolsonaro, with 60 million votes, should lead the opposition. Asked whether that could be done from abroad, Marinho cited Venezuela's María Corina Machado. Leading an opposition from exile is a recognised model; it is usually adopted after the election, not instead of conceding it. Bolsonaro has yet to concede.

The family situation is, by The Guardian's account, thin on available Bolsonaros. Patriarch Jair Bolsonaro is under house arrest for attempting a coup. Eduardo Bolsonaro lives in self-imposed exile in the US and faces an arrest warrant after a four-year sentence for lobbying Donald Trump to impose sanctions on the justices hearing his father's case. Eduardo said last Friday that his brother should leave Brazil if he lost, which is an unusually candid piece of campaign advice.

Political scientists quoted by The Guardian see a vacuum. Magna Inácio said the right's leadership is now "up for grabs", while Camila Rocha expects power to drift away from the sons. Polls before the vote showed supporters of Jair preferred figures such as Michelle Bolsonaro, newly elected senator, and São Paulo governor Tarcísio de Freitas. Inácio expects friction between Michelle and her two stepsons in congress, who publicly do not get along with her.

Flávio reportedly never wanted to run, fearing a campaign would make his life hell, and was drafted only because Eduardo was abroad. He still won 48.21% of the vote, which analysts read as partly an anti-Lula vote and partly a vote for whomever Jair chose. Next to watch: whether he actually lands in Florida, whether the supreme court's arrest order and the Interpol request go anywhere, and whether the party of 60 million voters finds a leader who is physically in the country.
