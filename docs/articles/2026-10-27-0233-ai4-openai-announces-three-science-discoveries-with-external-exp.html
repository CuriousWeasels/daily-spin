OpenAI Announces Three Breakthroughs, Asks Humans to Please Check Them

The company released claimed results in math, computer science and physics, plus a new system of outside "shepherds" to verify them, one day after its own advisers recommended against such a system.

The wire said: OpenAI announces three big science discoveries and a new way to work with academics · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 27, 2026 02:33 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Less than a month after dumping hundreds of manuscripts it said solved long-standing math questions, OpenAI has published three more claimed discoveries. This time it says outside experts are guiding how the results are examined and released.

Why it matters

If the claims hold, they would reshape parts of mathematics, computing theory and physics; if they don't, the field will have spent months checking machine-written homework. Either way, the norms set now for who sees AI results first, and who gets credit, will shape how science handles them.

OpenAI announced on GitHub three results it calls major: a proof in mathematics, a refutation of a central hypothesis in computer science, and an existence proof in physics. According to The Verge, the release comes less than a month after the company's earlier mass drop of manuscripts. The difference this time is that outside academics are involved, which is a sensible thing to add to a claim that a machine has overturned decades of theory.

The math result concerns local smoothing for the Euclidean wave equation. OpenAI says its model proved the local smoothing conjecture in every dimension, which would also resolve the Kakeya conjecture and Stein's restriction conjecture in all dimensions. Translation: a family of problems about how waves and lines behave in space, which mathematicians have worked on for decades, is claimed to be closed in one go. The Verge notes that NYU's Hong Wang won a Fields Medal just months ago for settling Kakeya in three dimensions, so the timing is awkward for anyone who had planned a victory lap. For this one, OpenAI is releasing raw model output and, on advice, letting researchers work out their own explanations.

The computer science claim is that the Exponential Time Hypothesis is false. Translation: a widely believed assumption that certain hard problems, such as 3-SAT, cannot be solved much faster than by brute force would be wrong. According to OpenAI, the paper gives a deterministic algorithm that counts satisfying assignments of fixed-width CNF formulas in subexponential time and polynomial space. Much of the field's list of proven limits is built on this hypothesis, so a refutation would be less a discovery than a renovation.

Verification will be handled by Russell Impagliazzo, Ramamohan Paturi and Ryan Williams. Impagliazzo and Paturi were among those who proposed the hypothesis in 1999, which makes them either ideal reviewers or the two people with the most to lose. At their request, OpenAI published the write-up and a Lean proof on GitHub immediately. Translation: Lean is software that checks a proof step by step, so a machine-written argument can at least be audited by a machine that does not get tired or impressed.

The physics claim is the boldest and the least visible. OpenAI describes a mathematically rigorous existence proof of certain interacting quantum field theories in four dimensions, including Quantum Chromodynamics and a unified completion of the Standard Model. The full material has not been released. David Gross will lead a group with Michael Douglas, Arthur Jaffe, Igor Klebanov and Nikita Nekrasov to shepherd the work, which is a lot of shepherds for a flock the public cannot yet see.

The shepherd model has a wrinkle. The Verge reports that AGMAI, a panel of math advisers OpenAI itself assembled, said just yesterday that it recommends AI companies not adopt the practice of designating certain people to see output first. It cited unequal access, borrowed reputation and biased selection. The panel instead suggested posting results on scholarly repositories with Lean-formalized proofs, an explanation of how problems were chosen, and funding for the people who work through the results. OpenAI acknowledged its approach could have its own problems, and used it anyway.

OpenAI also published an open letter asking the community three questions: what rules should govern early access and conflicts of interest, how credit should be split between discovering a result and the work of checking, fixing and explaining it, and how to support students as research changes. These are good questions, and asking them in the same announcement as the results is a bold sequencing choice. For now, the claims stand as claims, and the next news will come from three professors with a very large pile of reading.
