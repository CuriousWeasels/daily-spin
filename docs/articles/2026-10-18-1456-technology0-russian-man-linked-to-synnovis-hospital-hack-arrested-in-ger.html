Alleged Qilin Hacker Booked a Holiday to Japan; Police Booked Him First

German detectives now include the 2024 Synnovis hospital attack in their case against a 28-year-old Russian arrested in Japan, as UK police weigh an unlawful killing inquiry.

The wire said: Russian man linked to deadly London hospitals hack arrested · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 18, 2026 14:56 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

In June 2024, the Qilin ransomware gang hacked Synnovis, a blood-testing provider serving London hospitals, disrupting care and, according to King's College Hospital, contributing to one patient's death. A Russian man named by German press as Vladimir K was arrested in Japan in May.

Why it matters

If prosecutors can link a cyberattack to a death, ransomware stops being treated as a financial nuisance and starts being treated as something closer to violence. That could change how seriously governments pursue these gangs, and how hospitals are expected to defend themselves.

German police have detained a 28-year-old Russian man, named by German press as Vladimir K, who is accused of being a core member of the Qilin cyber-crime gang. The BBC reported that German detectives are now folding the 2024 attack on London pathology provider Synnovis into their investigation. He was arrested by Japanese police in May on arrival for what was, by all accounts, meant to be a holiday.

The detail worth savoring is the itinerary. According to German officials, his holiday plans were intercepted, which suggests that the one part of his business he failed to encrypt was his travel booking. It is very rare for hackers to be arrested in Russia, because its government refuses to cooperate with Western law enforcement requests. Translation: the main security measure for this profession is staying home, and he left.

He was originally wanted in Germany over a different matter: a September 2024 hack of a logistics company, in which data was encrypted and about $160,000 in cryptocurrency was demanded. Translation of the method: ransomware means locking a victim's files and selling back the key, like a locksmith who also breaks the door. His extradition was first reported in early October, and the BBC says the Synnovis attack is now part of the case.

Synnovis is a pathology partnership between the hospitals and German company Synlab. After Qilin struck on 3 June 2024, a critical incident was declared at some London hospitals. Blood transfusion services were severely disrupted, operations were cancelled and emergency patients were diverted elsewhere. In total, more than 10,000 appointments were disrupted over weeks.

King's College Hospital NHS Foundation Trust confirmed a year later that one patient had "died unexpectedly". A trust spokesperson said several factors contributed, including "a long wait for a blood test result". That makes it, per the BBC, the first cyber attack in history directly linked to a death. It is a modest milestone, in the sense that nobody wanted it.

The hackers demanded a ransom, and when none was paid, they published the highly private data online. They told the BBC at the time that the attack was not only about money but also a protest over the UK's lack of help in Russia's war on Ukraine. The hackers did not say whether they were from Russia or Ukraine, and the claims were dismissed as an attempt to distance themselves from Qilin's known extortion tactics. Extortion with a political statement attached remains extortion.

Qilin has allegedly hacked more than 4,000 organisations since 2024 and extorted more than $140m (£105m). It has been tracked since 2022, and it was behind the October 2025 attack on Japanese brewer Asahi. Japan, one gathers, has now had two unpleasant encounters with the group, one of which ended with a handcuffed tourist.

The legal question is harder than the arrest. The BBC has learned that British police are examining whether its own inquiry may lead to an unlawful killing case, potentially a world first in cyber-crime. A similar 2020 attack on a Düsseldorf hospital led to a negligent homicide investigation after an elderly patient died, but it was dropped. Medical experts concluded the patient would have died regardless, and prosecutors could not prove the attack was the direct cause.

That precedent shows the difficulty: a courtroom wants a clean chain of causation, while a hospital in crisis offers a long wait, a delayed result and many other variables. Next to watch is whether the German case produces charges that mention Synnovis, and whether British prosecutors are willing to try something no one has managed yet.
