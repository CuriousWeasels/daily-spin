Treasury Yields Dip Below 5% as Investors Flee AI Debt for Safety of Government Debt

The 10-year yield ended the week at 4.99% as tech borrowing worries sent money into bonds, even with oil near $100 and the Fed leaning toward a hike.

The wire said: 10-year Treasury yield drops back to 5% this week as investors seek cover from AI worries · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 23, 2026 15:58 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Weeks after 10- and 30-year Treasury yields hit their highest levels in more than 20 years, they have eased as tech stocks sold off over the huge debt that AI companies are taking on to build data centers. The Fed meets next week with rates at 3.75% to 4.00%.

Why it matters

The 10-year yield is used to price mortgages, auto loans and credit card debt, so its direction shapes what ordinary borrowers pay. Markets still expect a Fed rate hike in December, so relief from falling yields may be short-lived.

The yield on the 10-year U.S. Treasury note finished the week at 4.99%, up 3 basis points on Friday but still slightly under 5% after dropping below that level Thursday for the first time in a month, CNBC reported. Investors had been selling tech stocks and buying government bonds. In other words, the safest asset available is a loan to a government running large deficits, which tells you something about the alternatives.

Translation: a basis point is 0.01%, and bond yields move opposite to prices. When people rush to buy Treasurys, prices rise and yields fall. Yields are falling this week because nervous investors want somewhere to put money, not because anyone has become more optimistic.

The nervousness has a specific source: the hundreds of billions of dollars tech companies are spending on AI networks, much of it borrowed. CNBC reported that tech stocks sank, yields on outstanding tech bonds rose, and credit default swap spreads widened for a number of AI companies. Translation: a credit default swap is insurance against a borrower failing to repay, and a wider spread means the insurance is getting more expensive.

CoreWeave is the week's showcase. The stock fell 10% on Thursday and 38% in October. Its 9.75% bond due in 2031 now yields 15.72%, and the spread on its five-year credit default swaps is 1,138, above the 1,000 level generally considered distressed. The company is, by the market's own pricing, being treated as a borrower who needs to be asked about collateral.

Bond investors also had other things to ignore. BMO's credit strategy team noted that Treasurys rallied even as WTI crude tested $100 a barrel, which would normally push yields up through inflation worries. WTI futures rose nearly 3% to $102.39 on Friday and Brent gained 2.5% to $115.94, after fresh attacks by Iran-backed Houthi militants on Saudi oil installations. Reports this week suggested the U.S. and Israel could resume strikes on Iran after the Nov. 3 midterm elections. Weakness in French government bonds added to the appetite for U.S. debt.

The economic data was not recessionary. The S&P Global manufacturing PMI came in at 56 against an expected 55.3, the highest in 53 months. Translation: a PMI above 50 means factories are expanding. Meanwhile, the University of Michigan consumer sentiment index was 48, down 0.1 point from September, beating the 46.3 consensus but still down 10.4% from a year ago. One-year inflation expectations rose to 4.7% and five-year expectations to 3.5%.

That mix explains the Fed's position. It is not expected to change rates next week, but CME's FedWatch tool put the probability of a quarter-point hike in December at 70%. Byron Anderson of Laffer Tengler Investments said a sustained bond rally is hard to see unless the narrative flips to recession, and noted that we are far from that, with two hikes still priced in. BMO said a sustained deterioration in financial conditions could alter the Fed's calculus.

Official data point to the same tension. The latest figures showed the 10-year at 5.13% on Oct. 21 and inflation at 3.74% in September. The week's dip below 5% is therefore a pause in a high-rate environment, not a return to a low-rate one. Watch the Fed meeting next week, and whether the AI credit worries spread beyond CoreWeave.
