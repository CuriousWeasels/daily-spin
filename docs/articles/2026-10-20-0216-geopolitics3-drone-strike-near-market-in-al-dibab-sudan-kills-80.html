Drone Strike on Sudanese Market Town Kills 80 as Warring Sides Decline Dialogue

The attack in al-Dibab is among the deadliest single strikes of a war now defined by drones, and the UN says no breakthrough is in sight.

The wire said: Drone attack near market kills 80 in Sudan · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 20, 2026 02:16 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Sudan's army and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces have been at war since April 2023, after a power struggle between their leaders erupted in Khartoum. The conflict has killed hundreds of thousands of people and displaced many more.

Why it matters

Drones are now responsible for most civilian deaths in Sudan, and with outside powers reportedly supplying both sides, the incentive to negotiate remains thin. Civilians gathering at markets and transport hubs bear the cost.

A drone strike near a market in al-Dibab, in West Kordofan state in southern Sudan, killed 80 people on Sunday evening, the Guardian reported. Most of the dead were commuters waiting for rides home after market day. It is one of the deadliest single strikes since the war began in April 2023.

Ahmed Wadi, a witness who spoke over a satellite internet connection because of a communications blackout, said the drone struck twice, ten minutes apart, at a transport hub where people gather to travel back to their villages. He said he helped bury 50 bodies, one of them his brother Mohamed. A further 47 people were taken to the nearest town, al-Mujlad, and a medical source who requested anonymity for his safety said 30 of them died of their injuries. The medic also said a separate drone strike on al-Mujlad injured four people, who are being treated.

Responsibility is, as usual, a matter of assertion. The RSF-declared administration, known as Tasis, accused the army of carrying out the strike. All of West Kordofan is under RSF control. The Guardian's report does not include a response from the army on this attack.

The wider pattern is plain in the numbers. Between January and April, drone strikes accounted for at least 880 civilian deaths, more than 80% of all conflict-related civilian deaths in Sudan. Translation: the weapon that is cheap, remote and requires no one to be present at the scene has become the main way civilians are being killed.

The same week brought a similar dispute. After drone strikes on a busy livestock market in Nyala, the capital of South Darfur, on Friday, the army said it had killed RSF fighters. The RSF said the military had killed 30 civilians. Two accounts of one market, with only one of them involving a market.

The diplomatic picture offers little relief. Rosemary DiCarlo, the UN undersecretary-general for political and peacebuilding affairs, told the Security Council last week that the crisis was deteriorating "with no breakthrough on the horizon." She said the warring parties are not moving toward dialogue and appear to believe that continued external support will let them pursue their goals by force.

That last point is the mechanism. The war has been fuelled by foreign powers with vested interests who back each side. As long as each commander believes the next shipment will tip the balance, talks look like a concession rather than an exit. The people at the transport hub in al-Dibab were not part of that calculation, which is rather the problem.

What to watch next: whether the UN Security Council moves beyond warnings, and whether any outside backer decides that the supply line is more trouble than the territory.
