Diesel Shortage Survives Two Wars and a Presidential Deal, Which Was Mostly a Press Release

Ukrainian drone strikes have cut Russian diesel exports by more than 75%, leaving a US-brokered export boost looking optimistic as pump prices climb to $6.32 a gallon.

The wire said: How Ukraine’s drone strikes on Russia crippled the world’s second largest diesel supplier · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 21, 2026 16:10 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Ukraine has stepped up drone attacks on Russian refineries and ports, while the US-Iran war has disrupted Middle East fuel flows through the Strait of Hormuz. President Trump had brokered a deal with Russia to ship more diesel to world markets.

Why it matters

Diesel moves freight, farm equipment and airline schedules, so higher prices feed into the cost of nearly everything, including UK inflation and interest rate decisions. The International Energy Agency has warned that supplies will stay tight well into 2027.

CNBC reported that Ukraine launched more than 1,000 drones at Russia on Sunday alone, according to state media, setting an oil export terminal in St. Petersburg ablaze. Kyiv has also abandoned its brief observance of an energy ceasefire that President Trump declared on Oct. 11. Moscow, according to CNBC, ignored that truce from the start, which made it a ceasefire in the way a one-person handshake is a agreement.

The target is diesel, and Russia was the world's second-largest supplier after the US. Kpler data cited by CNBC show Russian diesel exports fell by more than 75% to 141,000 barrels per day in September, from 608,000 a year earlier. Rochan Consulting counted 31 Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries and related infrastructure in July, nearly triple the 11 in January.

Moscow banned diesel exports in July to protect domestic supplies, then resumed shipments this month to honor the Trump-Putin deal. Under it, Russia agreed to supply about 75,000 barrels per day in October and 124,000 in November. Columbia University's Tatiana Mitrova called those volumes a rounding error against total demand. Translation: the plan is to refill a swimming pool with a garden hose, while someone else keeps puncturing the pool.

The damage is also getting harder to undo. Mitrova said a destroyed fuel tank takes a couple of weeks to fix, but a secondary refining unit needs foreign equipment and parties willing to violate sanctions. "You cannot just order it on Alibaba," she said. Translation: the repair is slow because the people who sell the parts are legally not supposed to be selling them.

Kpler's Matt Smith said the deal is likely dead in the water without an energy ceasefire. The price of that outcome is visible at the pump: AAA put the national diesel average at $6.32 per gallon on Tuesday, nearly 75% above last year. With midterm elections less than two weeks away, CNBC reported that Trump has blamed Ukraine for the price surge, a theory that is at least easier to put on a rally sign than a second war.

The Ukrainian strikes are not the only cause. CNBC noted that the diesel market was already strained after Trump's decision to go to war with Iran in late February, and Kpler estimates diesel exports through Hormuz are down about 540,000 barrels per day. Global diesel exports are down about 1 million barrels per day from lost Russian and Middle Eastern supply. US refiners are already running at maximum capacity, which limits the White House to hoping for peace in two wars at once.

The effects are spreading. Investing.com reported that United Airlines expects elevated fuel costs through 2027 and sees margin challenges. In Britain, the Office for National Statistics said motor fuel prices rose 29.8% in the year to September, pushing inflation to 3.5% against a forecast of 3.4%. Markets, per LSEG data, price a 98% chance that the Bank of England raises rates by 25 basis points to 4% next month. Translation: a quarter of a percentage point more on borrowing costs, as a way of telling an oil shortage to calm down.

Chancellor John Healey presents his first Autumn Budget next week with national debt above £3 trillion for the first time, and he has promised both fiscal discipline and breathing space for families. Economists at the Institute of Chartered Accountants called it a perilous high-wire act. Meanwhile the S&P 500 ETF, SPY, closed at 772.45 as of Oct. 21, suggesting that equity investors are either unbothered or have not filled up a truck recently.

The International Energy Agency says diesel tightness will likely last into 2027. That is a longer timeline than any ceasefire announced so far, including the one that lasted until someone read it.
