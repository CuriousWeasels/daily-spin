Musk Reportedly Capped Ukraine's Starlink Speeds, Then Credited Trump With the Decision

The Wall Street Journal says Elon Musk personally ordered a 160 kph limit that hobbled Ukrainian drones, though both he and the president have denied it.

The wire said: Report: Musk ordered Starlink slowdown in Ukraine, then blamed it on Trump · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 22, 2026 16:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Ukraine relies on Starlink and its military cousin Starshield to connect drones and other equipment on the front line. Last week a speed cap on the military service abruptly cut connections for fast-moving hardware.

Why it matters

A single private executive appears able to degrade a wartime ally's military capabilities with a settings change, and no one in government seems sure who is supposed to be in charge of that. For anyone relying on privately owned infrastructure, that is a useful thing to learn.

The Wall Street Journal reported that SpaceX CEO Elon Musk ordered the throttling of Starlink service that caught Ukraine's air assault forces off guard last week. This contradicts Musk's own claim that decisions about the military version of Starlink are made by the US military and President Trump. Trump has denied ordering the restrictions, which leaves the decision with the one party who claimed it belonged to somebody else.

The Journal, citing unnamed US officials and people familiar with the matter, wrote that the move "sparked panic in Kyiv and confusion inside the Trump administration." Ars Technica noted that one source said Musk spoke with Trump and White House officials beforehand, but that Musk made the final call. Translation: he may have asked for opinions, but the button was his.

The mechanism is simple and a little unsettling. According to the Associated Press, the cap cuts the data connection whenever a terminal moves faster than 160 kph (100 mph), which is common for Ukrainian drones equipped with Starshield. In this story, "speed" does not mean internet speed. It means how fast the drone is flying, so the limit effectively tells a drone it may only fight at a pace the enemy finds convenient.

The AP said the restriction forced drones to fly slower than usual, making them easier for Russian weapons to target or evade. The Journal went further, reporting that some offensive and interceptor drones were rendered virtually useless. An interceptor drone that must slow down to stay online is a bit like a smoke detector that works only when the house is not on fire.

The timing is the awkward part. The Journal reported that the decision came after both Musk and Trump criticized Ukraine for attacking Russian diesel refineries. Nobody has to prove a causal link for readers to notice the sequence: public complaint first, throttling second, denials third.

There is also a naming problem. Ars Technica notes that officials often use Starlink and Starshield interchangeably, and Ukraine has used both the commercial service and the military variant. Ukrainian officials have been clear, however, that their objection concerns new restrictions on Starshield. The brand confusion helps with deniability, which is a feature no one listed in the product description.

What to watch next is whether the White House or the Pentagon clarifies who actually holds authority over a military service that runs on a private company's satellites. Until then, the chain of command appears to include a satellite operator with a settings menu.
