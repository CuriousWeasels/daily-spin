White House Removes Some Canada Tariffs, Adds Others, Net Effect on Maine Unclear

A Section 338 proclamation swaps exempted goods for valves, carpets and waste products, taking effect four days before the Nov. 3 election.

The wire said: Trump dropping some Canada tariffs but 'replacing' them with other products · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 25, 2026 15:06 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

President Trump put 50% tariffs on a list of Canadian goods over the summer using a 96-year-old trade law known as Section 338. Maine, which imports more from Canada than from any other country, is hosting a tight Senate race in which those tariffs are a top issue.

Why it matters

Tariffs are taxes paid largely by importers, so which products are on the list determines which businesses and shoppers absorb higher costs. A swap of products is not the same as a cut, and the details matter more than the announcement.

The White House said on Friday that it would remove some Canadian products from its list of tariffed goods and replace them with other Canadian products, CNBC reported. President Trump signed a proclamation removing certain items from the 50% tariff list and adding what a White House fact sheet called "certain valves, carpets, waste products, and others." The changes take effect Oct. 30, four days before the Nov. 3 election. Translation: the tariff bill is not shrinking, it is being rearranged, like a budget that balances because the expenses moved to another column.

The news surfaced because Sen. Susan Collins, R-Maine, announced she had secured exemptions on nine categories of products she called crucial for Maine's agricultural industries. According to Collins, the list includes corrugated paper, plastic boxes and packing bags, refrigeration equipment, several forms of machinery, and seaweed and algae products. The Wall Street Journal first reported her announcement, CNBC said. Her press release did not mention the new tariffs, which the White House announced less than an hour later.

The timing matters because Collins is fighting for reelection and voters in Maine have already started casting ballots. Tariffs are unpopular in the state, which imports far more from Canada than from any other country. Collins has pushed this line before: at an Oct. 6 debate she took credit for removing items including road salt. Her Democratic opponent, Troy Jackson, a fifth-generation Maine logger, answered, "What about everything else? We can't eat road salt."

Jackson was blunter on Friday. He said Collins enabled Trump's trade policies and voted to confirm the people behind them, and that she now wants credit for "bringing a cup of water to put out the fire." Collins' spokeswoman, Phoebe Keller, told CNBC the senator "requested exemptions for products that are important to Maine and will continue to do so." She did not say what, if anything, Collins had agreed to in return.

The White House framed the proclamation differently. Its fact sheet said the changes "further refine the scope" of the tariffs and did not mention Maine. Spokesman Matthew Middleton said the action responds to Canada's "continued discrimination against and additional retaliation targeting U.S. commerce." Translation: reciprocity, in trade policy, means one side does something and the other side is told it started it.

One footnote from CNBC is worth keeping. Collins' release called Canada America's largest trading partner, but according to the U.S. Census Bureau, Mexico holds that title. The correction does not change the tariff math, though it does suggest that even the framing of this story is being renegotiated.

The sources do not say how much trade is covered by the removed goods versus the added ones, so the net effect on prices is unknown. Against a backdrop of 3.74% year-over-year inflation as of September, any new tax on valves and carpets is not a rounding error for the businesses that buy them. Watch what happens on Oct. 30, and then on Nov. 3, when voters will deliver their own review of the refinement.
