Oil Dips 20 Cents as Saudis Visit Iran, Remains $116 Anyway

Brent crude slipped slightly on Monday as Saudi Arabia's defense minister held talks in Tehran, but a roughly $20 risk premium and drained inventories keep prices near records for the year.

The wire said: Oil ticks lower as Saudi defense minister meets with Iran's president in Tehran to de-escalate · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 26, 2026 17:43 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Eight months of Middle East conflict have drained global oil inventories, and Iran-allied Houthi militants in Yemen have recently attacked Saudi Arabia repeatedly. On Monday, Saudi Arabia's defense minister traveled to Tehran to try to cool things down.

Why it matters

Oil near $116 a barrel feeds into fuel, shipping and food costs, and with U.S. inflation at 3.74% there is little room for another supply shock. Any strike on Saudi infrastructure could push prices higher still, though CNBC's source notes prices above $120 would start destroying demand.

Oil prices ticked lower on Monday after Saudi Arabia's defense minister, Prince Khalid bin Salman, met Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian in Tehran, CNBC reported. Brent crude futures fell 20 cents to $116.52 a barrel, after briefly topping $117 earlier in the session. U.S. West Texas Intermediate dropped 79 cents to $102.48. In fairness, a 20 cent decline on a $116 barrel is the market's way of nodding politely.

The meeting also included Supreme National Security Council Secretary Mohsen Rezaei, plus Pakistan's Army Chief of Staff Asim Munir and Turkish intelligence head Ibrahim Kalin. Pakistan and Turkey belong to a defense pact with Saudi Arabia. Translation: when two rivals talk, it helps to bring friends who are formally obliged to care how it turns out.

The diplomacy has a backdrop. The Houthis, who are allied with Iran, have repeatedly attacked Saudi Arabia in recent weeks. On Monday they said they fired missiles at Riyadh's international airport and launched drones at oil pumping stations. The Saudis have not confirmed that any of this happened, which leaves traders to price a rumor at $116 a barrel.

That pricing is the point. Michael Alfaro, chief investment officer at Gallo Partners, told CNBC that the risk premium baked into oil is about $20 a barrel. Translation: risk premium is the extra amount buyers pay today because something worse might happen tomorrow. By that arithmetic, roughly a sixth of every barrel's price is anxiety.

The anxiety is not irrational. After eight months of conflict, global inventories have fallen sharply, and the U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve, the government's emergency stockpile, sits at its lowest level since 1982. On Friday the Energy Department authorized releasing up to 400,000 barrels from it to address a supply disruption at a Louisiana refinery. That is a small withdrawal from a shrinking account, though it does reflect the system working as designed.

Alfaro said a substantial hit to Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline or more refining infrastructure could send prices sharply higher. He also said prices above $120 would destroy demand. Translation: eventually oil gets so expensive that people stop buying it, which is the market's only built-in brake and a rather painful one. He added that refining capacity is the real challenge, with problems potentially extending into 2027 and beyond.

Help from American producers looks uncertain. Alfaro said drillers are adding some rigs, but are not committing to major capital spending, or capex, amid volatile prices and geopolitical risk. Translation: oil at $102 a barrel is not enough to persuade a company to spend big, because it fears the price may not last. Meanwhile, Saudi Arabia and Singapore said the kingdom will begin storing crude in Singapore from the second half of 2027, which is a long-range plan for a problem that is arriving this quarter.

Investing.com reported that UK stocks climbed as oil slipped on de-escalation hopes. Hope, it turns out, is a tradable asset, though it currently trades about 20 cents below fear. Watch whether the Houthi claims are confirmed, and whether the talks in Tehran produce anything beyond a group photo.
