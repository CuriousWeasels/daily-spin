Congressman Proposes That Buying a Video Game Should Involve, Technically, Buying It

Rep. Ritchie Torres's Power to the Players Act would direct the FTC to require game ownership and offline modes before servers shut down, as NBA 2K25 prepares to expire.

The wire said: NY announces game ownership bill requiring ownership rather than licensing · Hacker News

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 18, 2026 14:56 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Most digital games are sold as licenses, meaning publishers can revoke access when they shut down servers or delist titles. A consumer backlash, including the Stop Killing Games movement after Ubisoft's The Crew shutdown in 2024, has pushed the issue into legislatures.

Why it matters

If the bill or similar state measures advance, a purchased game could no longer disappear when a publisher stops paying for servers. The details, including what ownership means, will decide whether this changes anything.

Rep. Ritchie Torres, a New York Democrat, has introduced the Power to the Players Act, which would direct the Federal Trade Commission to write rules on game ownership. According to Ars Technica, the bill's stated aim is that buying a video game would give the consumer ownership of the game rather than a license to play it. Games with online functionality would also have to offer an offline mode before their servers are shut down, Tom's Hardware reported. That this needs a federal bill is the most honest summary of the current market.

Translation: a license is a permission slip, not a possession. Clicking Buy feels like a purchase, but the legal fine print says you have rented access that can be withdrawn. Tom's Hardware reports that Sony's lawyers argued in an ongoing class action that no consumer would assume the Buy button conveys a durable right to use a game rather than a revocable license. It is a defense that works only if no one has ever read the word Buy.

The bill's example is NBA 2K25. Ars Technica notes the game launched on September 6, 2025 and is scheduled to become unplayable on December 31 because of server shutdowns. Tom's Hardware adds that it is being pulled from digital storefronts, so existing owners lose a main feature and new customers cannot purchase it at all. That is roughly a four-month shelf life for something priced like a lasting good.

Torres put it this way in his statement: a game that can be taken away when a company pulls the plug is not a purchase but a rental disguised as a sale. He told Kotaku he wants a simple rule, that if you pay for it, you own it, and called it a restoration to the historical norm, pointing to how games worked in the 1990s. Back then, the game was a disc, and the disc did not need the publisher's continued enthusiasm.

The bill is not alone. Tom's Hardware says Pennsylvania state senator John Kane introduced the Protect Our Games Act, which would require publishers to provide an offline mode, community servers, or a 25 percent minimum refund. Note the refund option: a quarter of the price back for a game that has stopped working. Presumably the other 75 percent covers the memories.

Then there is The Crew. Ubisoft shut down the online-only racing game in 2024, rendering it unplayable even for recent buyers, because even the single-player campaign needed Ubisoft's servers. That prompted the Stop Killing Games movement, which wants offline modes, peer-to-peer patching and community server hosting. Tom's Hardware notes the bill might satisfy those demands, but concrete definitions have not been released.

That caveat matters. Tom's Hardware says it is unclear whether the right to transfer, which Torres mentions, would apply to digital purchases, and that the bill must define precisely what ownership means. Commenters on the article were divided: some argued offline versions would be watered down or that live-service games would turn free-to-play, while others replied that games once shipped with private server software as a matter of course. Both sides are, helpfully, describing hypothetical industries.

The fight also touches physical media. Ars Technica reports Sony says digital downloads made up 82 percent of full-game unit sales on PlayStation 4 and 5 in its latest fiscal year, and some consumers are protesting its plan to discontinue physical PlayStation releases. Less than 20 percent of buyers still choose discs, but preservationists say that minority is what remains when a game is delisted. Next to watch: whether the bill gets a definition of ownership, or just a very firm opinion about it.
