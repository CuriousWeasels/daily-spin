Inflation Hits 3.7% as Gasoline Does Most of the Work and Everyone Feigns Surprise

September consumer prices rose 0.6% on a 9.1% jump in gasoline, strengthening bets that the Fed will raise rates again after its first hike in over three years.

The wire said: Consumer prices rose 3.7% annually in September as gas prices skyrocket · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 15, 2026 14:38 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Consumer prices have risen faster than the Fed's 2% goal since March 2021, and a U.S.-Iran war launched in late February sent energy costs sharply higher. The Fed raised rates in September for the first time in more than three years.

Why it matters

Higher inflation means paychecks buy less, and the likely Fed response, more rate hikes, makes borrowing costlier. With midterms next month, the price of a gallon of gas is also a political variable.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Wednesday that the consumer price index rose a seasonally adjusted 0.6% in September, putting the annual rate at 3.7%, up from 3.4% in August, according to CNBC. Economists surveyed by Dow Jones had expected 0.6% and 3.6%, so the economy missed the forecast by one-tenth of a percentage point, which in this business counts as a plot twist.

The main culprit was energy. Gasoline rose 9.1% in a single month and accounted for about half of the headline gain, per the BLS. Over the past year gas is up 34.2% and fuel oil 74.2%. Translation: the thing you put in the car and the thing some people put in the furnace now cost roughly a third and nearly three-quarters more, respectively.

Strip out food and energy and you get core CPI, a measure designed to show underlying inflation by ignoring the items people actually buy most often. It rose 0.3% for the month and 2.6% annually, slightly above forecasts. Food was flat, shelter rose 0.4%, used vehicles rose 0.7%, and apparel, which is sensitive to tariffs, rose 0.3% and is up 3.4% on the year.

Markets reacted as markets do. Stock futures turned negative and bond yields rose sharply, per CNBC. Traders put the odds of a rate hike at the Fed's Oct. 27-28 meeting at about 54%, and the odds of one by year-end above 96%. Fed officials have lately suggested no need to rush, but most expect another increase this year.

The Fed's dilemma is a classic one. Energy shocks are usually treated as temporary, and central banks tend to look through them, which is a polite way of saying they squint and wait. But the longer the war lasts, the more likely higher prices seep into everything else and into what people expect to happen next. CNBC reported that Brent crude rose more than 14% in September and traded around $105 a barrel the day before the release.

Expectations are the real worry. CNBC noted that CPI has now run above 2% for about 65 consecutive months, the longest such streak of this century. Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack warned that consumers are starting to accept high inflation as a fact of life, which she called concerning. If workers demand bigger raises and businesses raise prices ahead of costs, inflation can feed itself, and the Fed's tools work less well.

For households, the arithmetic is unkind. Inflation has recently outrun wage growth, so the average consumer has lost purchasing power, according to CNBC. The University of Michigan's survey reported record shares of people blaming high prices for worse finances. Economists quoted do not expect prices to fall back, since falling prices would signal something worse. The realistic hope is for wages to outpace inflation long enough to catch up.

The political calendar adds flavor. This was the last CPI report before the November midterms, and sentiment has fallen more among Republicans and independents than Democrats this year, per Michigan data. The Fed insists it does not consider elections. Voters, for their part, are considering very little else besides groceries and gas.

Next up is Thursday's producer price index, which forecasters expect to show wholesale prices up 5.5% from a year earlier. That is the figure retailers will eventually describe to you as someone else's decision.
