Pennsylvania Orders AI Ads to Confess, Defines 'Confess' Loosely

Governor Josh Shapiro signed a law requiring labels on AI-generated advertising, though key terms like 'significantly modified' and 'evidently fictional' remain open to interpretation.

The wire said: Pennsylvania will require labels on AI-generated ads · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 25, 2026 15:06 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Pennsylvania has enacted the Artificial Intelligence Deceptive Advertising Act, one of the first state laws requiring disclosure of AI-generated content. The Verge reported Governor Josh Shapiro signed it on Thursday.

Why it matters

If you see a label on an ad, you may now be told a machine made it, but vague wording means plenty of AI-assisted ads may never carry one. Advertisers nationwide may also end up labeling commercials that air in Pennsylvania.

Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro on Thursday signed the Artificial Intelligence Deceptive Advertising Act, which requires disclosures on advertising that has been, in the law's words, "significantly modified or generated by artificial intelligence," The Verge reported. The label must appear in the same format as the ad itself, so a video gets a video disclosure, an audio spot gets an audio one, and a text ad gets text. It is a rare case of legislation insisting that honesty come in the same font.

The law covers a wide range of advertisers. According to The Verge, that includes TV commercials from multi-billion-dollar companies and also restaurants' crowded flyers about their wing specials. Translation: the same rule now binds a global consumer brand and the person who made a flyer in an evening and is proud of it.

The rule also applies regardless of where an ad was created. The Verge noted that viewers in other states may therefore see disclosures in commercials that are also expected to air in Pennsylvania. This is how one state ends up setting national practice: it is cheaper for a company to label everything than to produce a separate version for each place that has opinions.

Then come the loopholes, which The Verge described as numerous. What counts as "significantly modified" is subjective, so an ad touched up by AI may or may not qualify depending on who is doing the counting. There is also a carve-out for "parody, satire, commentary or criticism," as well as anything "evidently fictional." Translation: if it is obviously fake, no label is needed, and the law does not say what obviously means.

The wording also suggests that a disclosure is required only for content that uses synthetic material in a way that creates a "false consumer perception" about a product or service. That leaves open what counts as false and who decides. Presumably this will be settled the traditional way, by lawyers billing in six-minute increments.

The office of State Senator Nick Pisciottano, who introduced the bill, did not immediately return The Verge's request for comment. Whether that reflects a busy schedule or a desire to see how the definitions develop was not stated.

What to watch next is enforcement. A disclosure law is only as strong as its definitions, and for now Pennsylvania has required the label while leaving the question of when it applies largely to future arguments.
