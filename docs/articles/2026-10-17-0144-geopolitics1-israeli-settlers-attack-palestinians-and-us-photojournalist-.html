Olive Harvest Now Timed Like a Heist, Beating Sun and Settlers

Masked settlers attacked Palestinian farmers and a Washington Post photojournalist in the West Bank, as the UN reports more attacks in two weeks than all of last year's harvest.

The wire said: Israeli settlers beat Palestinians and a U.S. photojournalist in West Bank attacks · NPR World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 17, 2026 01:44 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Every autumn Palestinian families harvest olives in the occupied West Bank, and every autumn settler violence around the harvest draws attention. This year the UN says the pace of attacks is already ahead of last year's entire season.

Why it matters

The harvest is a major source of livelihood for Palestinian families, and the attacks show how settler violence, outpost growth and uneven enforcement are reshaping who can safely reach their own land.

Dozens of masked Israeli settlers attacked Palestinians during the olive harvest in the occupied West Bank on Friday, setting fire to cars and olive trees, according to the BBC and NPR. A local official told the BBC that 12 Palestinians were injured, including four dual Palestinian American citizens. Among the injured was Heidi Levine, a photojournalist working for The Washington Post. The harvest, in other words, now includes a step that agricultural manuals do not cover.

A BBC team saw one of the attacks on farmland near the village of Abu Falah. Cars carrying wounded farmers sped out along a stony track, loaded with picking equipment, as smoke rose behind them. Majdi Fheed, an American citizen, said attackers hit him on the head and neck. A council member from nearby Turmus Aya said he was struck with nail-studded sticks and that his elderly mother was also hurt.

Levine said dozens of settlers ambushed her while she was reporting. "I got jumped," she said, adding that she was kicked on the ground, made to stand, and kicked again for at least 10 minutes. She credited her helmet and flak jacket with protecting her. Washington Post executive editor Matt Murray said she was clearly identifiable as a journalist and called for a thorough investigation.

The official response was, by the standards of the genre, prompt and cautious. The Israeli prime minister's office said the facts remained unclear and were under investigation, which the BBC noted came despite video evidence and testimony. The Israel Defense Forces said troops were sent after a report of a violent confrontation and that all circumstances would be examined. Translation: a review has been scheduled, and reviews are famously patient.

The US State Department said it condemned criminal violence by any party in the West Bank and called for investigations, arrests and convictions. Note the phrase "any party." It is a sentence engineered to be agreed with by everyone, including the people in the black clothing.

The BBC described how farmers now treat picking as a race. Trees are stripped as soon as the sun rises, before settlers arrive. Three villages pooled workers on one plot, since landowner Amin Khalil said lone pickers at the edge of a village get attacked. His land, he said, is ringed by seven settlements and outposts. Safety in numbers worked until dozens of settlers came down the hill.

Some background on the terrain. All Israeli settlements are illegal under international law, which Israel disputes. Outposts, the small hilltop encampments, are also illegal under Israeli law, yet the BBC says many still receive state support. So the law is clear and the enforcement is a matter of interpretation.

As Israeli forces arrived, the standoff escalated. The BBC heard live ammunition and could not tell whether settlers or soldiers were firing. Troops then fired tear gas into the Palestinian fields, which the BBC called a well-worn pattern for managing these clashes. The UN says harvest attacks this year have killed one Palestinian and injured more than 120 across the West Bank.

Seventy-three-year-old Imm Khaldoun told the BBC she would always return to her land. That is the part of the story that the investigations will not have to establish. What to watch next is whether any of the promised examinations produce an arrest, since the State Department has now put that word in writing.
