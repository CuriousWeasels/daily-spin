Chinese Hackers Use AI Agents to Burgle 2,250 Organizations Via Their Backup Provider

Microsoft says state-aligned attackers stole ConnectWise cloud backup credentials, then used an open source AI framework to log in at nearly 3,000 attempts a minute.

The wire said: Chinese hackers spied on thousands of companies after breaching IT giant ConnectWise · TechCrunch AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 24, 2026 02:07 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

ConnectWise, which makes IT management and remote access software, suffered a breach of its cloud backup service. Microsoft researchers say China state-aligned hackers stole credentials there and used them to get into customer environments.

Why it matters

Companies that outsource their backups also outsource part of their security, and a single stolen credential at a vendor can expose thousands of customers. Attackers using AI agents also shrink the time defenders have to notice and respond.

Chinese state-aligned hackers broke into ConnectWise's cloud backup service and spied on thousands of its customers, according to Microsoft security researchers cited by TechCrunch. The company makes software that IT departments use to manage and remotely access other companies' computers. It is, in other words, a skeleton key that many firms voluntarily keep in a shared drawer.

The mechanism is simple. Microsoft says the hackers stole application credentials, which are the digital keys that set up data links between ConnectWise and the corporate customers who use it to back up their data. Translation: application credentials are the passwords that software uses to talk to other software, and they have the notable weakness of not being able to be suspicious about who is holding them.

With those credentials in hand, the attackers could log in to a customer's Microsoft-hosted environment as if they were an authorized user. From there, according to the report, they could read emails and other files. Nothing about this required breaking down a door. The door had been given a name tag.

The scale is where the story turns. Microsoft says the hackers used AI agents to get into around 2,250 organizations, at a rate of almost 3,000 attempted sign-ins per minute. Translation: an AI agent is software that carries out a series of tasks on its own, which here meant trying doors faster than any human intruder could walk down a hallway. Microsoft said that speed leaves defenders little time to detect and respond.

The tool was identified as ARTEX, an open source AI framework that works with AI models to stress-test cyber defenses. Stress-testing is a legitimate security practice in which you attack your own systems to find weaknesses before someone else does. The report does not say whether the hackers were as thorough about the paperwork.

The same software was reportedly used in a recent compromise of South Korea's financial systems, many of which had weak security and lacked multi-factor authentication. Translation: multi-factor authentication is the extra step, such as a code on your phone, that stops a stolen password from being enough by itself. It is widely available, and its absence continues to be a growth industry for attackers.

ConnectWise said it has paused access to its cloud backup system and is investigating the breach. As of Thursday, it said it was working on restoring the service. Customers who wanted a backup of their data now have a backup plan for their backup, which is to wait.

TechCrunch described this as a bad week for cybersecurity that got worse. That is a fair summary of an industry in which the products sold to protect companies keep turning out to be the most efficient route into them. Watch for whether ConnectWise's investigation says how the credentials were stolen, which remains the question everyone with a backup contract will be asking.
