Albanian PM Finds Source of Student Unrest: Unnamed Foreigners, Location Unspecified

Edi Rama rejects calls to resign as protests over corruption and campus conditions enter a fourth day of clashes, spreading beyond Tirana.

The wire said: Albanian PM blames ‘foreign hands’ for unrest as student protests intensify · Al Jazeera

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 17, 2026 01:44 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Albanians have protested since late May over a coastal development in Zvernec linked to Jared Kushner, and the movement has since broadened into a challenge to Prime Minister Edi Rama's government. This week, student-led demonstrations turned violent.

Why it matters

Albania wants EU membership as early as 2028, and a government that is burning, literally in places, is a weak negotiating position. For young Albanians, the outcome decides whether a €540 salary and a degree are ever enough for an apartment.

Albanian Prime Minister Edi Rama said on Friday that "foreign hands" are behind the student protests demanding his resignation, according to Al Jazeera. Riot police clashed with demonstrators outside the education ministry for a fourth consecutive day, using water cannon and tear gas while protesters threw rocks. Rama did not say which foreign hands, which is the traditional level of detail for this genre of accusation.

The unrest began as something quieter. Albanians have protested since late May in what DW says is called the "Flamingo Revolution," sparked by a multimillion-euro tourism development linked to Kushner, Donald Trump's son-in-law, in the protected coastal area of Zvernec. Rama's government, per Al Jazeera, denies corruption. Translation: the protests started over a beach, and the government's response over four months was apparently not to discuss the beach.

That silence is central to the students' complaint. A civil society activist, Rigels Xhemollari, told DW the main cause is the prime minister's failure to respond during four months of peaceful protest and his mockery of the demonstrators. A 22-year-old economics master's student named Samanta put it more bluntly: "You didn't listen to us for four months while we protested peacefully. Now you'll listen as the city burns." The lesson for governments is that ignoring peaceful protest is a cheap policy right up until it stops being one.

The damage is substantial. Al Jazeera reports that protesters set fire to buildings including the prime minister's office, the finance and justice ministries and several bank branches, and that 160 people were detained Thursday evening. DW counts 152 arrests and 324 people under investigation over three days, and says police report 71 injured officers. The two outlets' tallies differ slightly, probably because they count different days and categories, and nobody has offered a foreign hand to blame for the arithmetic.

Rama's argument has a second layer. He told Albanians that removing him would betray those who gave him a strong mandate to complete EU membership negotiations, per Al Jazeera. Translation: he cannot resign because he is needed to finish the process that corruption scandals have complicated. He also told Reuters, according to DW, that governments are changed in elections, which is accurate, and also the thing the protesters are asking for in a hurry.

Opposition leader Sali Berisha, 82, denied that his Democratic Party is behind the violence. "We have no idea whether it's Greece, Iran, Serbia or someone else," he said, mocking the foreign-hands theory, while saying his party fully supports the protest. The students are not especially grateful. Skerdi Topalli, 20, told DW they want the political class of the past 35 years gone, which covers both men.

That is the practical problem. Political analyst Mark Marku told DW that a protest must produce a result and that "you cannot vote for a protest." Xhemollari proposes a caretaker government under a non-partisan prime minister and elections in May 2027. Brussels has urged calm and dialogue, its standard contribution, which is also the one thing in short supply. Watch whether Rama finds the foreign hands, or whether the students find the exit.
