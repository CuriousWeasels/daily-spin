CoreWeave's Debt Insurance Hits Distress Levels, Though Everyone Agrees the Customers Are Real

Credit default swaps on the AI cloud company topped 1,000 basis points this week, even as its stock bounced and its CEO blamed irrational markets.

The wire said: CoreWeave credit default swaps hit distress levels as investors sour on AI debt · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 16, 2026 01:49 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

CoreWeave rents out AI computing power and has taken on more than $51 billion of debt and obligations to build data centers. Investors have grown nervous about AI borrowing, and the company's bonds and stock have swung sharply this week.

Why it matters

When the cost of insuring AI debt jumps, it signals that lenders are asking whether the spending will ever pay for itself. That worry can spread to other borrowers, such as Oracle, and to the pension and bond funds that hold their debt.

CoreWeave's credit default swaps, a form of insurance against default, climbed above 1,140 basis points on Thursday before settling near 1,038, CNBC reported. That is a 16% rise in a week and 164% higher than a year ago. Translation: a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so the market is charging roughly 10.4% a year to insure the company's debt against failure.

S&P Global Market Intelligence's Gavan Nolan told CNBC that a spread of 1,000 basis points or more is generally a sign of credit distress. CoreWeave crossed that line and then stayed on the far side of it, which is the financial equivalent of a smoke detector that keeps going after you open the window.

The bonds tell the same story. The yield to maturity on its 9.75% notes due in 2031 reached 15.78%. Translation: bond prices and yields move in opposite directions, so a yield far above the coupon means buyers will only hold the debt at a steep discount. The company pays 9.75% on the face value, and the market now wants about 16% on what it would actually pay for the bond.

The stock, meanwhile, behaved like a different company. After falling 15.5% on Wednesday, CoreWeave closed up 5.94% on Thursday following an intraday drop. MarketWatch described Wall Street's views on the company as starkly divisive, which is a polite way of saying that stock buyers and bond buyers looked at the same balance sheet and filed different reports.

The company's defense is its backlog. CEO Mike Intrator wrote in a memo obtained by CNBC that markets sometimes misunderstand new kinds of technology companies and act irrationally. He said the backlog more than supports all its debt. Deutsche Bank put that backlog at over $100 billion, with about two-thirds from investment-grade customers including Microsoft, Meta, IBM and Caterpillar, and kept its buy rating, calling the sell-off a buying opportunity.

Nolan made the sharper point. Nobody doubts that demand is real, since the customers are the big hyperscalers. The doubt is about timing, because the company has heavily negative free cash flow now and needs the cash to arrive to justify its capital spending. Translation: a full order book is not the same as money in the bank, and lenders are paid in money. The Evercore ISI trading desk put it more bluntly, saying the market is saying 'no mas' to AI debt without operating cash flow support.

The worry is contagion. Jim Reid warned that this kind of concern can become a self-fulfilling prophecy. Oracle's five-year CDS spread rose about 10% this week to 282 basis points, and Reid noted that a downgrade from its BBB rating could force investors restricted to investment-grade debt to sell. Meanwhile Nebius and Iren ended the day roughly flat to slightly lower, and Micron and AMD moved in opposite directions after a sell-off in South Korean chip stocks, so the market was less a stampede than a nervous shuffle.

What to watch next is whether the backlog turns into cash faster than the interest bills arrive. If it does, Intrator will have been right about the market's mood. If it does not, the insurance will have been a bargain.
