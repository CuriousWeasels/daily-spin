Treasury Tells IMF to Check Its Forecasts, Then Bets $40 Trillion Debt on Growth Forecast

A new Treasury counselor dismissed the IMF's debt warning as 'messy' forecasting, while the deficit nears $2 trillion and the 10-year yield tops 5%.

The wire said: U.S. Treasury dismisses IMF debt warning and 'messy' forecasting · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 15, 2026 12:56 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The IMF's Kristalina Georgieva warned in Bangkok that strong U.S. growth alone cannot stabilize rising federal debt, which passed $40 trillion this year. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has said the country can 'grow our way out,' and his new counselor, David Zervos, answered the IMF on Thursday.

Why it matters

Higher Treasury yields feed directly into mortgage rates, which are around 7.5%, and make the government's own borrowing costlier. With divided government expected after the midterms, a fix for the deficit looks years away.

David Zervos, the newly appointed counselor to the Treasury Secretary, told CNBC on Thursday that the International Monetary Fund should remember 'how messy their forecasts have been' before commenting on U.S. debt. He was responding to IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva, who said in Bangkok that strong American growth would not be enough to put federal debt on a sustainable path. It is a robust defense of the U.S. fiscal position, in the sense that it does not mention the U.S. fiscal position.

Zervos, a former chief market strategist at Jefferies, offered a list of IMF misses: a 2008 outlook with world growth at 3% before a global contraction, optimism on Southeast Asia before 1997, gloom on Russia in 2022, and optimism on Argentina after a $57 billion bailout. His conclusion was to 'take it all with a grain of salt.' Translation: the referee has made bad calls before, so the foul count should be disregarded.

The numbers the IMF was commenting on are not in dispute. CNBC's analysis says the fiscal 2026 deficit will land near $2 trillion, with the Congressional Budget Office estimating $1.993 trillion, or roughly 6.0% to 6.2% of GDP. Bessent has argued for a deficit below 4% of GDP by the end of President Trump's term, and CNBC says there is almost no way that happens.

The cost of that borrowing is already showing up. The 10-year Treasury yield, which was 5.21% in the latest official data, has climbed 123 basis points since the end of February. Translation: a basis point is a hundredth of a percentage point, so the government and everyone who borrows against its rates now pay about 1.23 points more. The 30-year mortgage hovers near 7.5%, and Redfin reported that pending home sales fell 4% in September, the biggest monthly drop in three years.

CNBC cites several causes: the war with Iran that began Feb. 28, which pushed up oil and diesel and raised the odds that the Federal Reserve hikes; heavy government bond issuance; and corporations borrowing hundreds of billions to build AI infrastructure. Treasuries are therefore competing for investors' money with companies promising to build the future, which is also the thing Bessent hopes will rescue the budget.

That hope has a price tag. The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget calculates that growth alone would need a decade of 7.2% growth to balance the budget. GDP growth for the second quarter was just revised up from 1.5% to 2.2%. Closing that gap would require what CNBC calls an almost unimaginable transformation of the economy.

Higher rates also make the arithmetic worse. The CBO estimates that rates a half-point above its forecast would add $1.9 trillion to 10-year deficits, and 1.5 points above would add $6 trillion. It expects the 10-year to average 4.3%, versus 5.3% at the time of CNBC's analysis. The IMF, for its part, has been telling France to 'get your house in order,' so the U.S. is not being singled out.

The politics are less encouraging. Republicans are widely forecast to lose at least the House in the Nov. 3 midterms, and Trump pledged $5,000 checks to every adult citizen if they swept. Rep. Hakeem Jeffries, the likely next speaker, wants to reverse $1 trillion in healthcare cuts. CNBC notes that higher yields are also attracting buyers, so expensive borrowing is not yet a crisis. Watch whether the Treasury's official deficit figure arrives with a forecast attached.
