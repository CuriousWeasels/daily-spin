Britain Hosts Omani Sultan to Praise Peacemaking While Sending Typhoon Jets to Saudi Arabia

On the same Tuesday, London toasted Oman's diplomacy over Hormuz and announced fighter jets for Saudi air defense, as Washington and Tehran each said the other must move first.

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 21, 2026 02:09 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The US-Israel war against Iran began in late February, and the Strait of Hormuz remains choked. Yemen's civil war has also escalated since a years-long truce collapsed in September, with Houthi attacks increasingly hitting Saudi Arabia.

Why it matters

Hormuz and the Houthi blockade both affect oil exports and energy prices, which eventually show up in what ordinary people pay for fuel and goods. A deal looks distant, and Gulf investment plans are already being postponed.

Britain had a full diplomatic day on Tuesday. King Charles III hosted Oman's Sultan Haitham bin Tariq at Windsor Castle for the first state visit by an Omani sultan in 44 years, and the Ministry of Defense announced it would send RAF Typhoon fighter jets to Saudi Arabia. The two events were not officially connected, though together they amount to a fairly complete menu: peace on one side of the table, air defense on the other.

At the state banquet, about 160 guests heard the king praise Oman's "noble efforts" to help reopen the Strait of Hormuz, according to Al Jazeera. He called Oman a "trusted interlocutor to all parties." Translation: Oman is the country everyone is willing to talk to, mostly because it has spent years declining to pick a side. The sultan, for his part, said Oman has chosen "an independent path," which is the sort of thing one can say with confidence when one borders the waterway everyone is arguing about.

The guest list was instructive. Prime Minister Andy Burnham attended, as did the chief executives of Shell, BP and Airbus. Talks at Downing Street on Wednesday are expected to cover security, trade and investment. Peace is a noble cause, and it is helpfully also a sales opportunity.

Meanwhile, Britain's Typhoons are headed to protect Saudi cities and critical infrastructure from drones and missiles, DW reported. The Ministry of Defense said they will not carry out offensive strikes on Houthi targets in Yemen. Translation: the jets are there to shoot things down, not to start things, a distinction the Houthis are free to ignore. Britain is also sending an Atlas A400M transport aircraft and military advisers, and has extended its offer of air-to-air refueling for Saudi aircraft for another 90 days.

The need is real. Defense Secretary Wes Streeting said the attacks were "absolutely despicable," and singled out strikes on Riyadh's international airport, which has been targeted 20 times since the start of October. Reports put the death toll from those strikes at no fewer than 18, with hundreds wounded. Yemeni authorities also said a Houthi strike on Monday killed 58 people at a training camp for Saudi-backed government forces. The Houthis have also imposed a naval blockade and attacked tankers, pushing up energy prices. Saudi Arabia and the United States accuse Iran of backing the Houthis; Tehran denies it.

The economic consequences are already visible. Saudi Arabia postponed its Future Investment Initiative conference, nicknamed "Davos in the Desert," until 2027. It had been due for October 26-29, and media reports said major banks and private equity firms had raised security concerns. A conference designed to persuade investors the kingdom is a stable place to put money has been delayed because investors were not persuaded. It is hard to find a cleaner demonstration of the problem.

On the main negotiation, there is little sign of movement. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio said a peace deal with Iran is extremely difficult and that economic pressure will continue until Tehran stops targeting commercial shipping in Hormuz. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said the strait will stay closed until Washington accepts Tehran's conditions, and that indirect talks continue through Pakistan and Qatar.

Put simply, each side says the other must act first, and the strait stays shut until someone does. Indirect talks through intermediaries are a way of negotiating while maintaining that no negotiating is occurring. Oman, for one, has the banquet to show for its trouble. Whether anyone gets a deal out of it is the question to watch after Wednesday's meetings.
