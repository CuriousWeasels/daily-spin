OpenAI Fires Three Safety Researchers for Leaks It Has Not Specified in Writing

Tomek Korbak, Mikita Balesni and Jasmine Wang say they were never told what they mishandled, and suspect the real offense was asking to see how the models think.

The wire said: Three fired OpenAI safety researchers say they were never told what they actually leaked · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 25, 2026 15:06 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

OpenAI dismissed three safety researchers after an internal leak investigation into alleged violations of its policies on sensitive information. The three have now given their account to the Wall Street Journal, as reported by The Decoder.

Why it matters

If employees who work on AI oversight fear being fired for talking to outside auditors, the outside checks on increasingly powerful models get weaker. Everyone else has to take the company's word that its systems are safe.

OpenAI fired three safety researchers, Tomek Korbak, Mikita Balesni and Jasmine Wang, after an internal investigation into alleged leaks. According to The Decoder's summary of Wall Street Journal interviews, all three deny sharing confidential information and say they were never told in writing which information they supposedly mishandled. This is an unusual approach to a leak investigation, in that the leak remains unidentified even after the leakers have been dismissed.

The sequence, as reported, is brisk. OpenAI's security team suspected the three of sharing details of the company's internal model architecture with an outside safety researcher, and asked them to unlock their personal phones. Investigators found Signal chats with disappearing messages enabled. A week later, all three were fired at the same time. OpenAI said Signal is not an approved communication tool but insisted it was not the reason for the firings. Korbak counters that disappearing messages are routine security practice across Silicon Valley.

The underlying dispute concerns something called chain of thought. Translation: reasoning models write out intermediate steps on an internal scratchpad, and safety researchers read it to understand why a model did what it did. It is roughly the difference between a student showing their work and a student simply producing an answer and asking to be trusted.

In a 2025 paper, the three joined leading researchers from several AI labs in urging that this ability be preserved. Over the summer, employees at AI companies began discussing models that think longer but write down fewer steps. Balesni pushed back internally by sharing a related proposal from Ryan Greenblatt, then chief scientist at Redwood Research. Korbak was also in regular contact with Greenblatt while they analyzed an incident involving OpenAI agents attacking Hugging Face, for which METR had hired Greenblatt to investigate independently.

That cooperation became a problem after The Information reported on September 1 that OpenAI's new model, Astra, was more capable but revealed less of its reasoning. Chief scientist Jakub Pachocki dismissed the report as muddled on X, though The Decoder notes he had himself warned of an industry-wide race toward models that can no longer be monitored. A few weeks later, OpenAI opened its leak investigation. Balesni says investigators named Greenblatt as the suspected source. Greenblatt denies passing confidential information to journalists.

Wang faced a separate accusation: that she repeatedly accessed an executive's inbox. She says she had asked nearly a year earlier for that access to be revoked, accidentally opened a sensitive email the previous month, did not share its contents, and reported it immediately. OpenAI says the access itself was not the issue, which leaves the reader to wonder what the issue was.

OpenAI told the Journal that the firings had nothing to do with safety concerns the researchers raised. The researchers suspect otherwise, and Korbak says colleagues are now afraid to talk to outsiders or voice safety concerns internally. In a letter to the board, the three call for closer cooperation with external safety organizations.

There is also a recent audit. Staff from METR and Redwood Research found numerous security gaps in OpenAI's infrastructure, and OpenAI redacted nearly half the final report. The company says it fixed the problems, resumed training in early October, and will soon announce several new external partnerships. Watch whether those partners are allowed to read the whole report, and whether they are allowed to use Signal.
