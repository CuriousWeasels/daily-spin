Alleged Ransomware Gangster Books Holiday in Japan, Which Cooperates With Police Unlike Home

German police are adding the 2024 London hospitals hack to their case against a 28-year-old Russian suspect, while British police weigh an unlawful killing inquiry that would be a world first.

The wire said: Russian man linked to deadly London hospitals hack arrested · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 18, 2026 01:40 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

In June 2024, the Qilin ransomware gang hit Synnovis, a blood testing provider serving London hospitals, disrupting pathology services and cancelling operations. A patient at King's College Hospital later died, with a long wait for a blood test result named as one contributing factor.

Why it matters

If cyber-crime can be prosecuted as a cause of death, hackers who target hospitals face the kind of charges that currently apply to people who hurt patients in person. It also shows that Russian suspects are most reachable when they leave Russia.

German police have detained a 28-year-old Russian man, named by German press as Vladimir K, who is accused of being a core member of the Qilin cyber-crime gang. The BBC reported that German detectives are now including the 2024 attack on London pathology provider Synnovis in their investigation. The suspect was arrested in Japan in May after travelling there on what he apparently regarded as a holiday.

Qilin has allegedly hacked more than 4,000 organisations since 2024 and extorted more than $140m (£105m) from victims. Translation: it is a business, and a large one, with a customer base that did not choose to be customers. The specific charge for which Vladimir K was originally wanted in Germany is more modest: breaking into a logistics company in September 2024, encrypting its data and demanding around $160,000 in cryptocurrency.

The Synnovis attack, on 3 June 2024, is the one the British public will remember. Synnovis is a partnership between NHS hospitals and the German company Synlab, and the hack led to a critical incident at some London hospitals. Blood transfusion services were severely disrupted, operations were cancelled and emergency patients were diverted elsewhere. In total, more than 10,000 appointments were disrupted over weeks.

King's College Hospital NHS Foundation Trust confirmed a year later that one patient had died unexpectedly. A spokesperson said a number of contributing factors led to the death, including a long wait for a blood test result. The BBC describes it as the first cyber attack in history directly linked to a death. Translation: the hackers did not merely lock up files. They locked up the part of the hospital that answers the question of what is wrong with you.

The BBC also reported that British police are examining whether its own inquiry could lead to an unlawful killing case, potentially a world first for cyber-crime. The precedent is not encouraging. A 2020 attack on a Düsseldorf hospital prompted a negligent homicide investigation after an elderly patient died, but it was dropped. Medical experts concluded the patient would have died regardless, and prosecutors could not prove the attack was the direct cause. Proving that a ransomware attack killed someone, it turns out, requires proving a chain of causation that the attackers spent their careers making as tangled as possible.

The hackers threatened to publish the stolen data on their dark net website unless Synnovis paid a ransom. No ransom was paid, and the criminals published the highly private data anyway. At the time they told the BBC the attack was not only about money but also a protest against the UK's lack of help in the Russian war on Ukraine. They did not say whether they were Russian or Ukrainian, and their claims were dismissed as an attempt to distance themselves from Qilin's known extortion tactics. Political motives, in other words, appeared only after the invoice went unpaid.

The arrest also illustrates the geography of ransomware enforcement. The BBC notes it is very rare for hackers to be arrested in Russia, as its government refuses to cooperate with Western law enforcement requests. The practical result is that suspects are safest at home and least safe on a beach. Qilin is also blamed for the October 2025 hack on Japanese beer maker Asahi, which makes the choice of holiday destination look less like a getaway and more like a tour of the client list.

What to watch next: whether German prosecutors formally tie Vladimir K to Synnovis, and whether British police decide a death can be charged as an unlawful killing. The extradition story, first reported in early October, is still unfolding.
