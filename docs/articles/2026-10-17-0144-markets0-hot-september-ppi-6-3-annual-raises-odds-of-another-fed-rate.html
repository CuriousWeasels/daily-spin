Wholesale Prices Rise 6.3%, Fed Told Its Work Is Not Done, Again

September's producer price index rose 1.3% on the month, more than double forecasts, as the Iran war's energy shock spread into the rest of the economy and put a rate hike back on the table.

The wire said: Wholesale prices jumped 6.3% annually in September, biggest gain since 2022 · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 17, 2026 01:44 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Inflation has stayed above the Federal Reserve's 2% target since 2021 and has climbed since tariffs began in April 2025, then faster after the U.S. war with Iran disrupted oil supply. The Fed raised rates last month, and the question is now whether it does so again.

Why it matters

Higher wholesale costs tend to reach store shelves, and a further rate hike would make mortgages, car loans and credit cards more expensive. The data arrive just ahead of the midterm elections, when voters will have views on both.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Thursday that the producer price index rose 1.3% in September and 6.3% from a year earlier, according to CNBC. Those were the largest monthly and annual gains since March 2022 and December 2022, respectively. Economists polled by Dow Jones had expected 0.6% and 5.5%, which means the forecasters were off by roughly the amount of the thing they were forecasting.

Translation: the producer price index measures what businesses receive for their goods and services before the rest of us encounter them at a register. It is the part of inflation that is still in transit. Think of it as a package tracking page for your future grocery bill.

Energy did much of the work. CNBC noted that gasoline prices rose 20%, and the BLS said almost half of the September increase in processed goods for immediate demand was tied to diesel. Diesel runs the trucks, and trucks deliver everything else, so a fuel shock has a way of becoming a shock to everything. Brent crude stood at 126.55 in the latest official data.

The comforting theory is that this is just energy, and energy comes and goes. The data are less cooperative. Core PPI, which strips out food and energy, rose 0.7% on the month and 5% annually. A second measure that also excludes trade services still rose 0.5%, which CNBC's source read as a sign that inflation is broader than fuel.

The consumer price index, released Wednesday, was less dramatic but still warm. Prices rose 0.6% on the month and 3.7% annually, against forecasts of 0.6% and 3.6%. Core CPI rose 0.3% on the month and 2.6% on the year, slightly above expectations. Bespoke Investment Group found that the Fed's Beige Book, its collection of anecdotes about the economy, mentioned inflation-related terms more often than any edition on record. Nobody had to be told twice.

Consumers are responding the way consumers do, by spending on gasoline and cutting back on everything else. Retail sales rose 0.8% in September, beating the 0.4% forecast. Excluding autos and gas stations, however, they rose about 0.25%, down from roughly 1.1% the month before. The headline looks like strength, but a good part of it is people paying more for fuel.

That leaves the Fed with a decision on Oct. 28. CME's FedWatch tool put the odds of a hike at nearly 34%, up from under 18% a week earlier, while Fed futures cited by CNBC's second report showed 29%. Both showed about 90% odds of at least one hike by year-end. Several Fed officials have said there is no need to hurry, though they have suggested more hikes are coming. Translation: the Fed is not in a rush to do the thing it expects to do.

Economists expect the Fed's preferred gauge, core PCE inflation, to rise 0.32% in September, lifting the annual rate to 3.1% from 3.01%. Chairman Kevin Warsh has said one test for not hiking is confidence that inflation will return to 2% in a timely way. Inflation has been above target since 2021, so that confidence is being asked to hold for quite a while.

MarketWatch noted that the bond market largely shrugged, with buyers stepping in as yields became attractive. The 10-year Treasury yield stood at 5.1% and the 2-year at 4.77%, while the fed funds rate was 3.88%. Bond investors appear to believe that rates have already gone up enough. The Fed has until Oct. 28 to agree or disagree.
