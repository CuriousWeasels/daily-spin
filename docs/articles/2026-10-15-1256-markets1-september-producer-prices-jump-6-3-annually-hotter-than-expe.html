Wholesale Prices Rise 6.3%, Giving Consumers a Preview of What They Will Soon Be Paying

September's producer price index jumped 1.3% on the month, more than double what economists expected, as the war with Iran keeps energy costly.

By The Daily Spin Desk · Oct 15, 2026 12:56 UTC · 2 min read
Catch upThe U.S. is in a war with Iran that has curtailed Middle East oil supply and pushed energy prices up, according to CNBC. The producer price index, which tracks what businesses get paid for goods and services, is the latest inflation gauge to come in hot.
Why it mattersWholesale costs tend to flow through to store shelves and service bills, so a hot PPI is often an early look at what consumers may pay next. With Treasury yields above the Fed's policy rate, borrowing is already getting pricier.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Thursday that the producer price index rose 1.3% in September and 6.3% from a year earlier, CNBC reported. Those were the biggest monthly gain since March 2022 and the biggest annual gain since December 2022. Economists polled by Dow Jones had expected 0.6% and 5.5%, so the forecasters missed the monthly figure by roughly the amount of the monthly figure.

Translation: the producer price index measures the prices businesses receive for what they sell, before it reaches you. It is sometimes called wholesale inflation, on the theory that inflation, like a houseguest, announces itself at the back door before arriving in the living room.

Energy did a sizable share of the work. CNBC said the Middle East conflict has curtailed oil supply and sent prices surging. A narrower measure that strips out food, energy and trade still rose 0.5% on the month and 5% on the year. This is the part that complicates the argument that it is all just oil.

Consumers got their own data the day before. The consumer price index rose 0.6% in September and 3.7% over twelve months, against forecasts of 0.6% and 3.6%. Core CPI, which excludes food and energy because they are considered too volatile to be trusted, rose 0.3% on the month and 2.6% on the year, against expectations of 0.2% and 2.5%. Separate official data cited in the sources put annual CPI at 4.02% as of September 1, a figure that differs from CNBC's 3.7%. The sources do not explain the gap, so readers may pick whichever number matches their grocery bill.

The Federal Reserve's Beige Book, a periodic compilation of anecdotes about the economy from around the country, contained the most mentions of inflation-related terms on record, according to an analysis by Bespoke Investment Group. Translation: the Fed asked businesses how things were going, and they used the word prices a great deal.

Spending has not yet buckled. Retail sales rose 0.8% in September, ahead of the 0.4% economists expected, though slower than the more than 1% gain in August. Households are, for now, paying higher prices with the confidence of people who have not yet seen next month's statement.

Markets noticed. Investing.com reported that Nasdaq 100 futures held onto losses after the report. Official data show the 10-year Treasury yield at 5.21% and the 2-year at 4.79%, both above the 3.88% federal funds rate, which suggests bond investors expect the central bank to have a harder time cutting rates than it would like. Unemployment sits at 4.2%, leaving the Fed with a labor market that is fine and prices that are not.

What to watch next is whether the hotter wholesale costs show up in core consumer prices, which have so far been better behaved. If they do, the Fed's options narrow, and the war's economic effects will have moved from the gas pump to the rest of the store.
