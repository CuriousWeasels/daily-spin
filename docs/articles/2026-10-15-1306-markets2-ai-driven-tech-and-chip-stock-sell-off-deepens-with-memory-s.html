AI Stocks Lose $1 Trillion in Four Days, Blame Interest Rates, China, and Year 2029

Twenty of the world's most valuable AI and chip stocks have shed more than $1 trillion this week as borrowing costs rise and memory makers face a forecast boom-and-bust that has not yet happened.

The wire said: Nvidia, AMD, Micron fall as AI stock sell-off deepens · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 15, 2026 13:06 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Chip and AI stocks have rallied for months on surging demand for data center hardware, especially memory. This week, hotter U.S. inflation data, rising Treasury yields, and talk of Chinese competition turned that rally into a global sell-off.

Why it matters

AI spending is financed largely with borrowed money, so higher yields make the build-out costlier and can hit the tech shares that anchor many retirement portfolios. Whether this is a rate scare or a demand problem will shape how much of the boom survives.

CNBC reported that 20 of the most valuable global AI and semiconductor stocks have lost more than $1 trillion in market value so far this week. In premarket trading Thursday, Nvidia was down 1.8%, AMD 3.1% and Micron 4.2%. Translation: premarket trading is the period before the official open when investors place early bets on how bad the day will be, and they were not optimistic.

The pain was larger overseas. According to CNBC, SK Hynix fell more than 10% and Samsung Electronics 8.73%, while Japan's Advantest dropped almost 14% and SoftBank 7.9%. In Europe, ASML slid 5.2% and ASM International 8.6%. TSMC fell a comparatively polite 1.81%.

The first culprit is money. Wednesday's hotter-than-expected U.S. inflation data raised fears the Federal Reserve could raise rates further, and the Nasdaq fell 1.88% to 26,692.87. Official data show inflation at 3.74% year over year, the 10-year Treasury yield at 5.21%, and the federal funds rate at 3.88%. Translation: the government can now borrow more cheaply from itself than the 10-year market lends to it, and anyone building a data center wants to know what that means for the bill.

That bill is the heart of the matter. CNBC reported that Broadcom's Hock Tan warned on Monday that financing could be a bigger obstacle to AI expansion than chip or electricity shortages. Intel's chief executive also warned of financing headwinds. MarketWatch added that reported details about Anthropic's financial progress are feeding worries about the health of the AI ecosystem, though its summary gave no further specifics.

The second culprit is memory, where CNBC said SK Hynix has dropped 26% over five days, Samsung 22% and Micron about 11%. China's CXMT announced a server memory product on a new architecture that could pack in more data, and Elon Musk posted that China would make "a LOT of RAM in 2028." A South Korean newspaper column then cited two experts expecting a "flood of new supply" in 2029. Translation: the market is selling today over a glut that, by this forecast, arrives in roughly three years.

Analysts were skeptical. Quilter Cheviot's Ben Barringer noted that a new design is worthless if yields are poor, since with 50% yield you are putting half the chips in the bin. Rosenblatt's Kevin Cassidy said it will not swing the supply-demand equation. CNBC also noted that a forecast that far out has not been echoed by other analysts.

There is also a structural argument that this time differs. Memory has historically boomed when demand outran supply and busted when factories caught up, and new plants take three to five years to build. Counterpoint Research sees DRAM prices falling about 23% in 2028 from 2027, yet still about 230% above 2025, with long-term contracts softening any drop. A 23% fall that leaves prices more than triple is the sort of bust most industries would call a bonus.

Dan Ives of Yorkville Ives called the dip a buying opportunity, saying rates are "a financing question, not a demand question." He said he would turn cautious on cut capital spending plans, canceled contracts or falling GPU rental prices. Translation: the bull case holds until customers stop buying, which, helpfully, is the one thing nobody has yet reported.
