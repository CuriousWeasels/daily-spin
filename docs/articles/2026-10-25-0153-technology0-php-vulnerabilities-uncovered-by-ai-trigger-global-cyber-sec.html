AI Finds Decade-Old PHP Flaws; World Unplugs Websites, Then Checks Whether Anyone Was Actually Attacking

Two critical bugs in the language behind much of the web set off a global alert, though no successful exploits have been reported so far.

The wire said: What is PHP and why did it spark a global cyber-security scramble? · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 25, 2026 01:53 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Researchers at Wiz and Calif, using OpenAI tools, disclosed two serious flaws in PHP, the server-side language that runs a large share of websites. Governments and companies rushed to patch or pull sites offline after details leaked early.

Why it matters

If AI keeps finding old bugs faster than humans can patch them, warnings like this will become routine, and the next one may be real. Site owners, especially small ones running outdated PHP, are the ones left doing the patching.

The BBC reported that two vulnerabilities in PHP, tracked as CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, set off a global cyber-security alert this week. Researchers rated them 9.2 out of 10 for severity, and no successful exploits of the flaw, nicknamed php2shell, had been reported as of Friday morning. The internet responded to this the way it responds to most things: by turning parts of itself off.

PHP is an open-source language, created in the early 1990s, that runs on web servers and assembles the data behind a page into something you can read. Translation: it is the unseen kitchen behind a large part of the web. According to Wiz, it powers 70% of the world's websites, including Wikipedia and WordPress. The two flaws, Wiz and Calif said, sit in the language itself and affect versions released over the last decade.

Under the right conditions, Wiz wrote, attackers could take over affected servers and steal sensitive data. Calif researcher Ryan Austin called it "the most serious vulnerability I've ever found." The tool that found it was AI from OpenAI, which can read code far faster than people. Salil Kanhere of UNSW Sydney put it plainly: the bugs were not new, but the ability to find them was.

The response was global and, in places, theatrical. Wikimedia took some systems down as a precaution. The Finnish government, Edinburgh council and some BBC pages went offline, and the Netherlands' cyber-security agency pulled its own website. The UK government said 32 sites were taken down, none of them large public-facing services. The US National Security Agency advised organisations that could not patch immediately to go offline, citing a possible "direct path" to a system's database. Japan's prime minister, Sanae Takaichi, urged people to check their sites on X. In Japan, SoftBank reportedly suspended its emergency earthquake alerts, which is a rather sharp way to learn what depends on PHP.

The PHP Foundation released patches on Wednesday, and then the second phase began: working out how bad things were. Cloudflare's Matthew Prince wrote that exploit attempts went from thousands in the 10 hours before the announcement to millions afterwards. Amazon said it was targeted in multiple attempts before disclosure but its sites ran normally. Translation: the internet's front door was being rattled hard, and so far nobody has reported it opening.

Some of the people doing the work are not impressed with the signal quality. System administrator Adam Mudd told the BBC that "sometimes it takes me longer to take a website down than to fix the PHP problem." Another administrator, Loganaden Velvindron in Mauritius, said many PHP versions in use are end-of-life, meaning some apps need rebuilding rather than a patch. Translation: some of the software in question was already abandoned by its maker, which is a hard thing to update.

Then there was the intelligence problem. UK cyber expert Daniel Card said responders were "flooded with rubbish intel" about hackers with working proof-of-concept attacks. The BBC reported that one major security firm warned that attackers had a working exploit, then retracted it. Mark Maunder of Wordfence warned of "alert fatigue", the condition in which people stop reacting to alarms because too many were false, just before the one that is real.

The caveats cut both ways. Kanhere expects more exploitation attempts now that technical details are public, and experts warn that poorly maintained sites run by small organisations are most at risk. Patch rates are climbing, though not as fast as hoped. Meanwhile, on Hacker News, someone posted a "Show HN: Steal My Bitcoin" challenge, inviting anyone to use the PHP flaw against them. It had 402 points and 245 comments at the time of the source, and it is as close to a live-fire test of the panic as anyone has offered.

What to watch: whether the first confirmed exploit shows up, and whether the next AI-found bug is announced with a little less Hollywood. Patch now, and keep the sirens for when they matter.
