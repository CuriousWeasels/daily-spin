Anthropic Files to Go Public, Asks Wall Street to Underwrite $1 Trillion in Compute Bills

The S-1 shows revenue up fourteenfold to $16.3 billion, plus roughly $850 billion in commitments that assume the growth never stops.

The wire said: Anthropic is asking Wall Street to believe in AGI · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 30, 2026 03:06 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Anthropic has filed an S-1, the document a company must submit to sell shares to the public, for a Nasdaq listing that could start trading as soon as next month at a reported $1.5 trillion to $2 trillion valuation. It is the first detailed look at the finances of a leading frontier AI lab.

Why it matters

If the filing's growth assumptions hold, one of the largest listings ever becomes a bet on AI spending. If they slip, the contracts behind it will not shrink to match, and chip makers, cloud providers and investors are all on the other side of those contracts.

Anthropic has filed to go public on the Nasdaq, and the document finally shows what a frontier AI lab costs to run. According to The Verge, revenue grew fourteen times to $16.3 billion between the first half of 2025 and the first half of 2026. The company also reported $1.62 billion in adjusted income for the first half of 2026, after removing an $80 billion loss that the writer describes as largely an accounting matter.

Translation: adjusted income is profit after you subtract the parts of the bill you would prefer not to discuss, such as employee compensation and certain other charges. The Verge's reading is that the answer to whether AI can pay for itself is, for this company and for now, yes. That is a notable change from the usual answer, which was a slide deck.

Analysts were enthusiastic. Joey Brookhart of SemiAnalysis told The Verge's Decoder podcast that he saw little to nitpick, citing gross margins, compute margins and free cash flow. He also noted that chip and data-center stocks rallied again in the two days after the filing. Investors in companies that sell Anthropic its hardware appear to have read the document as a purchase order.

The less cheerful part is in the commitments. The Verge adds up $265.6 billion in non-cancelable purchase commitments, $321.4 billion in leases not yet started, and about $245 billion in newer compute agreements. It then adds another $125 billion to $150 billion in deals not yet finalized. The total comes to a minimum of about $850 billion over 20 years, or roughly a trillion dollars with the pending deals. The writer admits he may have missed something, which is a reasonable thing to say about page 64 and page 137, where some figures are left blank.

Translation: a non-cancelable commitment is a promise to pay whether or not the customers show up. Analyst Harrison Rolfes of PitchBook estimates about $90 billion a year from 2027 to 2030, which would need $188 billion in revenue to keep today's ratio of cost to compute steady. His summary, quoted by The Verge: "Growth is not optional for this company."

Anthropic's case rests on discipline. It skipped image generators and consumer novelties, focused on coding and cybersecurity, and sold to enterprises. Three-quarters of customers in the first half of 2026 spent at least $100,000 a year, and net revenue retention was 900 percent, meaning existing customers spend far more each year. The Verge notes that Anthropic names only OpenAI and Google as rivals, which is one way of handling a crowded field.

The field is crowded all the same. Brookhart said Anthropic took about 90 percent of industry net new annualized revenue in the first half of the year, but OpenAI was probably the majority in the third quarter. He added that open-source inference spending grew from about $1 billion to more than $10 billion over the same period. Customers also switch coding tools easily, which is a polite way of saying loyalty is a feature you have to keep buying.

Brookhart also sees growth beyond coding. He argues that reaching $1 trillion in revenue requires white-collar work to move from AI augmentation to labor replacement, which would mean the customers' payroll is the product's real revenue line. Futurum Group analyst Brendan Burke cautioned that the filing freezes a happy period when demand outran compute supply and Anthropic could charge more for what it had.

Translation: the S-1 is a photograph of a good quarter, taken by a company that has promised to keep having good quarters until 2045. Next to watch is whether the third-quarter numbers match the first half, and whether the next model can cut customer costs, as Opus 5.5 reportedly did by 40 percent over Opus 5, without cutting margins with it.
