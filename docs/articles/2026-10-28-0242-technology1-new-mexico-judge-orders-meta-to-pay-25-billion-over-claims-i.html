Meta Loses $25 Billion in New Mexico, Making 50 Other States Check Their Receipts

A New Mexico judge ordered Meta to pay $25 billion over privacy claims that most other states settled for $459 million, prompting some awkward arithmetic among attorneys general.

The wire said: Meta’s $25 billion legal loss just put blood in the water · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 28, 2026 02:42 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

This summer, attorneys general from across the US announced a $17 billion settlement with Meta over kids' online safety and related claims. New Mexico did not settle those claims, took them to a jury, and last week a judge ordered Meta to pay $25 billion.

Why it matters

The ruling could change how much states and private plaintiffs demand from tech companies, and whether they bother to settle at all. If it survives appeal, the price of misleading users about privacy may be much higher than Meta budgeted.

A New Mexico judge has ordered Meta to pay $25 billion over claims that the company misled users about protecting their data and policing misinformation and hate speech, according to The Verge. The claims stem from the 2018 Cambridge Analytica scandal and related incidents. The sum is larger than the $17 billion multistate settlement announced this summer, which until last week was the number people used when they wanted to say unprecedented.

The comparison is awkward for the states that settled. The Verge reported that in most of them, the Cambridge Analytica-style claims were covered by a section of the kids online safety settlement, resolved with a $459 million payment. Translation: some attorneys general sold a particular claim for about $459 million, and a neighboring state has just been awarded $25 billion for what looks like the same kind of claim.

Daphne Keller, director of platform regulation at the Stanford Program in Law, Science & Technology, said the settling attorneys general may feel some regret about the claims they waived. If even a fraction of the award is upheld, she said, the settling states "took pennies on the dollar compared to New Mexico." Waiving a claim means giving up the right to sue over it later, which is the legal equivalent of selling your house the week before the neighborhood booms.

There are reasons the settlers may not deserve all the sympathy. A settlement brings certainty and faster payment, even though Meta will pay over 10 years. It also secured product commitments, such as restrictions on teen usage, that a court might not have ordered, and they can take effect soon after approval. Some states may also have worked with different laws or different evidence than New Mexico had.

Meta, for its part, says it will win. Chief legal officer C.J. Mahoney said the company is "confident we will prevail on appeal" and called the lawsuit an attempt to impose "a tax on speech." The appeal matters a great deal, because a $25 billion judgment that gets reduced is a very different signal from one that sticks. The Verge also noted a separate judgment of nearly $1 billion against Meta in New Mexico.

Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier, whose state stayed out of the settlement, called it "a slap on the wrist" on X and said Florida would hold Meta accountable. The District of Columbia never settled its Cambridge Analytica claims and is still litigating, so it may be the next test of whether New Mexico's result can be repeated. School districts, individuals and tribes with outstanding claims now have fresh reason to keep going, Keller said.

Not everyone expects a stampede. Keller noted that Meta would go broke if every case produced a result like this one, though any one, or 10, or 50 of them could hit the jackpot. Blake Reid, an associate professor at Colorado Law, pointed out that 50 states have different resources, politics and litigation skill, so Meta faces up to 50 small opponents that will break in different ways. Congress has stalled on major tech bills and the Trump administration has often favored lighter regulation, leaving states to do the work.

Settling attorneys general cannot revisit the covered claims against Meta, but they can still press harder against TikTok, YouTube and Snap. The lesson for everyone is that a courtroom, unlike a settlement table, occasionally pays out like a slot machine. Watch the appeal, and watch how many attorneys general suddenly discover an appetite for trial.
