Marseille Bans Protests for Eight Hours, Reports Tourists Were Only Crowd

After a weekend in which more than 500 businesses were damaged, a short assembly ban and a minors' curfew brought calm, while the grievances behind the unrest went unaddressed.

The wire said: Marseille braces for more unrest after protests give way to 'apocalyptic' disorder · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 20, 2026 15:53 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Protests across France turned into serious disorder on Saturday, with Marseille among the worst hit. The interior minister offered to resign, and President Macron rejected the offer and condemned the violence, according to the BBC and NPR.

Why it matters

The calm in Marseille depends on bans and curfews rather than any change in the conditions that sent people into the streets, and more demonstrations are planned for the weekend. Residents, shop owners and police are all bracing for a repeat.

Marseille spent Monday under thunderstorms and police supervision, one day after protests in France's second city gave way to looting. Local business leaders told the BBC that more than 500 premises were damaged, most of them looted. By Monday, the BBC reported, the largest crowd in the city centre was a group of weather-beaten tourists on the quayside, which is one way to measure the success of a public gathering ban.

The ban lasted eight hours and was meant to prevent a third consecutive day of disorder. Translation: the authorities concluded that the surest way to stop a demonstration from turning violent was to forbid demonstrations. From Tuesday evening, an overnight curfew for minors takes effect in parts of Marseille, and an overnight curfew in parts of Paris, which began Monday, has been extended.

The human toll was heavy on the police side. Arnaud Blaise of the UN1TE police union told the BBC that 118 officers were injured in Marseille, more than a tenth of the national figure, and called the violence "unprecedented." He said police were overwhelmed after firing 4,000 tear gas grenades and then running out. Translation: the state brought a large supply of crowd control and still ran out before the crowd did.

Police made 101 arrests in the city, a quarter of them children, according to the BBC. Blaise blamed far-left agitators known as the Black Bloc for provoking disorder and young people from the deprived northern suburbs for exploiting it. Critics, as the BBC put it to him, have accused police of heavy-handedness toward peaceful students while looters went unchecked. He rejected that and said more police than students had been hurt.

Mayor Benoît Payan, a Socialist, called the response "a fiasco" and said police were under-staffed and poorly deployed. He warned that if the only target remains high school students, looting scenes will return. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez, whose resignation Macron rejected, travelled to Marseille on Monday evening to meet him, which is the ministerial equivalent of answering a complaint by showing up to the meeting.

Residents offered less tactical analysis. Maryame, an 18-year-old student who arrived in Marseille only weeks ago, described the earlier march as beautiful and the looting as terrifying, and said she was conflicted over whether violence could make the government listen. Chantal Bellanger, 79, said she had been worried for a long time, citing poor public services, hospitals and schools and abandoned neighbourhoods.

The BBC also reported that a suspect was arrested after a historic town hall was set alight, according to French media. With more demonstrations planned for the weekend, the ban and the curfews have bought a few quiet days. What they have not bought is an answer to the question Payan put to the government, which is how order was lost in the first place.
