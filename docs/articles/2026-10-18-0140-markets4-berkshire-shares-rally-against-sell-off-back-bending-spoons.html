Berkshire Buys Into Bending Spoons, Proving Even Patience Has a Price-to-Earnings Ratio

Berkshire Hathaway has put about $930 million into an Italian software consolidator that buys fallen tech names, and its shares are near record highs.

The wire said: Berkshire shares buck market sell-off, rally to highest since Buffett said he would step down as CEO · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 18, 2026 01:40 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Warren Buffett said in May 2025 that he would step down as CEO at year-end, and Berkshire shares then fell more than 14% over three months. Greg Abel now runs the company and its roughly $360 billion cash pile, and he has started spending some of it.

Why it matters

The deal suggests Berkshire is willing to leave its comfort zone for tech, while the software industry's decline is feeding a new kind of buyer whose model often means layoffs and price increases for customers. Investors in Berkshire, and users of Evernote or Airtable, are all affected.

Berkshire Hathaway shares closed Friday at their highest level since Warren Buffett announced his plan to step down, according to CNBC. The Class A shares ended at $798,000, 1.4% below their all-time closing high of $809,350. The Class B shares finished at $532.02. It is a recovery of sorts: the stock lost more than 14% after Buffett's announcement, as investors feared a 'Buffett premium' was evaporating. Translation: people had been paying extra for Buffett himself, and then wondered what they were left holding.

The stock's rise has several explanations, and only one of them involves a decision. CNBC cited analyst Meyer Shields of Keefe, Bruyette & Woods, who said higher short-term interest rates help, because Berkshire's roughly $360 billion in cash earns more. The Fed funds rate sits at 3.88%, and the 2-year Treasury yields 4.77%, so sitting still pays well. A quiet hurricane season has helped its insurance operations. Chevron, a $19 billion holding, hit a record after gaining almost 36% since July, with Brent crude at $126.55. Coca-Cola, now a $37.6 billion holding, also closed at a record.

The decision is Bending Spoons. On Wednesday the Milan-based company disclosed that Berkshire is investing a 'further' $330 million by buying 10 million new shares at $33, a discount of almost 12% to Tuesday's close of $37.86. The word 'further' was news in itself. The release also disclosed, apparently for the first time, that Berkshire put $600 million into the company's July IPO. Translation: Berkshire had been quietly a shareholder for three months, and nobody noticed until it bought more.

Bending Spoons stock rose 41% on Wednesday to $53.46. It closed Friday at $47.07, which CNBC calculates is 42.6% above Berkshire's $33 price. The CNBC newsletter author's estimate is that Berkshire holds about 30.7 million shares, worth more than $1.4 billion, a gain of 55.3% on $930 million. That assumes the $29 IPO price, and the company would not confirm the math. The September 30 portfolio disclosure next month should settle it.

What does Bending Spoons do? Founded in 2013, it buys older internet companies, including AOL, Evernote, Vimeo, Eventbrite and Brightcove, uses AI to cut costs, and spends the savings on more acquisitions. CNBC reported it recently agreed to buy Airtable for about $1.3 billion, almost 90% below its late-2021 peak valuation, and Miro for $1.36 billion, against a former valuation of $17.5 billion. Bending Spoons said a 'large portion' of Airtable's pre-acquisition team would leave with 'substantial separation packages.' Translation: the company buys the software and often not all of the people who wrote it.

The setting is what some call the 'SaaSpocalypse': AI tools displace software-as-a-service products, and the shares of Intuit, HubSpot and Adobe are each down at least 30% this year. Private equity firms such as Thoma Bravo and Vista, former big buyers, are constrained, according to former Gainsight CEO Nick Mehta, because they 'got their nose bloodied.' Bending Spoons says it hasn't lost a bidding contest in several years. Mehta said 'Bending Spooned' has become a verb in Silicon Valley, meaning to be bought for pennies on the dollar.

Customers are part of the model. After acquiring Evernote, Bending Spoons raised the annual personal subscription price to $130 from $70 in April 2023. Mizuho analyst Lloyd Walmsley said he considered switching to OneNote but stayed, because switching costs outweighed the price. That is the business thesis in miniature: own something people find annoying to leave.

Analysts are divided on the surprise. Morningstar's Greggory Warren called it, along with a $36 billion Alphabet stake, a sign Berkshire is 'finally moving out of its comfort zone.' Barron's Andrew Bary noted that many holders wanted more stock buying but that Bending Spoons was a surprise given its high valuation. Revenue more than doubled to over $700 million in the first public quarter, with growth almost entirely from acquisitions. Whether the model works when the acquisitions stop is the question the market will answer, and Berkshire's $930 million will wait for the result with its usual patience.
