Ebola Investigators Seek Origin of Nigerian Case as Three Governments Supply Three Itineraries

Switzerland says the patient never visited Congo, Uganda says it has no cases, and health officials are checking one airport on one date.

The wire said: Mystery over source of Ebola infection in Nigeria fuels concern about spread of virus · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 24, 2026 14:57 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

An outbreak of Bundibugyo Ebola, first announced on 15 May in the Democratic Republic of Congo, has spread to Uganda, Kenya and now Nigeria. It has infected more than 9,500 people, according to The Guardian.

Why it matters

With no approved vaccine or treatment for this strain, containment depends on contact tracing, which depends on knowing where patients have been. A disputed travel history means officials may be tracing contacts from the wrong starting point.

Health officials across Africa are trying to work out how Nigeria's first Ebola patient in more than a decade became infected. The patient is the Ugandan-born wife of a Swiss diplomat based in Abuja. Nigeria's health minister, Muhammad Ali Pate, confirmed an imported case in the capital, and Nigeria's account of how it arrived has since come apart.

Uganda's Health Ministry said preliminary findings showed the woman had travelled to eastern Congo on business, then passed through Uganda without symptoms and returned to Nigeria. Switzerland's foreign ministry told Reuters the opposite: she and her husband did not go to Congo. Swiss authorities said she was believed to have been infected while travelling with her husband in Uganda, though DW noted that assessment has not been independently confirmed. The WHO and Africa CDC said they had no confirmed information placing her in Congo.

Translation: the one detail that made the case easy to explain has disappeared, and the case is now harder to explain. Uganda says it has no active cases. The WHO declared it Ebola-free in August, after cases in May and June. A Ugandan government spokesperson told Reuters that if there were cases, it would make them public. DW noted, drily, that zero confirmed active cases does not establish that every infection has been ruled out.

Attention has turned to Entebbe International Airport. Wessam Mankoula of Africa CDC said both the Nigerian patient and Kenya's first confirmed patient passed through Entebbe on 3 October. The Kenyan patient had travelled from Congo through Uganda and died in Nairobi on 5 October. Africa CDC is examining whether the two overlapped.

Scientists are cautious. The WHO's Catherine Smallwood said a link was possible but that officials must compare timing with the onset of symptoms. Marion Koopmans, a member of the WHO's Ebola emergency committee, called the theory "very speculative" without evidence of shared space. Random presence in a large airport, she said, is not a supported epidemiological link. Translation: being in the same building is not the same as being in the same outbreak.

The numbers explain the urgency. According to DW, symptoms appear two to 21 days after infection, and people are not considered infectious until symptoms start. The Nigerian woman developed symptoms on 7 October, visited a private clinic on 11 October, and was later confirmed positive. Nigeria has identified 44 contacts, including health workers and caregivers. Pate said the case remains contained and tracing continues.

Kenya has its own gap. Kenya's director-general for health, Patrick Amoth, told the Daily Nation that officials know a Ugandan contact of the dead patient by name, passport number and phone, but cannot reach him. He was reportedly using a South Sudanese number. DW stressed that being untraced does not mean he is infected. It does mean the tracing system knows everything about him except where he is.

The outbreak is the largest and deadliest in Congo's history and second globally only to the 2014-16 West African epidemic, which infected more than 28,600 people. The current strain has killed nearly half of those infected. Residents in Abuja told DW they were worried, one saying people were "worried about surviving" in a weak economy.

What to watch next is a timeline comparison of symptom onset and travel dates, and whether Uganda and Switzerland can be persuaded to publish the same itinerary.
