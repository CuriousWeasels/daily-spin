CoreWeave's Bonds Turn Distressed, and Nvidia Discovers Its Customers Are Also Its Financing Plan

After a week of falling shares and a pulled IPO, executives expect most neoclouds to vanish, and the chipmaker that props them up has the most exposure.

The wire said: CoreWeave’s debt is becoming the whole AI industry’s problem · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 20, 2026 15:53 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

CoreWeave rents out Nvidia-powered computing capacity to AI companies and has borrowed heavily to build it, using the chips themselves as collateral. Last week its bonds, its shares, and the mood around similar firms all took a beating.

Why it matters

If the AI buildout is financed by debt that gets costlier, the weakest builders may fail or be absorbed, which could slow the supply of AI computing and hit the chipmaker at the center of it. Anyone whose retirement fund holds Nvidia, Oracle, or Microsoft has an interest in how this ends.

CoreWeave had a bad week. According to The Verge, its bonds entered distressed territory by a common definition, meaning investors demanded 1,000 basis points more in yield than government bonds pay. Translation: a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so the market is asking for ten extra percentage points to lend to the company. The shares fell more than 20 percent from Tuesday's close, and Bloomberg reported the company lost a quarter of its market value.

The company owed more than $35 billion as of its second quarter, according to The Verge, partly because it pioneered using GPUs, the chips that run AI, as collateral for loans. Its business is selling computing power, and it lost more than a billion dollars in the first half of the year. This is a model in which the product is expensive, the collateral is the product, and both depreciate in the same direction.

Then came the interview. CoreWeave cofounder Brannin McBee told the Financial Times the company wants investment-grade debt so it can borrow from insurers, whose capital he called unlimited. Translation: investment-grade means a rating that says you are unlikely to default, and it unlocks cheaper money. The Verge notes the ambition is not unreasonable, since CoreWeave secured its first investment-grade chip loan earlier this year. Still, telling nervous bondholders you plan to find a much larger pool of money to borrow from is not the reassurance it may have felt like in the room.

The Verge's author is not ready to call a burst bubble. CoreWeave has $103.7 billion in remaining performance obligations, which is contracted revenue customers have promised to pay and can cancel only in limited circumstances. About 40 percent is due in 2028. Microsoft, OpenAI, and what is probably Nvidia generated almost three-quarters of second-quarter revenue. Microsoft and Nvidia are likely good for it, the author argues, partly because a CoreWeave collapse would be messy for everyone. OpenAI is the shakier name.

The Decoder, citing The Information, describes a wider reckoning. Two executives said only seven to ten of hundreds of AI cloud providers, known as neoclouds, will survive in a few years. Australian provider Firmus pulled its IPO, and Gartner predicts 80 percent of independent neoclouds will cease independent operations by 2030. Bernstein estimates only 35 percent of announced North American data center capacity will ever be built, per Bloomberg. Cirrascale CEO David Driggers expects asset managers and telecom firms to buy up neoclouds, one here, one there, which is how an industry shakeout is described by people who plan to be the shaker.

Nscale shows the strain. The Telegraph reported Microsoft pulled out of a Loughton, England deal to lease 23,000 Nvidia chips, at a site where utilities do not expect power until the 2030s. Nscale says the Microsoft relationship remains strong and long-term. Bloomberg reports investors are pushing to cut its target valuation from about $30 billion to between $16 billion and $19 billion. Over 40 percent of its $103 billion in contracted revenue comes from Microsoft, per The Telegraph.

Nvidia is the quiet party at risk. Customers outside the big cloud providers brought in $40 billion in the July quarter, 45 percent of its data center revenue and more than double the year before, according to The Decoder. The company backs neoclouds with equity investments, chip allocations, loan guarantees, and help finding sites with power. One executive said larger neoclouds rejected its first guarantee, which took a share of future revenue, so it now offers shorter terms for a smaller cut. Supporting the customers who buy your product is a sales strategy that works until the customers need supporting.

CoreWeave's CEO Michael Intrator blames smaller rivals for the turmoil, saying in a memo that they raise huge capital without its execution, revenue, or scale, according to Bloomberg. The longer-term worry is oversupply. Satya Nadella said in February 2025 that he expected a glut of compute starting in 2027 and that prices would fall. For the buyers of AI computing that is good news, and for the people who borrowed to sell it, it is the plot.

Oracle shares have fallen almost 10 percent over the same period, and its bonds sold off, The Verge says. Some people, it adds, are buying the dip. They may be right, though a dip bought with borrowed money is just a smaller version of the original problem.
