Anthropic Files to Go Public, Reports $80.7 Billion Loss Alongside $16.3 Billion in Sales

The Claude maker's S-1 shows revenue up from $1.2 billion to $16.3 billion in a year, a seven-founder voting bloc, and compute bills measured in hundreds of billions.

The wire said: Anthropic officially files for its public debut · TechCrunch AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 26, 2026 17:43 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Anthropic, the company behind the Claude AI models, confidentially filed with the SEC in June, and leaked drafts circulated for weeks. On Monday it made its S-1 prospectus public, planning to list on Nasdaq under the ticker ANT.

Why it matters

If the listing goes ahead, ordinary investors and index funds could own a piece of one of the most heavily scrutinized AI companies, but with little say over how it is run. The filing also shows how much money AI labs are committing to computing power years before they turn a steady profit.

Anthropic filed publicly on Monday to sell shares to the public, and it plans to trade as ANT on Nasdaq, according to TechCrunch and The Verge. The company has not said how much it wants to raise or at what price. Having already leaked most of its contents, the document arrived with the suspense of a surprise party where everyone has seen the guest list.

The headline numbers are large in both directions. Anthropic reported $16.3 billion in revenue for the first half of 2026, up from $1.2 billion a year earlier. It also reported a net loss of $80.7 billion. Translation: a net loss is what remains after every cost and accounting entry is counted, including ones that involve no actual cash leaving the building.

Those entries are the explanation. TechCrunch reported that the loss was largely driven by noncash adjustments tied to convertible debt, meaning loans that can turn into shares. The Verge, citing earlier Wall Street Journal reporting on the leaked drafts, said most of the loss came from accounting charges on notes the company issued to raise money. The operating loss, which tracks the actual business, was $693 million, down from $2.1 billion a year earlier. The company still says it may not achieve or sustain profitability.

The customer figures explain the revenue. As of September 30, Anthropic had more than 11,900 customers paying at least $100,000 a year, more than 1,900 paying at least $1 million, and about 250 paying $10 million or more. Net revenue retention was about 900% at the end of the third quarter. Translation: existing customers, taken together, were spending roughly nine times what they had spent before, which is the sort of figure that makes a finance department either delighted or nervous, depending on which side of the invoice it sits.

TechCrunch reported that the strategy is a familiar one: get a customer in through one door, then sell them the rest of the building. More than half of customers spending $100,000 or more now use both the Claude platform and a first-party app such as Claude Code or Cowork, up from about 30% at the end of last year. The top 5% of Claude Enterprise customers using Claude Code spent about 20 times as much per month as the average enterprise user who did not use it.

The bills are on the other side of the ledger. The Verge reported commitments including about $3.1 billion per month to SpaceX for access to Nvidia GPUs once planned capacity is delivered, totaling as much as $120.5 billion by 2029. Anthropic also listed $44.9 billion with Microsoft through 2033, at least $114.5 billion with Google through 2033, and $110 billion with Amazon over ten years. Those three are also its distribution partners, and the filing warns they increasingly see Anthropic as a competitor and may prioritize their own needs when allocating computing capacity.

Governance is the other point of contention. Seven co-founders, including Dario and Daniela Amodei, will form a Founder LLC holding 50.1% of voting power, which Anthropic says is meant to protect its AI safety mission. A Long-Term Benefit Trust, set up in 2023, will appoint most of the board. TechCrunch noted that public shareholders will have little practical control over a company that itself acknowledges its technology poses catastrophic or existential risks to humanity.

The risk factors section is candid. It mentions the possibility that AI models develop forms of sentience, AI-specific taxes, and unfavorable outcomes in copyright disputes over training data. It also says no single insurer, and potentially not the insurance industry in aggregate, could cover a catastrophic AI incident. Dario Amodei's 2025 compensation was nearly $18 million, and Daniela Amodei received $16.4 million, per the filing.

What to watch next is the price range and the size of the raise, which the filing leaves blank. Investors will then decide how much to pay for a company that has told them, in writing, about both the upside and the uninsurable downside.
