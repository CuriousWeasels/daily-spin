OpenAI Claims AI Disproved Core Computing Assumption, Asks Humans to Please Check

Three claimed breakthroughs in math, computer science and physics arrive with a new release method: outside 'shepherds' to work out whether any of it is true.

The wire said: OpenAI announces three big science discoveries and a new way to work with academics · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 28, 2026 02:42 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Less than a month after dumping hundreds of manuscripts it said solved long-standing math questions, OpenAI released three more claimed results and recruited outside experts to examine them. The headline claim is that the Exponential Time Hypothesis, a foundational belief in computer science, is false.

Why it matters

If the proof holds, hundreds of papers lose a foundation, and researchers must rethink how hard certain problems really are. Whether anyone's chips, software or encryption benefits, or suffers, is unknown.

OpenAI said it has produced, with its internal models, proofs in three fields: a wave-equation conjecture in math, a refutation of the Exponential Time Hypothesis in computer science, and an existence proof for certain quantum field theories in physics. According to The Verge and The Decoder, the company calls the manuscripts a kind of raw research data. This is a candid description, since the usual term for an unchecked result is 'claim.'

The computer science result is the one drawing attention. The Exponential Time Hypothesis, or ETH, says that solving a certain class of logic puzzles (SAT, in which you try to make a formula come out true) takes time that grows exponentially as variables are added. Translation: there is no clever shortcut, and each new variable roughly doubles the work. The Decoder notes that 100 variables already yields a 31-digit number of combinations. OpenAI says its algorithm counts solutions in subexponential time, which would refute the hypothesis.

Hundreds of papers in graph theory, databases, computational biology and geometry rely on the ETH, according to The Decoder. If it falls, their results would not automatically be wrong, only no longer proven. This is the academic equivalent of learning the floor beneath your office was a rumor.

The new part is the process. After the October release, which MIT professor Ryan Williams once called a 'DDOS attack on mathematicians,' TechCrunch reported, OpenAI has appointed 'shepherds.' Williams, along with UC San Diego's Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi, who proposed the ETH in 1999, will examine and explain the proof. Williams wrote that the group has signed no nondisclosure agreements and works unpaid. Their open letter says their involvement is not an endorsement. OpenAI has also agreed to fund a conference.

The math result is handled differently. OpenAI says its model proved Sogge's local smoothing conjecture in every dimension, which would also settle the Kakeya and Stein restriction conjectures. No group is shepherding it. On an outside expert's advice, OpenAI published the output and a Lean proof and left researchers to sort it out. Translation: Lean is software that checks each logical step, though experts must still confirm the statement it checked is the one that was intended.

For physics, a group led by David Gross will shepherd a claimed rigorous existence proof of certain quantum field theories, including QCD. The full material has not been released.

There is a complication. The Verge reports that OpenAI's own math advisory panel said a day earlier that AI companies should not adopt the practice of giving selected people early access, citing unequal access, borrowed reputation and biased selection. OpenAI released the shepherded results anyway, while acknowledging the approach could have its own issues. The panel preferred public repositories, Lean proofs, funding for those doing the understanding, and disclosure of how problems were chosen.

TechCrunch adds a rivalry. An Anthropic mathematician suggested OpenAI relied on pre-existing work, and hours later Anthropic released its own refutation of the ETH. Williams said parts of the proof are routine, but two or three look 'alien,' and that he asked whether any cryptography is now insecure. OpenAI reportedly said it was reasonably confident there were no breaks.

On practical payoff, Williams is candid. He said practical SAT solving might see serious progress within 12 months, 'or we may see nothing of the sort.' Real-world solvers already handle formulas with millions of variables, The Decoder notes, because they are easier than the worst case.

The proofs are public, the checkers are unpaid, and the conference is funded. Whether the hypothesis is false will now be settled the old-fashioned way, by humans reading carefully, which is the one step the announcement could not automate.
