Israel Votes, Produces Two Equal Blocs and a Kingmaker Both Swore Not to Call

Exit polls project 51 seats each for Netanyahu's bloc and Eisenkot's opposition, short of the 61 needed, with Arab-led parties on 18 seats and record turnout.

The wire said: Israel election results: No clear winner projected, what’s next? · Al Jazeera

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 28, 2026 02:42 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Israel held a parliamentary election on Tuesday with turnout of 77.7 percent, the highest since 1999. Exit polls show Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's Likud-led coalition and an opposition bloc led by former military chief Gadi Eisenkot tied, according to Al Jazeera.

Why it matters

A tie means weeks or months of coalition bargaining, possibly another election, with Netanyahu staying on as caretaker in the meantime. Whether a government forms may depend on parties that both main leaders have publicly ruled out working with.

Israelis voted on Tuesday, and according to exit polls released by the major television networks, they split almost perfectly in half. Al Jazeera reported that the coalition led by Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's Likud and the opposition bloc led by Gadi Eisenkot's Yashar party were each projected to win 51 of the Knesset's 120 seats. A majority requires 61. Arithmetic, as usual, remains undefeated.

Translation: nobody won, but two groups of people are free to say they did. Eisenkot said the results show a lack of confidence in Netanyahu, while opposition leader Yair Lapid called it a crushing defeat for the prime minister's bloc. Foreign Minister Gideon Saar, of Likud, called the celebrations premature and noted that Likud is the largest party in Israel.

The remaining 18 projected seats belong to Arab-led parties, chiefly the Joint List and Ra'am, which sit outside both blocs. Al Jazeera reported this would be a record, driven by turnout in Palestinian-Israeli communities of around 80 percent, according to Israel's Channel 12. Palestinian citizens make up about 21 percent of the population and have traditionally voted at lower rates than Jewish Israelis.

This makes them kingmakers, meaning the people whose votes decide who governs. There is one complication: both Netanyahu and Eisenkot have ruled out partnering with them, citing a lack of shared values. Analyst Alon Pinkas told Al Jazeera there is a price to pay, because Arab parties cannot be kept outside and only courted when their votes are needed. Ra'am leader Mansour Abbas put it more briefly: with the current numbers, there is no government.

The campaign itself did not lower the temperature. Witnesses told Al Jazeera that cars flying Likud flags drove through Jerusalem broadcasting the Islamic call to prayer and urging supporters to vote because Arabs were voting. Netanyahu posted images of long queues in Arab communities and warned of a government of Abbas and Ahmad Tibi. Arab politicians denounced the tactic as racist and inflammatory. Whether it motivated Likud voters is not yet clear, though it evidently motivated others.

What happens next is procedural but long. After final results on November 4, President Isaac Herzog consults party leaders and picks whoever he thinks can build a coalition, not necessarily the largest party's leader. That person gets 28 days, extendable by 14. If they fail, Herzog can try another candidate for 28 days, then the Knesset can nominate a third with 14 days. After that, the Knesset dissolves and elections must be called within 90 days, with Netanyahu's government serving as caretaker.

Israelis know this script. Gridlock produced three elections between April 2019 and March 2020, ending in a Netanyahu-Gantz rotation deal that collapsed a year later over a budget. Analyst Ehab Jabareen cautioned against declaring either a Netanyahu victory or the end of his career, noting that Netanyahu, prime minister for nearly 19 of the past 30 years, could simply remain as caretaker.

Watch the final tally and Herzog's choice. Exit polls are not results, and a seat or two moving in either direction would change who is negotiating. If not, expect a long autumn of phone calls to people who were promised, in public, that nobody would be calling.
