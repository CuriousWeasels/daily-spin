U.S. Deficit Hits $2 Trillion as Treasury Tells IMF to Check Its Own Homework

With 10-year yields above 5% and debt past $40 trillion, the Treasury's answer to a warning about growth is a list of the IMF's old forecasting errors.

The wire said: Analysis: Why a $2 trillion deficit and 5% Treasury yields are becoming America's biggest economic challenge · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 15, 2026 14:38 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

U.S. government debt has passed $40 trillion, the deficit is running near $2 trillion, and long-term Treasury yields have climbed above 5% since the war with Iran began on Feb. 28. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent says the U.S. can grow its way out, and the IMF says it cannot.

Why it matters

The 10-year Treasury yield sets the tone for mortgage rates, which are around 7.5%, and for what the government pays to borrow. Higher rates make the deficit harder to shrink, and the politics for fixing it look stuck for at least two years.

The U.S. federal deficit for fiscal 2026 will come in near $2 trillion, CNBC reported, and the Treasury is due to publish the official figure in the next few days. The Congressional Budget Office estimated $1.993 trillion, which works out to roughly 6.0% to 6.2% of GDP. Bessent has said the target should be below 4% by the end of President Trump's term, which CNBC describes as something the country has almost no chance of hitting.

The bond market has noticed. The 10-year Treasury yield closed Wednesday at 5.19%, up 123 basis points since the end of February. Translation: a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so lenders now demand about 1.2 percentage points more than they did before the war. Official data put it at 5.21% on Oct. 13, against a Fed funds rate of 3.88%.

CNBC lists several causes. The Iran war pushed up oil and diesel prices, which makes bond traders worry that the Federal Reserve will raise rates. The government keeps issuing debt, and companies are issuing hundreds of billions more to build artificial intelligence infrastructure. Translation: the Treasury is no longer the only borrower asking for money, and investors can pick among borrowers.

Ordinary people feel this through mortgages. The average 30-year fixed rate is hovering around 7.5%, and Redfin said pending home sales fell 4% in September, the biggest monthly drop in three years. Mortgage rates follow the 10-year yield, so the Treasury's borrowing problem becomes a household's down-payment problem.

The debt arithmetic gets worse as rates rise. The CBO estimates that rates a half-point above its forecast would add $1.9 trillion to deficits over 10 years, and 1.5 points above would add $6 trillion. The agency expects the 10-year to average 4.3%, against 5.3% today, so the good scenario requires yields to fall by a full point.

Bessent's answer is growth. 'We have to grow our way out of it,' he said in Dallas last month, and he and others point to AI as the engine. The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget calculates that growth alone would need a decade of 7.2% annual expansion to balance the budget. Second-quarter GDP was just revised up to 2.2% annualized, so the plan requires more than tripling it and keeping it there.

The IMF's Kristalina Georgieva said in Bangkok that strong U.S. growth would not be enough to put debt on a sustainable path. David Zervos, the newly appointed counselor to the Treasury Secretary, replied by email that readers should remember 'how messy their forecasts have been.' He cited 2008, Southeast Asia in 1997, Russia in 2022 and Argentina. Translation: the Treasury is not arguing with the arithmetic, only with the person who did it.

Politics offers little help. Republicans are widely forecast to lose at least the House in the Nov. 3 midterms, and Trump has pledged $5,000 checks to every adult citizen if they sweep. Rep. Hakeem Jeffries, the likely next House speaker, wants to reverse $1 trillion in healthcare cuts. CNBC notes that divided government sometimes produces compromises, but the two sides start far apart.

Across the Atlantic, France offers a preview of how this goes. Its parliament begins debating the 2027 budget Tuesday, its borrowing costs are the highest in nearly 25 years, and student protests are making consolidation unpopular. INSEAD's Antonio Fatas says the ECB 'will not save a country that cannot save itself.' CNBC stresses that high borrowing costs are not yet a crisis, since higher yields also attract buyers to Treasuries.

Markets, for now, are calm: the S&P 500 stood at 7,718.94 on Oct. 14 and the VIX at 16.87. The kicker is that investors are relaxed about a problem that, by CNBC's account, only politics can fix, and politics is the one asset nobody is currently issuing.
