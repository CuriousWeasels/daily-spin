Missile Strike Kills Over 50 Yemeni Recruits as Both Sides Dispute What Camp Was For

Local authorities say a Houthi attack on a Shabwa training centre killed 53 and wounded more than 200, while the Houthis call it a strike on Saudi forces.

The wire said: Houthi missile strike on Yemen camp kills 50 security recruits, local authorities say · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 19, 2026 17:49 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Yemen's civil war began in 2014 and drew in a Saudi-led coalition in 2015. An informal ceasefire of about four years began to unravel in July, and fighting has intensified since.

Why it matters

The strike is among the deadliest of the recent escalation and shows an informal truce giving way to a wider fight over Red Sea territory and Saudi targets. Civilians and trainees keep paying for a war with no agreed rulebook.

More than 50 security personnel were killed and about 200 injured when Houthi missiles hit a camp in Yemen's central Shabwa province, local authorities say, according to the BBC. A spokesman for the Saudi-backed Yemeni armed forces said three ballistic missiles struck al-Alam camp near Ataq city, a training centre for recruits to the security forces and police. Shabwa's media office put the preliminary toll at 53 dead and more than 200 wounded.

The deputy governor, Fahad al-Khalifi, told AFP that hospitals were overwhelmed and had to send the most seriously wounded to Hadramout and Aden. Some are in critical condition. The BBC noted that a video posted online appeared to show several bodies on the ground.

Then came the part where each side describes the same building differently. Information Minister Moammar al-Eryani called the attack a war crime, arguing that a training centre for internal security forces is not a military target. The Houthis' military spokesman, Yahya Sarea, said the missiles hit a gathering of "Saudi enemy" forces at a camp he said was run by Saudi officers. Translation: one side says it was a police academy, the other says it was a Saudi base, and the missiles did not wait for the dispute to be settled. There was no immediate comment from Saudi authorities.

Rashad al-Alimi, head of Yemen's Presidential Leadership Council, condemned a "criminal attack" and promised a "firm response." Mr Eryani also accused the Houthis of trying to manufacture a media victory to cover setbacks on the battlefield. Those are claims from one party to the war, and the BBC reported them as such.

The context is a conflict that has been heating up. In early October, pro-government forces launched an offensive to retake Houthi-held territory in western Yemen. With Saudi-led air support, they regained ground on the Red Sea coast overlooking the Bab al-Mandab Strait, which the Houthis had seized in a surprise assault in September. Fighting continues there, around besieged Taiz, and in Marib and al-Jawf.

The Houthis have answered Saudi air strikes with missiles and drones aimed at airports, energy facilities and military bases in Saudi Arabia. At least 19 people have been killed in Houthi attacks on Saudi airports since the start of October, the BBC reported. Houthi-run media said a Saudi air strike on a home in Saada killed at least 10 people on Sunday, many of them women and children. The Houthi-run health ministry says 141 people have died in Saudi strikes over seven weeks, while the government's health ministry says at least 1,282 have been killed across Yemen since 6 August.

The informal ceasefire began to fray in July, when the Houthis declared a "maritime embargo" on Saudi Arabia, citing a Saudi blockade of their territory and an air strike on Sanaa airport. Informal ceasefires, it turns out, have the structural weakness of being informal. The next thing to watch is what a "firm response" turns out to mean, and whether anyone is left to enforce the word ceasefire.
