Anthropic Stops Hiring Juniors as Its AI Works Six Times the Hours and Humans Attend More Meetings

An internal report says junior hires in technical roles fell from 27 percent to 9 percent, while meeting time per employee rose 20 percent.

The wire said: Anthropic has nearly stopped hiring junior staff because AI agents now work six times as many hours · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 19, 2026 01:58 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Anthropic, the maker of the Claude AI models, published an internal study on how AI has changed its own workforce over the past year. It describes fewer entry-level hires, higher output across departments, and more time spent in meetings.

Why it matters

If other firms copy the pattern, the entry-level jobs where professionals learn their trade could shrink, leaving a shortage of experienced people years later. The study's authors concede that Anthropic is an unusual company, so the results may not carry over to the wider economy.

Anthropic is hiring far fewer junior employees in technical roles, according to an internal study the company published. The Decoder and TechCrunch both reported that the junior share of technical positions fell from 21 percent to 14 percent since early 2025. The junior share of new technical hires fell more sharply, from 27 percent to 9 percent. A company that builds machines to do work has, by its own account, found that it needs fewer beginners.

Anthropic points to its AI agents as the reason. According to The Decoder, by August 2026 those agents were logging six times as many work hours as human employees. Translation: the software now puts in the overtime, and it doesn't ask for a title change. Junior hiring dropped most in roles where Claude handles the most complex work, which is where a junior employee would once have been handed the tedious parts.

The productivity numbers are the kind that appear in a pitch deck. Engineers shipped 5.1 times as many code commits, data scientists ran 3.1 times as many queries, and the legal team processed 3.1 times as many contracts. Sales closed 1.7 times as many deals, recruiters hired 1.4 times as many people, and accounting handled 1.4 times as many invoices. TechCrunch notes that the company itself admits more code does not necessarily mean better code, though it says it has found no sign that quality is slipping.

The humans who remain have found a new job, which is supervising. In an April 2026 survey, 20 percent of employees said their work had shifted from doing work to managing Claudes. One engineer compared it to babysitting, saying every child brings concerns that demand equal attention. The report also describes human attention and judgement as a bottleneck, because Claude produces a deluge of text that someone must read. Translation: the machine writes faster than anyone can read, and reading has become the scarce resource.

Meetings have grown to fill the gap. Based on calendar data, meeting time per employee is up 20 percent from a year ago, and up 16 percent for technical staff. Anthropic attributes this to human trust and accountability, with employees describing face-to-face coordination as indispensable. It is a notable finding that the more work is automated, the more the remaining work consists of people getting together to agree on what the automation did.

The study is careful about its limits. It admits Anthropic is an extremely AI-heavy software company, that rapid growth and voluntary adoption could skew the numbers, and, according to The Decoder, that the wider economy has not yet shown measurable productivity gains from AI. Technical teams also shrank as a share of the workforce, from 61 percent to 56 percent. Anthropic attributes that to business expansion and to coding tools that let non-technical staff build software.

The longer-term worry comes from researcher Nolan Lovett of the NATO Special Operations University, who calls it a tragedy of the cognitive commons. Each company gains the efficiency from cutting entry-level roles, while the cost, a shortage of experienced experts, is spread across everyone. Lovett argues the effect will show up between 2030 and 2045, because today's experts trained 5 to 20 years ago. The Decoder adds that Anthropic has suggested programmers and call center workers may eventually need to become electricians or nurses, which would make the shortage someone else's problem.

What to watch next is whether any company with a less AI-saturated workforce reports the same pattern. Until then, the study is a detailed account of one very unusual office, where the new hires are software and the veterans spend more time in meetings.
