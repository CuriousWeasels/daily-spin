Musk Confirms He Is Weighing Merger of Musk's Two Largest Companies, Musk Not on Committee

Tesla ($1.5 trillion) and SpaceX ($2 trillion) could form the largest merger ever, pending special committees, a shareholder vote, and Musk's insistence he is merely a conflicted bystander.

The wire said: Elon Musk confirms he is considering merging Tesla and SpaceX · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 22, 2026 02:03 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Elon Musk said on a SpaceX earnings call that special committees at Tesla and SpaceX are examining a combination of the two companies. SpaceX has already absorbed xAI and X and listed on the stock market in June.

Why it matters

Tesla shareholders would likely own a smaller slice of a larger, more complicated business, and Musk's wealth and voting control would be concentrated further in one stock. Car buyers are unlikely to notice much soon, though analysts suggest cars may rank lower among the combined company's priorities.

Elon Musk said on Tuesday that he is actively considering merging Tesla and SpaceX, according to the BBC. Asked if it would be a good idea, he said: "Yes." He then added that nothing has been agreed, which is the standard way to announce something you would like to happen.

The mechanics are as follows. Special committees have been formed at both companies to examine the idea. Translation: a special committee is a group of board members meant to negotiate for shareholders when the person on the other side of the table is also the person on their own side of the table. Musk, chief executive of both firms, said he is not on either one and called himself a "conflicted party."

The scale is considerable. The BBC reports Tesla is valued at roughly $1.5 trillion and SpaceX at $2 trillion, which would make the combination the largest merger ever. SpaceX shares rose 8% in after-hours trading on the comments, and Tesla rose 2%. Markets, it seems, price in a thing that has not been agreed with great confidence.

Musk himself described discussing the subject as "tap dancing through a minefield." On Tesla's earnings call the next day, he said he had nothing more to add, then repeated the line. He also said calling the merger a certainty would be "overstating" things. Analyst David Nicholson of The Futurum Group was less restrained, putting the odds at "100%" and calling it "just a question of when."

The approval step is shareholders. At least half of Tesla's would need to back a deal, which Musk called "quite a high hurdle." The BBC notes that Tesla investors last year approved a pay package for Musk valued at around $1tn, so the hurdle has been cleared before, in the sense that a hurdle is cleared when everyone agrees to lower it.

The logic offered is industrial. Musk said the companies overlap on chips, artificial intelligence and software, and the two already run a joint chip-making effort called Terafab. SpaceX's own numbers show where the weight lies: Starlink revenue rose to $5.2bn in the quarter to 30 September from $3bn, but cloud computing capacity sold to firms such as Anthropic and Google brought in $7.5bn, up from $813m. For the year's first nine months SpaceX lost $1.1bn on $27bn of revenue, though it reported a $4.4bn profit in the latest quarter.

For Tesla owners, experts told the BBC to expect little near-term change. Nicholson said vehicles seem almost like "an afterthought" next to the technology tie-up. Analyst Dan Sheehan of Telos Wealth Advisors asked how much priority cars would get inside a company increasingly driven by AI infrastructure. Tesla's third-quarter sales were $28.05 billion against $28.1 billion a year earlier, and BYD overtook it as the world's largest EV maker in January.

Musk would be the clearest beneficiary. The BBC reports a merger would give him more voting control than he has at Tesla today, which the special committee must weigh. Sheehan said the deal "doesn't add to Musk's wealth" but concentrates it in one stock. Tesla shareholders, he noted, would probably hold a "smaller slice of a much larger and more complex business," with no real precedent for how to price it.

Complications remain. SpaceX's Starshield business faces scrutiny over claims that Ukraine's network access was limited, which Trump denied ordering, while Musk suggested the US government may have been behind it. Analysts also flagged possible regulatory pressure if Democrats gain seats in next month's midterms. Watch the committees: they are the only participants in this story who have not yet said yes.
