French Supermarket Fridges Hacked Into Ovens, Because Passwords Were Basically the Date

An estimated 700 French stores lost perishables and in some cases suffered electrical fires after internet-connected refrigeration controllers with known flaws were compromised, according to Ars Technica.

The wire said: Fridge hack costing millions and sparking fires stemmed from poor security · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 16, 2026 01:49 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Earlier this week, attackers broke into the remote-administration controllers that run store-wide refrigeration systems at an estimated 700 French supermarkets. They then triggered defrosters until the fridges and freezers heated up.

Why it matters

Industrial equipment that keeps food cold is now reachable from the internet, and unpatched flaws can turn a software problem into spoiled groceries and fires. Shoppers and retailers both end up paying for security that vendors and operators skipped.

An estimated 700 supermarkets across France had their refrigeration systems malfunction this week after a mass hack, Ars Technica reported. The result was untold tons of spoiled perishables and, in some cases, fires. The outlet's assessment of the cause was blunt: basic cybersecurity would have prevented it.

French authorities say the targets were internet-connected controllers that let technicians remotely manage the refrigerators and freezers across an entire store. Translation: one login can control every cold thing in the building, which is convenient for technicians and, it turns out, for strangers. The unknown attackers ran the defrosters long enough that temperatures reached as high as 50° Celsius (122° Fahrenheit). A freezer is not designed to be a sauna, and several electrical systems reportedly caught fire.

According to Ars Technica, three controller models were involved, sold by Danfoss of Denmark and by Dixell and its US parent company Copeland. Advisories and public research reports show that all three contained critical vulnerabilities letting attackers log in remotely with administrator-level rights. Translation: the door was not merely unlocked, it came with a staff badge.

The Danfoss flaw is a good illustration. Until recently, these controllers used a credential that changes each day, which sounds rigorous until you learn how it works. The password is generated by the publicly available firmware based on the date. Anyone willing to study the code can compute it for any device that has not been updated, and the daily password is identical across models in hundreds or thousands of stores. It is a secret in the same sense that today's date is a secret.

Copeland and Dixell controllers have their own problem, according to the report. A raft of critical vulnerabilities fixed in just the past few months gave similar remote access. The word fixed carries weight there, because a patch only protects a store once someone installs it. The attack worked on devices that had not been brought up to date.

The incentives are familiar. Manufacturers ship remote access because customers want it, stores connect the controllers because technicians prefer not to drive out at night, and updating a refrigeration controller ranks below almost everything else on a store manager's day. The cost of that arrangement arrives later, as a warm aisle of yogurt and a scorched electrical panel. Nobody budgets for that line item until it is lit.

What to watch next is whether stores patch, whether the affected vendors face questions about the date-based password, and whether anyone attributes the attack. In the meantime, the lesson for the cold chain is that the cold part is now optional for anyone who can read firmware.
