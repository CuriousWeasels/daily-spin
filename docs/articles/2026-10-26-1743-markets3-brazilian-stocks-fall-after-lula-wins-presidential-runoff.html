Brazil Markets Fall Sharply After Lula Wins Fourth Term, Surprising Investors Who Had Priced In Someone Else

Lula beat Flávio Bolsonaro 51.8% to 48.2%, the real fell 10.5%, and the Treasury cancelled two bond auctions rather than watch them happen.

The wire said: Brazilian stocks rocked as Lula wins presidential election, Bolsonaro reportedly refuses to concede · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 26, 2026 17:43 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva won Brazil's presidential runoff after trailing in the first round, according to CNBC. Flávio Bolsonaro reportedly refused to concede, and Brazilian assets sold off.

Why it matters

Brazil's debt load and high interest rates mean the market's reaction affects borrowing costs for the government and for companies. Bolsonaro's refusal to concede adds political risk to an already fiscal one.

Brazil's Superior Electoral Court said Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva won the presidential runoff with 51.8% of valid votes to Senator Flávio Bolsonaro's 48.2%, with nearly all ballots counted, CNBC reported. Lula had lost the first round three weeks earlier, and no candidate had come back from that position in a final runoff since direct elections resumed in 1989. Markets, which had apparently treated the first round as a final answer, responded with the composure of a man who has just been told the meeting was moved up.

The numbers were not subtle. CNBC reported that the iShares MSCI Brazil ETF was down 25% at 10:30 a.m. ET, and trading on Brazil's stock exchange was halted by an automatic circuit breaker. Translation: a circuit breaker is a pre-agreed pause that forces everyone to stop selling and think, which in practice gives them time to think about selling more. Among U.S.-listed names, Itaú Unibanco fell 20%, Petrobras 16%, Ambev 10.3%, Embraer 5% and Vale 1.2%. The Brazilian real dropped 10.5% against the dollar to its lowest since December 2025, according to LSEG data cited by CNBC.

The Brazilian Treasury then cancelled auctions of inflation-linked and fixed-rate bonds scheduled for Oct. 27 and Oct. 29. Its notice said it would keep monitoring market conditions to ensure the proper functioning of the bond market. Translation: a government that sells debt would prefer not to do so on a day when buyers are holding the price they want. A separate auction of Treasury Financial Bills, scheduled for Oct. 27, is still going ahead.

The reason for the fear is fiscal. ING's Chris Turner said a 7-8% budget deficit and 70% net debt-to-GDP leave Brazil vulnerable in a global bond sell-off, and that investors would have to contend with campaign pledges such as a shorter working week without pay cuts. Citi cited concern about fiscal policy, state-owned enterprises and the reform agenda, and warned that rates staying higher for longer would hurt banks' asset quality. That explains why a bank like Itaú fell harder than a miner that sells to the world.

Analysts did not all predict disaster. The Atlantic Council's Alejo Czerwonko expects a continued gradual approach to stabilizing debt, and Goldman Sachs expects modest spending-containment measures and higher taxes, with major structural reforms unlikely. Translation: the base case is slow, imperfect budgeting, which is not a crisis but is also not what the market had paid for. Lula will also face a Congress where Bolsonaro's Liberal Party holds the largest bench in decades.

Then there is the politics. Reuters reported that Bolsonaro refused to concede overnight and said he would seek to annul the result, and called a justice's two-hour extension of voting unacceptable. CNBC noted that Lula's campaign had alleged voter suppression through suspended public transport, while Bolsonaro's campaign had also asked for more time because of rains that closed polling stations in the south. Both sides, in other words, wanted longer voting hours and only one is upset about getting them.

Markets will now watch how quickly Bolsonaro accepts the outcome, as ING's Turner put it, or allows the constitution to run its course. Leaders across Latin America largely congratulated Lula, though the result also blocks a rightward shift that Donald Trump had backed. Lula is due to govern from 2027 to 2030. The first test comes sooner: whether the Treasury can sell bonds again at a price it likes.
