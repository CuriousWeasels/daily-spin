Alberta Votes 3 to 1 to Remain in Canada, Separatists Pivot to Plan B: Blaming Premier

With about 76% backing unity in unofficial results, the referendum settled the province's question and opened a more interesting one inside the governing party.

The wire said: Alberta voters overwhelmingly reject plan to leave Canada in referendum · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 21, 2026 02:09 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Alberta, Canada's oil-rich western province, held a referendum on Monday with 10 questions, one asking whether to begin a legal process that could lead to a second vote on separation. Prime Minister Mark Carney and Premier Danielle Smith both campaigned for the province to stay, while Canada is in a trade war with the United States.

Why it matters

Canada's richest energy province just signaled it is staying, which gives Carney political room for pipelines and trade diversification while the US still buys about 70% of Canadian exports. The other nine questions, still being counted, could start new bargaining with Ottawa and other provinces.

Albertans voted overwhelmingly to remain in Canada. With nearly 1.9 million votes counted, about 76% backed staying and 24% backed beginning a process toward separation, according to unofficial Elections Alberta results reported by The Guardian. Translation: a referendum meant to measure how much Albertans wanted out measured it at roughly one in four.

Technically, the question was not whether to leave. It asked whether to begin a legal process that could lead to a second vote. Separatists were frustrated by that distinction, The Guardian noted, though it proved a fine way to lose twice in one sentence.

Carney, who grew up in Edmonton, called it a clear choice for unity and said it would let the country speed up trade diversification and major infrastructure. He also spoke of making Canada a global energy powerhouse with Alberta at its centre. The BBC observed that his political and economic priorities now seem to outweigh his earlier green credentials, a polite way of saying the former UN climate envoy has discovered pipelines.

The Trump factor loomed over everything. The BBC reported that some prominent separatist leaders admitted travelling to Washington three times last year to meet US State Department officials and outline what an independent Alberta could offer, including a possible oil pipeline through the US to the Pacific. On referendum day itself, Trump told a Kansas rally he would let Canadians keep hockey and their anthem, then added: "We're only kidding." Canadians, the BBC noted, did not find it funny.

Pollster David Coletto told The Guardian that many Albertans saw the vote as a distraction, with a general sadness about energy spent on it rather than on threats like the Trump administration. His summary of the mood: the marriage may be rocky, but very few want it to end. A radio host put the price of the exercise at $130 million and said someone owes Albertans that money.

The separatists, naturally, found a silver lining. Keith Wilson said at least a quarter of Albertans think the province needs better treatment, which is one way to describe a 24% result. David Parker was blunter, calling it bleak and a defeat, though he said it changes nothing about Albertans' circumstances.

The more consequential fight may be within Smith's United Conservative Party. She owes her job in large part to separatists who ousted her predecessor, Jason Kenney, and has tried to placate both camps. Now Jeffrey Rath told Global News that independence will stay a dream while the "avowed federalist" Smith remains in power, and a separatist leader said he would push for a special general meeting to remove her. Translation: having lost the vote against Canada, the movement will try the vote against its own premier.

Not everyone is calling it settled. Calgary mayor Jeromy Farkas said no result will permanently put the matter to bed, since separatists see it as a long-term project. Political scientist David Moscrop told the BBC that unity looks fine now, but asked what happens when Trump is gone and Canada loses a single bombastic target to rally against.

Counting on the other nine questions, covering immigration, healthcare, education and provincial powers, could take days. Watch those results. A province that just voted to stay may now start listing what it expects in return.
