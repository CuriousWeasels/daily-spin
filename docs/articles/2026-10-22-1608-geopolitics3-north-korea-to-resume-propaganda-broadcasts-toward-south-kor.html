North Korea Prepares to Restart Border Loudspeakers, Reviving Sixteen-Month Pause in Shouting

Kim Yo-jong says the speakers will be ready within days along the whole border, while Seoul says any response would be 'proportional' but undecided.

The wire said: North Korea says it will resume propaganda broadcasts across border into South Korea · NPR World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 22, 2026 16:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

North and South Korea have long blasted propaganda across their border, and the South halted its broadcasts in June 2025 to ease tensions. Relations have worsened since a September landmine blast injured three South Korean soldiers.

Why it matters

Loudspeaker wars are cheap, but they are a reliable gauge of how bad relations are, and they raise the odds of a misjudged reaction along one of the most heavily armed borders on earth. For South Korean border residents, it may also mean noise.

North Korea said it will restart propaganda loudspeakers aimed at South Korea, with Kim Yo-jong, sister of leader Kim Jong-un, saying they would be ready within days along the entire border. According to The Guardian, the statement ran on state news agency KCNA on Thursday, a day after she said the speakers would operate along the whole frontier from some time in October. It is the diplomatic equivalent of announcing that you will be turning the stereo back on, directed at neighbors you have just described as hostile.

The practice has a long history. Both countries have used giant loudspeakers to broadcast across the border, the Guardian noted, with the North in recent years favoring haunting bestial howls, and the South choosing K-pop and news criticizing Pyongyang. Translation: psychological warfare, in which one side tries to demoralize the other with animal noises and the other responds with pop music. Each side appears to believe it has the stronger material.

If the broadcasts resume, they would be the first loudspeaker exchange in 16 months. South Korean president Lee Jae Myung suspended the Southern broadcasts days into his presidency in June 2025 to ease tensions, and the military dismantled its speakers that August. That leaves the South in the awkward position of having disarmed unilaterally in a contest where the other side kept its equipment, and apparently did maintenance on it.

Indeed, Seoul's joint chiefs of staff said they had observed routine maintenance on the North's loudspeakers before Kim Yo-jong's statements. A defence ministry spokesperson said that if the North goes ahead, 'naturally there would have to be a proportional measure', while making clear no decision had been made. Translation: we will respond in kind, though we have not yet worked out with what, since we took down our speakers.

The backdrop is grim. Tensions have risen since September, when a landmine blast south of the military demarcation line injured three South Korean soldiers, two seriously. Seoul and the UN command, which oversees the 1953 armistice that halted but did not end the Korean war, concluded it was a North Korean anti-personnel mine and an armistice violation. Pyongyang denied responsibility and called it a 'self-staged farce'.

Meanwhile the North keeps building. Since Kim Jong-un declared in December 2023 that the two Koreas were 'two states hostile to each other', it has laid mines, strung barbed wire and erected anti-tank barriers. On Wednesday Kim Yo-jong toured fortification points with Pak Jong-chon, a marshal and adviser to the defence ministry, and said the border had been 'clearly blocked and turned into a fortress more thoroughly'. She added that her brother wants it to be 'a permanent line never to be crossed'.

Seoul says North Korean troops have repeatedly crossed the 250km demarcation line during that construction, which is an interesting way to build a line never to be crossed. South Korean soldiers have answered with loudspeaker warnings and warning shots, which the North calls provocations by 'armed rogues'. So the loudspeakers, in a sense, already exist in limited form. The new plan simply gives them a larger audience and a playlist.

What to watch: whether Seoul reinstalls its own speakers, and what 'proportional' turns out to mean. If the answer involves K-pop, the North has at least been warned.
