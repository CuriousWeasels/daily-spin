Inflation Hits 3.7% as Gasoline Does Most of the Heavy Lifting

September consumer prices rose 0.6% on the month, with a 9.1% jump in gasoline supplying about half the increase and the Fed edging toward another rate hike.

The wire said: Consumer prices rose 3.7% annually in September as gas prices skyrocket (CNBC Economy)

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & Economy Oct 15, 2026 12:56 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Inflation has run above the Federal Reserve's 2% target since March 2021, and it spiked again this year after U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran began a war in late February. The Fed raised rates in September for the first time in more than three years.

Why it matters

Prices are rising faster than wages, so the average household is losing purchasing power, and higher rates mean borrowing costs may climb as well. With midterm elections next month, the cost of living is also a political problem.

The consumer price index rose a seasonally adjusted 0.6% in September, putting the 12-month rate at 3.7%, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Wednesday, according to CNBC. Economists surveyed by Dow Jones had expected 0.6% and 3.6%, so the surprise was one-tenth of a point, which in inflation terms counts as a plot twist. August's annual rate was 3.4%.

Translation: the consumer price index is a basket of things Americans buy, priced repeatedly, and then blamed on whoever is in office. Gasoline rose 9.1% in the month and accounted for about half of the headline gain. Over the past year gas is up 34.2% and fuel oil is up 74.2%, which is a lot of Americans learning what a barrel of Brent crude is. CNBC reported Brent near $105 on Tuesday, after a roughly 42% advance in the third quarter.

Core CPI, which strips out food and energy because they are considered too volatile to count, rose 0.3% on the month and 2.6% on the year. Economists had forecast 0.2% and 2.5%. Translation: the things that are supposed to be calm are also moving, which undermines the case that this is only about oil. Shelter rose 0.4%, used vehicles 0.7%, and tariff-sensitive apparel 0.3%. Food was flat on the month.

Carson Group's Sonu Varghese noted that core inflation ran at a 3.7% annualized pace in September. He said the only surprise is that investors keep being surprised, and that the full effect of higher diesel prices and the AI boom has yet to arrive. Prices have also been pushed by tariffs and demand for goods tied to the AI build-out, so the oil shock has company.

Markets reacted as markets do. Stock futures turned negative and bond yields rose sharply. Traders put the odds of a Fed hike at the Oct. 27-28 meeting at about 54%, according to CME's FedWatch tool, and the odds of a hike by year-end above 96%. The sources are a little inconsistent about October: CNBC also reports that markets expect the Fed to skip it and hike in December, so the market is confident the Fed will hike, just not sure when.

The Fed's problem is one of definitions. Supply shocks like an energy spike are usually considered temporary, and central banks often look through them. Translation: they wait it out and hope. But officials fear that the longer the war lasts, the more people will assume high prices are permanent. That is the self-fulfilling prophecy in which businesses raise prices early and workers ask for bigger raises in advance.

The worry is not abstract. CNBC calculated that CPI has now run above 2% for about 65 consecutive months, the longest such streak of the 21st century. Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack said consumers are starting to accept high inflation as a fact of life, and the New York Fed's survey showed one-year inflation expectations rising to 3.9%. In other words, the public has begun to price in what the Fed keeps describing as temporary.

For households, the arithmetic is unforgiving. Annual inflation has outrun wage growth in recent months, according to CNBC, and the University of Michigan reported record-low sentiment earlier this year. Economists quoted by CNBC say that prices are not coming back down, because falling prices would signal a different kind of trouble. The realistic hope is that inflation slows below the pace of pay raises for a long time. Until then, the cure, according to KPMG's Diane Swonk, hurts but beats the disease.

The next data points are Thursday's producer price index, forecast to rise 5.5% on the year, and the PCE index, the Fed's preferred gauge, due later this month. With this the last CPI reading before the Fed meets and before the midterms, voters and policymakers will both be reading the same number and drawing very different conclusions.
