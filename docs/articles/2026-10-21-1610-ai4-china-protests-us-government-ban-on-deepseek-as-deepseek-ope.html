China Condemns US Ban on Deepseek, Having Long Since Banned Everyone Else

Beijing demands Washington lift its restriction on the Chinese AI firm, while its own regulators keep ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini off the national market, The Decoder reported.

The wire said: China protests the US Deepseek ban while blocking ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini at home · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 21, 2026 16:10 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The US Department of Homeland Security ordered civilian federal agencies to remove Deepseek products from their systems, citing fears that sensitive data could reach Beijing. China's Foreign and Commerce Ministries responded with protests and promises of countermeasures.

Why it matters

The dispute shows that AI software is now treated like a strategic import, with each government restricting the other's products. For ordinary users and businesses, the practical result is a market splitting into two blocs, while cheap open-source tools from Deepseek keep pushing prices down.

The US Department of Homeland Security has ordered civilian federal agencies to strip Deepseek's products from their IT systems, according to The Decoder. The ban also covers software from other vendors built on Deepseek's open-source models, and it prohibits future purchases. Washington's stated worry is that Beijing could reach sensitive data through the tools. China has objected, which is its right, though the objection arrives with some context.

China's Foreign Ministry accused the US of stretching the concept of national security too far. The Commerce Ministry demanded the ban be lifted and vowed what it called resolute countermeasures. Translation: a government that inspects foreign AI services at the border is upset that another government has begun inspecting one of its own.

The context is that ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are not approved in China at all. Since 2023, the Cyberspace Administration has been required to review AI services, which must also comply with political guidelines. That rule applies to the whole market, not just government offices. State bodies and state-owned companies are meant to gradually replace foreign hardware and software with domestic products, and reports point to restrictions on iPhones for officials. The US ban covers federal agencies. The Chinese approach covers, roughly, everyone.

The US concerns are not invented, The Decoder notes. China's 2017 National Intelligence Law requires companies and citizens to support intelligence work. Chinese models also steer clear of certain topics and repeat state propaganda less critically than Western ones. US AI companies, the FBI and other agencies have accused Chinese firms of copying top American models on an industrial scale, which Beijing denies.

So why protest at all? The Decoder argues it serves three purposes. It reassures domestic companies that the state is behind them, it supports the story abroad that Washington wants to monopolize AI, and it lets Beijing frame later retaliation as merely a response. According to the NYT, Beijing also proposed its own export and licensing controls for AI technologies last week, similar to the American approach. Imitation, as the saying goes, is the sincerest form of trade policy.

Meanwhile Deepseek has been busy being inconvenient in a different way. It has open-sourced DeepInfer, the inference framework behind its V4.1-Flash model, under the Apache License 2.0. Translation: inference is the part where a trained model actually answers your question, and Deepseek is now giving away the software that does it quickly and cheaply. The company says the goal is to help the community lower compute costs, reduce energy use and make AI more affordable.

The Decoder adds that the release also adds pricing pressure on Western providers like OpenAI and Anthropic, and could be politically motivated given the race between the two countries. If Western AI companies miss their growth targets, it says, that is a risk for the US economy and beyond. Both governments are protecting their markets in the name of security, and one of the products in question is available free of charge under a permissive license. Expect the next round of countermeasures to be announced before anyone reads the licence.
