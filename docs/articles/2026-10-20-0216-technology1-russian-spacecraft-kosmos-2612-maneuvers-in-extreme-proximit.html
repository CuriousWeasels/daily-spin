Russian Satellite Spends Weekend Loitering Next to Ukraine-Linked Imaging Craft, Insists It Was Just Passing

LeoLabs says Kosmos 2612 closed from hundreds of kilometers to within 500 meters of a US commercial satellite, in what it calls the first known unsanctioned rendezvous in low-Earth orbit.

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 20, 2026 02:16 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Russia launched a group of six satellites in April on a Soyuz rocket, and analysts soon noticed at least four had adjusted their orbits to line up with ICEYE-X36, a US-operated commercial Earth-imaging satellite. This week, one of them, Kosmos 2612, moved in close.

Why it matters

Commercial imaging satellites now supply the pictures that governments and militaries rely on, which makes them part of the battlefield whether or not they were built for one. If close approaches become routine, operators will have to treat other companies' spacecraft as potential threats, and insurance and safety planning will follow.

A Russian military satellite, Kosmos 2612, maneuvered in what the orbital tracking firm LeoLabs called "extreme proximity" to ICEYE-X36, a US-operated commercial imaging satellite, according to Ars Technica's report on the company's findings. LeoLabs described it as the first known unsanctioned rendezvous in low-Earth orbit. In a field where spacecraft normally try to avoid each other, this was a deliberate visit nobody had been invited to.

The sequence, as LeoLabs tells it, was methodical. Kosmos 2612 closed from hundreds of kilometers away, circled the ICEYE craft, stayed nearby for an extended period, and then began moving away. By Friday it was holding station 2 to 3 kilometers off before shifting to positions ahead of and behind its neighbor. Over the weekend the two were within 500 meters (1,640 feet) of each other, and "likely quite a bit closer than that," said LeoLabs vice president Adam Marsh.

LeoLabs was explicit that this was not an accident of geometry. "This was not a routine conjunction or 'fly by,'" the company said, citing a controlled approach, changes in relative position, close-proximity loitering, additional small maneuvers, and a controlled departure. Translation: when two satellites pass each other by chance, they do not also rearrange themselves to stand in front of and behind each other. The company called the behavior consistent with sophisticated rendezvous and proximity operations, known in the trade as RPO, which is the polite term for parking next to something on purpose.

LeoLabs runs a global radar network that can track objects as small as 2 centimeters, roughly the size of a marble. Even so, the two spacecraft were close enough that they occasionally made it difficult to tell them apart. That is an unusual problem for a company whose business is knowing exactly where everything is, and a useful reminder that tracking is easier when nobody is trying to stand right next to the thing being tracked.

This was not a surprise to everyone. US military officials and independent analysts have followed Kosmos 2612 since its April launch from a spaceport in northern Russia. Within weeks, at least four of the six satellites from that launch appeared to be lining up with ICEYE-X36's orbit. Ars Technica's headline links the target to use by Ukraine, though the excerpt provided here does not detail what that use involves or what Russia intends.

That gap matters, because proximity alone is not an attack. A satellite sitting 500 meters from another can inspect it, listen to it, or simply let its owner know it is being watched. It can also, in principle, do worse, which is why the distinction between a neighborly visit and a threat rests mostly on trust, a resource not abundant in orbit at the moment.

For now, the Russian spacecraft has begun moving away, and ICEYE-X36 is still up there taking pictures. What to watch next is whether the other satellites from the April launch make visits of their own, and whether anyone with a spacecraft to protect decides that polite distance needs an enforcement plan.
