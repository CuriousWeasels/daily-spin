Apple Unveils Touchscreen MacBook Pro After 15 Years of Saying Touchscreens Were a Bad Idea

The new OLED MacBook Pro starts at $2,899 and lets users poke the screen, a feature Apple executives spent over a decade calling fatiguing, ergonomically terrible and, in one case, a toaster-refrigerator.

The wire said: Apple announces touchscreen MacBook Pro with OLED display · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 29, 2026 17:39 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Apple announced a redesigned MacBook Pro with an OLED display and, for the first time on a Mac, a touchscreen. Apple leaders including Steve Jobs, Tim Cook and Craig Federighi had publicly dismissed the idea for years.

Why it matters

Anyone shopping for a high-end Mac now pays a premium for a screen they can touch, though most of what touch does is already possible with the trackpad. The move also hints that Apple's Mac and iPad lines may drift closer together.

Apple on Tuesday announced the first major MacBook Pro redesign in more than five years, according to Ars Technica. It has an OLED display (Translation: each pixel makes its own light, so black is actually black) and a touchscreen, which is new to macOS. The company has also named the panel PROLED XDR, which is the kind of name that only gets approved after many meetings.

The specifications are strong. Ars Technica reports the display reaches 2,000 nits of peak brightness (Translation: bright enough to be seen outdoors, or to annoy a seatmate on a plane) and a claimed 10,000,000:1 contrast ratio. It keeps the 120 Hz variable refresh rate. Apple referenced burn-in protection, the tendency of OLED screens to retain ghost images, but did not say how it works. The 14-inch model weighs 3 pounds, about half a pound less than before.

The touchscreen is the part that required a change of heart. The Verge notes that Jobs called touchscreen laptops "ergonomically terrible" in 2010. Cook said in 2012 that you can converge a toaster and a refrigerator, but the result is unlikely to please anyone. Federighi said in 2014 that Apple was not all that interested in building one, and in 2018 that lifting your arm to poke a screen is fatiguing.

What changed, according to Apple marketing chief Greg Joswiak in a Wired interview quoted by The Verge, is that a generation of young creators "expect to touch." That is a gentle way of saying the toaster-refrigerator market had been quietly doing fine without Apple. Microsoft, which had been trying touch on laptops since Windows Vista, reportedly has staff who feel vindicated. The Verge says a former Windows chief once gave a Surface boss a 3D rendering of a toaster and a refrigerator.

The product itself is cautious. Ars Technica's hands-on found it is not a convertible, does not fold into a tablet, and does not support Apple Pencil. Touch handles swiping between desktops, dragging files, pinching and zooming, and finger painting. Both outlets note that most of it duplicates trackpad gestures. Apple says it redesigned the hinge to reduce wobble, though The Verge says demos show some wobble remains. Translation: you may now poke the screen, and the screen may poke back.

Software has been adjusted too. The menu bar expands for touch and the window buttons grow when approached by a finger. The camera notch is replaced by the Dynamic Island, the iPhone feature that turns a hardware obstacle into a menu. Ars Technica's reviewer worries about how it behaves with fullscreen games, which he could not test.

Then there is the price. The Verge says the line starts at $2,899, nearly $1,000 more than the regular MacBook Pro. Ars Technica lists a 16-inch model with an M5 Max, 64 GB of RAM and 2 TB of storage at $5,799, and upgrading from 64 GB to 128 GB adds $1,600. Apple says the future belongs to young people who expect to touch, and has priced the future accordingly.

Apple also needs developers to support touch, which went badly last time. The Verge recalls the Touch Bar, launched in 2016 as revolutionary and scrapped in 2023 after inconsistent app support. This time the basics like scrolling and pinch to zoom come built in, so developers have less to do. What to watch: whether the touchscreen trickles down to cheaper Macs, and whether Apple keeps insisting the Mac and iPad are separate things.
