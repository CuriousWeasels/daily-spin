Israel Votes After Years of War, and Gaza Wonders Whether Anyone Is Asking

On the eve of the general election, some Israelis want Netanyahu out, others want him kept, and Palestinians in Gaza expect little to change either way.

The wire said: ‘Enough is enough’: Palestinians and Israelis share views ahead of election · Al Jazeera

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 26, 2026 17:43 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Israel holds a general election this week, the first since years of war that followed the Oct. 7, 2023, attacks. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu faces a center-right challenger who is a former general.

Why it matters

The result will shape Israel's wartime policy and its approach to Gaza, but Palestinians interviewed by Al Jazeera say a new leader would not change life under occupation. A vote that matters enormously to one population may matter much less to the one living with the consequences.

Israel goes to the polls this week in a general election that NPR describes, for many Israelis, as the most consequential in decades. The vote comes after years of war and the shock of the Oct. 7, 2023, attacks. It is rare for a country to hold an election in which nearly everyone agrees the stakes are high and almost no one agrees on what they are.

The incumbent is Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. His main challenger, according to NPR, is a center-right former general. Translation: the opposition's leading pitch is that Israel should replace a longtime political leader with a man from the military. That is not a contrast in worldview so much as a contrast in résumé, and both résumés include the word security.

Al Jazeera reported from both sides of the line on the eve of the vote. Some Israelis said they want change, with the phrase 'enough is enough' featuring in the headline. Others said they back Netanyahu to stay in power. Both groups, it seems, have reached the limit of something, and they disagree about which direction the limit is in.

In Gaza, the view is different. Al Jazeera reported that Palestinians there say a change of leadership would do little to alter the reality of life under occupation. This is a significant point about how elections work: the people most affected by a government's decisions are often not among those who get to vote for it. Gazans cannot vote in Israeli elections, so their forecast is a review rather than a ballot.

NPR's framing is that Israelis have changed after the war, and the open question is whether they will change their leadership. That is the standard test of any wartime election. Voters decide whether the people who led them through a catastrophe should be rewarded for surviving it or blamed for it, and both arguments are available to anyone with a campaign slogan.

The sources are thin on polling, coalition math and the challenger's name, so it is not possible to say here how close the race is. What can be said is that Israeli voters will choose between continuity and change, while Palestinians in Gaza have told reporters they expect the two options to look similar from where they sit. The first results will show which of those predictions needs a correction.
