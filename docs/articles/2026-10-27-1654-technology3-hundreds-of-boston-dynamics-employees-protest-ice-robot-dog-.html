Boston Dynamics Staff Ask Company Not to Sell Robot Dogs to ICE; Company Sells Robot Dogs to ICE

More than 300 employees signed a petition against a $1.3 million contract for four modified Spot robots, but executives say the deal stands and the dogs will not be weaponized.

The wire said: ICE plan to buy robot dogs spurs protest from hundreds of Boston Dynamics employees · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 27, 2026 16:54 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The Department of Homeland Security awarded a $1.3 million contract to a Connecticut firm, Radeco, to supply ICE with modified versions of Boston Dynamics' Spot robot. Employees objected in a petition shared with The Boston Globe and WBUR.

Why it matters

The dispute shows how far a robot maker's sales terms can govern what a government agency does with its machines once the check clears. It also shows what happens when staff morale and a growth plan point in opposite directions.

More than 300 employees at Boston Dynamics signed a petition urging executives to halt the sale of the company's robot dogs to US Immigration and Customs Enforcement, according to Ars Technica. Executives declined, saying the deal is consistent with the company's purpose and that the robots will not be weaponized. The petition was sent in early September, and the contract was awarded on September 25, which suggests the timeline was not a negotiation.

The money is modest by federal standards. The contract, worth $1.3 million, goes to Radeco, a Connecticut company that buys Spot robots and modifies them into a product called Cerberus. It covers four robots. Boston Dynamics' role, according to The Boston Globe, would mainly be technical support. Translation: the company is not selling directly to ICE, but it is the one you call when the dog stops working.

The contract describes the Cerberus as a tool to detect radiation, toxic gas leaks and explosive threats. It has an oxygen sensor for confined spaces such as tunnels, a 30-times zoom camera, 360-degree cameras and thermal imaging. An optional arm lets it open doors and move objects. The contract also credits it with 'repeatable autonomous missions with minimal human intervention,' which is a phrase that reads differently depending on who is standing on the other side of the door.

The stated purpose is Homeland Security Task Forces work on cross-border tunnels and smuggling networks. Boston Dynamics stressed that the robots would mainly go to Homeland Security Investigations, the unit focused on transnational criminal organizations. The petition's authors were not reassured. The Brennan Center for Justice has documented that the same unit was frequently involved in deportations and workplace raids during Trump's first term, and the Immigration Policy Tracking Project reports that thousands of its agents were redirected to immigration enforcement after a 2025 executive order. Translation: the unit's job description is whatever the current administration says it is.

The petition cited the September 4 raid at a Hyundai Motor Group battery and vehicle plant in Georgia, where 475 mostly South Korean workers were detained. Hyundai owns Boston Dynamics, having acquired it in June 2021. 'We cannot be proud of working at Boston Dynamics,' the petition said, when its work is used by an agency accused of brutalizing civilians, including colleagues at Hyundai. Hyundai, for its part, has kept the Georgia plant running and still plans to deploy thousands of Atlas humanoid robots in its US facilities.

Company leadership responded with the terms and conditions. Former interim CEO Amanda McMaster wrote that the decision fits the company's purpose of 'ethically advancing intelligent machines in service of humanity,' and asked staff to 'respect and accept this decision.' The sales terms prohibit using Spot to harm or intimidate any person or animal, or as a weapon. A spokesperson said there have been no violations by Homeland Security or other public safety agencies. If there were, the company says it would void the warranty, refuse software updates, terminate the license, pursue legal action and likely never sell to that customer again. Translation: the enforcement mechanism is a very firm customer service department.

The timing is awkward for a company that is growing. Boston Dynamics is building a $100 million robotics and AI facility in Waltham, Massachusetts, which a company press release says will create 1,250 jobs by 2033. It currently employs about 1,400 people, so the petition represents roughly a fifth of the workforce. A company that needs to hire close to double its headcount may eventually find that recruits also read the news.

What to watch is whether any of the four dogs is ever the subject of a terms-of-sale dispute, and whether the next petition draws more than 300 names. Until then, the robots will presumably continue to detect gas leaks, and the employees will continue to detect something else.
