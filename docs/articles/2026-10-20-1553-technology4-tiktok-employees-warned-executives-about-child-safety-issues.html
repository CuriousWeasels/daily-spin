TikTok Staff Warned of Child Safety Risks, Court Records Say; Executives Reportedly Remained Calm

Depositions filed in federal court describe employees who say they raised alarms about minors on the platform and were frustrated by the response.

The wire said: TikTok employees said they knew about child safety issues, but execs failed to act · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 20, 2026 15:53 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Hundreds of lawsuits, combined into one multi-district case since 2022, allege that TikTok, Meta, Snap and Google knew their platforms harmed children and did not disclose the risks. Newly public depositions from former TikTok staffers were filed in federal court in March, according to NPR and The Verge.

Why it matters

The case tests whether internal warnings can be used to show a company knew about harms to minors, which could shape liability and what parents are told about the apps their children use.

TikTok employees repeatedly warned the company about child safety risks on its platform and were frustrated that executives did not act, according to court records obtained by NPR and reported by The Verge. The concerns included minors getting around age restrictions and appearing in what one witness called "transactional sexual content" that the company could profit from. It is a familiar corporate life cycle: a problem is identified, escalated, and then admired from a safe distance.

The depositions were filed in March as part of a multi-district litigation. Translation: a judge has gathered hundreds of similar lawsuits into one proceeding so that everyone can fight about the same evidence once instead of hundreds of times. The plaintiffs allege that TikTok, Meta, Snap and Google knew their platforms were harmful to children and failed to disclose the risks.

One deposition came from Reagen Maher, who held senior safety and investigations roles between 2021 and 2024. Maher described flagging to then-CEO V Pappas the prevalence of minors stripping on TikTok's Live feature in exchange for "gifts" that can be converted into real currency. Translation: a virtual present is a payment with better branding, and the platform has a stake in the transaction.

Forbes reported on the issue in 2022. Maher said the company already knew about the exploitation concerns before the article ran. In an internal chat excerpt, Maher wrote of "fighting about fixing this for months with people who didn't think the harms are severe enough." Maher added that senior leaders knew, that some projects were under way to fix parts of it, and that the writer hoped the article would put "a fire" behind other solutions. Press coverage, in this telling, served as the company's escalation process of last resort.

A second deposition came from Eric Han, TikTok's former head of safety. Han said that in 2020 he was concerned about algorithmic "rabbit holes," which an internal report described as having a "real contagion effect" regarding suicidal ideation and behavior. Han said that despite flagging that "minor safety is an urgent issue that we must address effectively," TikTok did not warn parents of the potential dangers of their children using the platform. The sources do not include TikTok's response to these accounts.

The depositions are testimony and the case is unresolved, so none of this amounts to a finding. But the legal theory depends heavily on what companies knew and when, and internal chats are the kind of evidence that tends to answer that question in the employees' own words. What to watch next is whether the defendants argue that the warnings were just healthy internal debate, which is a defense that has the advantage of describing every unheeded memo ever written.
