Epic Vibe-Codes a Faster Launcher, Steam Reportedly Wins the Race the Next Day

Epic's AI-assisted rewrite beats Steam on startup by a few seconds, according to Ars Technica, until a Hacker News post says Valve patched its way back ahead.

The wire said: Epic says its new vibe-coded Epic Games Launcher is faster than Steam · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 16, 2026 01:49 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Epic launched its Game Store and launcher nearly eight years ago to compete with Valve's Steam. It has now released a preview of a rewritten launcher, built with AI coding tools, and is marketing it mainly on speed.

Why it matters

For PC gamers, launcher speed is a real if modest convenience, and competition between storefronts is what produces it. But the dispute shows that a speed claim measured in fractions of a second can be erased by a single patch.

Epic Games has released a public preview of a new Epic Games Launcher that it says is faster than Steam, and that it built partly by vibe coding. Translation: vibe coding means describing what you want to an AI tool and letting it write much of the software. Epic's announcement said "programming is hard," which is an unusually candid thing to tell customers about the product they are about to install.

The company's pitch is more careful than the joke. Epic Games Store general manager Martin Keely told PC Gamer the team started from scratch and used AI as a tool to write and review features in a different tech stack. Translation: tech stack means the underlying software foundation. The goal, he said, was to replicate the old launcher's functionality but not its code. He told IGN that AI let a small team rewrite the code base and launch faster across platforms, with Windows and Mac supported and Linux planned.

Epic's social media post says the launcher was vibe coded to be faster than the other guy. The other guy is Valve, which runs Steam and has not, in this story, been asked for comment. Ars Technica's testing found the new launcher gets from a cold start to the store page a few seconds faster than Steam, assuming no login or update issues interfere. That caveat is doing real work, since login and update issues are a traditional part of the launcher experience.

Epic also claimed its launcher restores from the system tray to the Library screen slightly faster than Steam, by 0.06 seconds. Ars said that gap is so small as to be unnoticeable, and that it did not notice it in testing. A speed advantage that cannot be felt is, strictly speaking, a marketing advantage.

Then came the sequel. A Hacker News post said that 24 hours after Epic's launch, Steam pushed a small update that put its client back in front. Its table lists Epic's preview at roughly 5.47 seconds for a cold boot and Steam at roughly 5.23 seconds after the patch. On tray restore, it lists Epic at about 0.36 seconds and Steam at 0.42, so Epic keeps that one. The post also treats Valve's quick reply as evidence that targeted optimization can beat what it calls AI speedruns.

The sources do not fully agree. Ars measured Steam as slower on startup, while the Hacker News post reports a post-patch Steam that is faster, and we have no independent confirmation of the patch or its numbers. The difference between the two is about a quarter of a second, roughly the time it takes to decide you will play something else.

The Hacker News post also lists what Epic's client still lacks compared with Steam, including community forums, user-written reviews, Remote Play, a Big Picture mode, family library sharing and game version rollback. It is a long list, and the author's point is that a launcher can load quickly and still have little to load. Epic's claim is about speed, and on features it has not claimed anything.

Epic has said a Linux version is coming, and the preview is open for anyone to test. The next thing to watch is whether the benchmark war continues with another round of patches, which would be the best outcome for users and the only one in which everyone launches games a little faster.
