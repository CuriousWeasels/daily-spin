Google Releases Gemini 4 Argon, Discovers Frontier Is Crowded Again

Google's new flagship model arrives at $2 per million input tokens, one day after Meta's rival launch and with free users still on the weaker tier.

By The Daily Spin Desk · Oct 15, 2026 13:06 UTC · 2 min read
Catch upGoogle unveiled Gemini 4 Argon a few weeks ago and held back a full release, citing the potential for misuse. It is now rolling out worldwide, a day after Meta launched its own frontier model, Muse Wonder Full.
Why it mattersPrice cuts among top AI labs lower costs for developers and businesses, but the best models are increasingly reserved for paying subscribers. Free users get the lighter Flash tier.

Google began rolling out Gemini 4 Argon on Tuesday, according to The Decoder, making what it calls its most capable model available to developers, businesses and consumers. Ars Technica noted that Google had said it needed to move cautiously because of the potential for nefarious use, and that the cautious phase lasted a few weeks. Nothing about the model's capabilities has changed in the interim, so the risk appears to have been managed mostly by the calendar.

Access depends on how much you pay. Translation: a tier structure is a price list that describes who gets the good model. Google AI Ultra subscribers can use Argon in Google Antigravity, while Ultra and Pro subscribers get it in the Gemini app and Search's AI mode. Free users and Plus subscribers are limited to the weaker Flash models, according to The Decoder. Argon replaces the Pro class as Google's top series, and Google says more Gemini 4 models will follow, which The Decoder reads as eventual trickle-down.

The pricing is where Google is competing hardest. Argon costs $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens at the introductory rate, doubling to $4 and $20 later. Translation: a token is a small chunk of text, and these are the units in which AI companies bill you for thinking. By The Decoder's table, GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 charge $10 and $50, while Claude Opus 5.5 matches Argon's regular rate. The Verge calculates the promotional discount at up to 80 percent against rivals.

Google also released internal results, which are self-reported, as launch statistics tend to be. Engineers using Argon raised agent-driven code submissions by more than 35 percent, and rollbacks fell by nearly a third. In Chromium, Argon helped finish a refactor across hundreds of files that would have taken months. Ars Technica adds that it found redundant emoji rendering in YouTube comments, and cutting it reduced server load by 45 percent. It also found bugs dating to 2018 in the Project Wing drone simulator. The bugs had been there for years, which is presumably what made them reliable.

On safety, Google says external bio-domain red teaming bypassed fewer than 1 percent of tested checks. It also reports a 0.7 percent successful attack rate on the Gray Swan prompt injection benchmark, against 8.5 percent for GPT-6 Astra and 4.8 percent for Claude Opus 5 in an earlier test. The Decoder points out that Google's write-up does not make clear whether the tests were identical. Translation: the numbers are impressive if the exams were the same exam.

The rankings are less flattering. Argon tops the Vals Index but trails Claude Opus 5.5 in independent overall rankings, according to The Decoder. After waiting roughly two weeks to ship, it also sits behind Meta's Muse Wonder Full, which launched a day earlier. The Verge argues the two launches have reopened a race that looked settled between Anthropic and OpenAI, after a stretch in which Google lost Jeff Dean to a startup and Demis Hassabis stepped back from day-to-day DeepMind leadership.

Google is reportedly already testing a successor codenamed Carbon, aimed at coding. If the pattern holds, Argon will remain the best model for roughly as long as it takes someone else to schedule a launch.
