Ofcom Lists Ways to Prove You Are Over 16, Asks Tech Firms to Pick One

The regulator's age-check menu for the UK's March social media ban is subject to government approval, and Ofcom concedes some children will still get through.

The wire said: Ofcom outlines age check methods ahead of social media ban for under-16s · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 31, 2026 02:52 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The UK plans to bar under-16s from ten major platforms, including Instagram, TikTok and Snapchat, from March next year. Ofcom has now set out which age-checking methods it considers effective, and the government will decide by the end of the year.

Why it matters

Many adults, not just teenagers, will likely have to prove their age to keep using their accounts, which means handing over a face, a bank record or an ID. Who carries the burden of checking, the platforms or Apple and Google, is still unsettled.

Ofcom, the UK communications regulator, has published its recommendations for how social media companies should work out which users are over 16. According to the BBC, the ban takes effect in March next year and covers ten of the biggest platforms. The recommendations are subject to government approval, so the menu has been written before the restaurant has agreed to open.

Ofcom named four methods it calls "highly effective", in addition to traditional ID. The first is photo ID matching, meaning a passport is compared with a live image taken during the check. Second is banking-based checks, which use the age a bank already has on file. Third is face age estimation, which the regulator says has recently improved. Fourth is digital ID services. Translation: a passport, a bank, a camera or a digital wallet will soon be asked to vouch for you, and you will have to choose which one you trust least.

The difficulty, the BBC notes, is that 16 and 17 year olds often lack credit cards, full driving licences or passports, and can look much older or younger than they are. Age checks for adults are easy because adults own things. Age checks for teenagers depend on a camera guessing, which is the reason Ofcom spent so long on the section about cameras guessing.

Notably, Ofcom is not telling companies which method to use. Chief executive Dame Melanie Dawes told the BBC that other countries were "very prescriptive and it didn't work". Her approach is to say what can work, insist on privacy and data protection, and leave the rest to the firms. Translation: the regulator sets the exam, and the students write the syllabus.

Meta would prefer a different arrangement. It has called for checks at device level, so that an under-age user is blocked from installing social apps via app stores. Meta told the BBC this would spare people from verifying their age repeatedly on multiple platforms. It would also move responsibility to Apple and Google, which between them run most of the world's smartphones. Ofcom replied that social networks would still have to do their own checks, so the plan reduces nobody's workload in the sense Meta might hope.

The scale is a problem of its own. Ofcom has no firm number for how many adults will have to prove they are not children, but conceded it would be a "large number of accounts". It also acknowledged that "the risk of circumvention cannot be entirely eliminated". This is a notable admission given that Australia introduced a similar ban last year and, the BBC reports, many children there found ways around it. The UK says it will be tougher.

Campaigners welcomed the guidance with reservations. The NSPCC's Rani Govender said firms must be held accountable for meeting standards. Andy Burrows of the Molly Rose Foundation cited what he called Ofcom's "dire track record" enforcing the Online Safety Act. Dawes, for her part, pointed to the longer prize: eight to 10 year olds starting secondary school without a class group chat on Snap or sports fixtures organised on Instagram.

Next comes the government's decision, due by the end of the year. After that, the ten platforms find out whether they are to be inspected on results rather than methods, and the nation's teenagers begin their own independent assessment of the methods.
