Meta Delays Terms Publishers Say Permit Scraping, Insists Terms Do Not Permit Scraping

The company pushed enforcement from October 30th to November 20th after publishers and lawyers objected, giving it three more weeks to explain a document it says is not about what critics think it is about.

The wire said: Meta delays new business terms after publisher pushback on AI scraping · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 23, 2026 15:58 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Meta updated its business terms in a way that appeared to let it use content from websites tied to business accounts to develop and train its products. After publishers objected, Meta removed the word "train," but legal experts said the revised wording still left the door open.

Why it matters

The dispute is about whether a company's commercial contract can override robots.txt, the long-standing way websites tell crawlers to keep out. If it can, publishers' main tool for controlling AI scraping becomes a polite suggestion.

Meta is delaying enforcement of its new business terms from October 30th to November 20th, according to The Verge, after publishers worldwide warned the terms could let the company scrape news sites for AI training. Meta spokesperson Daniel Roberts said the extra time would help explain "what's changing and what's not." Three weeks is a generous allowance for clarifying a document whose authors say it changes nothing.

The original terms said Meta "may use any content" from websites associated with a business account to "develop, research" and "train" its products. Legal experts told Nieman Lab this could let Meta argue that its commercial terms outrank robots.txt. Translation: robots.txt is a small text file on a website that tells crawlers, including Google's, which pages they may visit. It operates on an honor system, which works best when everyone is honorable and has not yet signed a contract saying otherwise.

After the backlash, Meta removed the language about "training." Lawyers were not reassured. James Grimmelmann, a Cornell Law School professor, told Nieman Lab the change "does nothing to restrict" the phrase "associated with your products or services," which he said would let Meta scrape entire sites. Matt Topic, an attorney at Loevy & Loevy, argued that the revised terms still allow use of scraped content to develop any "business product," and that "develop" is broad enough to include "train."

This is the standard drafting technique of removing a specific word while leaving behind a larger, vaguer one that covers it. It is the contractual equivalent of taking the "No Parking" sign down and noting that the curb is still technically a curb.

Pressure came from several directions. The News/Media Alliance sent a letter to Mark Zuckerberg demanding revisions, warning the terms would cause "severe harm" to publishers. Digital advertisers and publishers in the EU and UK also urged regulators to force Meta to halt the terms. Meta responded by delaying them entirely, which is how a company shows it has listened without conceding anything it said.

Meta's position, repeated by Roberts, is that "content owners can use robots.txt to signal their preferences." He added that the update "is not about scraping news sites" and does not give Meta new rights that supersede those preferences. If so, the dispute is a misunderstanding, and the cure is a clearer document. If not, publishers have spotted the problem before the deadline, which is the only time spotting it is useful.

What to watch is the wording that appears on or before November 20th. A plain sentence saying robots.txt preferences prevail would settle the matter. An explainer about how Meta uses web crawlers would suggest the explaining is still underway.
