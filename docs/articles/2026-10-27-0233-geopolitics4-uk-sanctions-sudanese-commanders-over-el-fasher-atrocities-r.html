UK Sanctions 15 Sudanese Commanders, Reminding Them That Travel Bans Exist

Britain froze assets and banned travel for officers from both sides over sexual violence at El Fasher, a year after the city fell and as drone strikes darken the country's east.

The wire said: UK sanctions Sudanese military leaders implicated in El Fasher ‘campaign of rape’ · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 27, 2026 02:33 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Sudan's army and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces have fought since April 2023. The RSF took the city of El Fasher after an 18-month siege, and the war has since become largely a drone war.

Why it matters

Sanctions rarely stop a war, but they can mark who is accountable and limit where commanders can bank and travel. Meanwhile, civilians in Sudan are losing electricity, food storage, medicine and farming income as the fighting goes on.

The British government announced on the first anniversary of the fall of El Fasher that it has sanctioned 15 commanders from both the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) and the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF), according to The Guardian. The men, suspected of involvement in sexual violence during the siege, face a travel ban and an asset freeze. The Foreign Office said it hoped the action would ensure "the horrors that took place at El Fasher will be known to the world."

Translation: an asset freeze means that any money the officers keep in places Britain can reach becomes unavailable to them. A travel ban means they cannot visit. Whether commanders of a civil war were planning shopping trips to London is a question the announcement does not address.

The Guardian named two of those targeted. Hussein Abu Jalak is an RSF colonel linked to atrocities during the siege, which forced hundreds of thousands of civilians to live off animal feed. Al-Nour Ahmed Adam, known as "Al-Qubba," was identified as playing a prominent role in the attack on the city.

The scale of what is being addressed is not small. During the RSF takeover, up to 10,000 men, women and children are believed to have been killed over two days, which The Guardian called the fastest and largest massacre of civilians this century. Another 40,000 people remain unaccounted for. Fewer than 70,000 people are believed to remain in a city that had more than 1 million before the war, and testimony points to routine detention, torture and unlawful killings.

The siege was allegedly backed by the United Arab Emirates, which continues to deny supporting the RSF. The Guardian also reported earlier this year that internal US and UK warnings were sidelined before the attack and that action which might have saved lives was not taken. So the sanctions arrive after the warnings, which is the usual order of operations.

Elsewhere, the war keeps moving. The RSF launched a coordinated drone attack on five power stations, two each in Al-Jazira and Gedaref states and one in White Nile state, according to a government official who spoke anonymously. Large parts of all three states, which the army controls, lost power.

For ordinary people, a power station is not an abstraction. Babiker Youssef, a resident of Al-Fao, told The Guardian his diabetic son's insulin must be kept in a fridge and that he feared he could not replace it. A grocer in Gedaref said he must either lose his stock or sell it at any price, which is still a loss. A farmer in Al-Jazira said a repaired station that had given him hope of running irrigation pumps again was hit after nearly four years of being unable to work his land.

The wider numbers are grim. A UN tally found that drone strikes killed more than 300 people in the first three weeks of October. Aid workers estimate the war has killed more than 200,000, 14 million have been forced from their homes, most hospitals have closed and at least 8 million children are out of school. The currency has also lost more than 25% of its value in recent weeks.

The sanctions are aimed at both sides, which is at least consistent with a war in which both sides stand accused. What to watch next is whether other governments follow with their own lists, and whether anything on a list can reach a drone operator before the next power station.
