Internet Discovers It Runs on PHP, Responds by Switching Itself Off

Two flaws found by AI in the language behind roughly 70% of websites, per Wiz, sent governments and broadcasters offline while they patched.

The wire said: Websites taken down worldwide as critical cyber-security update issued · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 23, 2026 02:08 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Researchers using advanced AI tools found two critical flaws, nicknamed php2shell, in the PHP programming language that had gone unnoticed for more than a decade. After details leaked on social media, the PHP Foundation rushed out a fix on Wednesday and agencies worldwide urged operators to patch or take sites offline.

Why it matters

Most readers need do nothing, but anyone with a website, especially a small business on WordPress, must ask their host whether PHP itself has been patched. Attackers are already probing, and security agencies say the full impact could take months to appear.

The BBC reported that websites around the world went dark on Wednesday and Thursday after the developers of PHP disclosed two critical vulnerabilities in the language. Finland shut its government web services, the Dutch cyber-security agency took down its own site, and parts of Wikipedia became unavailable. Security agencies had advised that if you cannot fix the problem, you should turn the thing off, which is the digital equivalent of dealing with a gas leak by leaving the house.

PHP is a back-end language that runs on web servers and turns stored data into the pages you read. Translation: it is the kitchen of the website, and most people have never seen it. According to Wiz, one of the firms that found the flaws, it powers about 70% of the world's websites, though the BBC said it could not verify that figure. It sits under WordPress, Drupal, Joomla and Magento, plus a good deal of software that never mentions it, Huntress noted.

The flaws, nicknamed php2shell, could allow what CERT-EU described as unauthenticated remote code execution. Translation: a stranger on the internet could run commands on your server without a password. Wiz said the bugs are inherent in the language itself and had gone undetected for more than a decade. A Calif researcher, Ryan Austin, called it the most serious vulnerability he had ever found.

The discoverer was artificial intelligence. Calif and the PHP Foundation took part in an OpenAI program called Patch the Planet, which gave them advanced AI tools, and within weeks the flaws surfaced. Wiz said the models let it develop working exploits across multiple applications in hours. OpenAI called the find one of the most impactful security issues in recent memory, which is a notable thing for a company to say about a product it also sells.

The schedule then went wrong in the usual way. The PHP Foundation had planned to publish its findings later this month with a complete fix, but details leaked on social media on Tuesday and forced an early announcement. Cloudflare's Matthew Prince wrote that attempts to exploit the flaws went from thousands in the 10 hours before disclosure to tens of thousands within 45 minutes, hundreds of thousands within two hours, and millions by Thursday. Amazon also reported attempts, though its sites ran normally.

In Britain, tech minister Baroness Lloyd told the House of Lords that 32 government services were unavailable and four partially so, largely from precautionary measures. The government said this did not mean they were hacked, and the NCSC's Olly Whitehouse called the event unprecedented but reported no indications of successful breaches. A Chepstow garage owner, Dean Presley, said his MOT system had been down since Wednesday morning, so he was writing certificates on paper, a technology the flaw cannot reach.

The hard part is not installing the patch but finding every place PHP lurks, Sophos's Don Smith said, since it can sit beneath hosting platforms and third-party products without the owner knowing. The fix is also issued only for recent versions, with PHP 8.1 users and older told to upgrade. Ethical hacker Ken Munro compared patching to running a Windows update, which is reassuring until you recall how many people postpone those.

NCC Group's Matt Hull said the window between a flaw and its exploitation has shrunk from weeks to days or hours, and warned that responders may already be burned out. The lesson is that AI finds bugs faster than anyone can fix them, and attackers have the same tools. Watch whether the scans showing slow patching speed up before the exploit attempts, now in the millions, start to succeed.
