Southwest Cuts Profit Forecast as Customers Keep Paying Higher Fares to Be Disappointed Less

Third major U.S. airline this month trims its 2026 outlook on fuel costs, even as fares rose almost 27% and the quarter beat estimates.

The wire said: Southwest Airlines cuts 2026 profit forecast on higher fuel · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 22, 2026 02:03 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Southwest Airlines reported third-quarter results and lowered its full-year 2026 earnings forecast because of higher fuel prices, following similar cuts from Delta and United. The airline has spent several years dropping signature policies such as free checked bags and open seating to raise revenue.

Why it matters

Fuel costs are being passed to travelers through higher fares, and airlines are betting demand holds. If it does not, the gap between strong revenue and shrinking profit forecasts gets harder to explain.

Southwest Airlines became the third major U.S. airline this month to cut its 2026 profit forecast because of higher fuel prices, CNBC reported. It now expects adjusted earnings of $2.60 to $2.85 a share, down from the $3.25 to $4.25 it projected in July. The company did this in the same release in which it beat Wall Street expectations, which is a respectable way to deliver bad news: first the good news, then the arithmetic.

The quarter itself was fine. Adjusted earnings were 66 cents a share against 58 cents expected, according to LSEG consensus estimates, and revenue was $8.42 billion against $8.25 billion expected. Net income was $270 million, or 55 cents a share, up from $54 million a year earlier. Translation: adjusted earnings are profit with one-time items removed, a process in which a company decides which events were not really part of its business.

The more interesting number is the fare. Southwest said its average passenger fare was $231.69, up almost 27% from the third quarter of 2025, and a spokesman said that figure excludes add-on fees. Meanwhile, revenue passengers fell 3.2% to 33.47 million. Fewer people paid much more, which is a business strategy when planned and a warning when not.

CEO Bob Jordan said in the earnings release that demand remained strong as the airline lapped last year's bag fee and other commercial initiatives, and that its earnings power is durable even with significantly higher fuel costs. Translation: lapping means the fee has now been in place a full year, so it no longer flatters the comparison. That Southwest is citing a bag fee as evidence of durability says something about how far the airline has traveled from two free checked bags.

The company has been remaking its model under pressure to grow revenue, scrapping free bags and open seating in favor of charges for many seat assignments and a bag fee, much like its competitors. The upside is that it now earns money the way everyone else does. The downside is that it now has the same problem everyone else does, which is jet fuel.

For the fourth quarter, Southwest guided to adjusted earnings of 55 to 80 cents a share and unit revenue, a gauge of airfare, up as much as 21% from a year earlier. It plans to grow capacity by up to 4%. Delta and United also lowered their outlooks and both missed on revenue and earnings for the quarter, so Southwest's beat makes it the relatively cheerful member of a gloomy group.

For markets, the SPY exchange-traded fund, which tracks the S&P 500, stood at 773.09 as of October 21. Whether investors keep valuing airlines on revenue or begin valuing them on fuel will be the thing to watch. Passengers, meanwhile, have already voted with their wallets, and the ballot cost $231.69.
