Uganda Reports Zero Ebola Cases, While Two Neighbors' Patients Both Pass Through Its Airport

Kenya and Nigeria's first Ebola patients both transited Entebbe on October 3, and Switzerland has contradicted Uganda's account of the Nigerian woman's travels.

The wire said: Uganda says it has no active Ebola cases, but infections in Kenya and Nigeria are prompting fresh scrutiny · DW

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 24, 2026 02:07 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

An Ebola outbreak caused by the Bundibugyo virus was announced in DR Congo on 15 May and has since reached Uganda, Kenya and now Nigeria. The Guardian reports more than 9,500 infections and nearly half of those infected have died.

Why it matters

If health agencies cannot agree on where an infected traveler went, they cannot reliably find who was exposed, and this strain has no approved vaccine or treatment. Contact tracing is the main tool left, and it depends on accurate travel histories.

Health officials in Africa are trying to work out how Nigeria's first Ebola patient in more than a decade became infected, after Switzerland contradicted the account Uganda had given of her travels. According to DW and The Guardian, the Swiss foreign ministry said the woman, a Swiss national of Ugandan origin married to a Swiss diplomat in Abuja, had not traveled to the Democratic Republic of Congo. Uganda's health ministry had said preliminary findings showed she went to eastern DRC on business first. One of these accounts is wrong, and the virus is not waiting for the paperwork.

The Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention said the Nigerian patient and Kenya's first confirmed patient both passed through Entebbe International Airport on October 3. Dr Wessam Mankoula, the agency's head of emergency preparedness and response, said investigators are checking whether their movements overlapped. DW notes it has not been established that the two met or that any transmission happened there. Translation: two people were in a large airport on the same day, which describes most days at most airports.

Scientists are cautious. Marion Koopmans, a Dutch virologist on the WHO's Ebola emergency committee, called the airport theory "very speculative" without evidence of shared space. WHO's Catherine Smallwood said a link was possible, but that officials must compare timing with when the Nigerian woman's symptoms began. Ebola symptoms typically appear two to 21 days after infection, and per the WHO people are not infectious until symptoms start. That means an airport meeting is a hypothesis, not a finding.

Uganda's position is awkward. Its government says it has no confirmed active cases, and a spokesperson told Reuters that it would make any cases public. The Guardian reports Uganda had cases in May and June before the WHO declared it Ebola-free in August. Its health ministry argues that "travel through Uganda does not establish that infection occurred here." That is true, and it is also not the same as showing the infection did not occur there. The Swiss government has said she was believed to have caught the virus while traveling with her husband in Uganda, though DW says this is unconfirmed.

Kenya has its own gap. Its first patient died in Nairobi on October 5 after traveling from DRC through Uganda, and further infections have been linked to that case. Kenya's health director-general, Patrick Amoth, told the Daily Nation that officials know a Ugandan contact by name and passport number but cannot reach him. He reportedly uses a South Sudanese phone number. Being untraced does not mean he is infected, but contact tracing only works if the contacts can be found.

In Nigeria, the woman developed symptoms on October 7, visited a private health facility on October 11 and tested positive for Ebola. Health Minister Muhammad Ali Pate said she is stable and 44 contacts have been identified. He said the case remains contained. Residents in Abuja told DW of fear, and one said people are worried about "surviving" more than about sickness.

The outbreak is already the most geographically widespread in history, according to The Guardian. It is second only to the 2014-16 West Africa epidemic, which infected more than 28,600 people. Aid groups say what matters now is speed and sharing information across borders. Right now the information is arriving from several governments, and not all of it agrees. Watch for the timing comparison between the Nigerian woman's symptoms and the airport date, which should settle whether Entebbe was a meeting point or just a very busy place.
