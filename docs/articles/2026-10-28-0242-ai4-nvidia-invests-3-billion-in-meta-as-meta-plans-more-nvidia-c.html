Nvidia Invests $3 Billion in Meta, Which Plans to Spend It on Nvidia

The chipmaker is buying into Meta's stock sale as Meta seeks up to $95 billion to fund its AI buildout, much of it for Nvidia hardware.

The wire said: Meta gets $3 billion from Nvidia and plans to spend more on Nvidia chips · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 28, 2026 02:42 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Meta is raising money through stock offerings to pay for AI data centers. Nvidia, which sells the chips that fill them, has agreed to be one of the buyers.

Why it matters

If chip suppliers fund their own customers, strong sales figures may reflect borrowed enthusiasm as much as organic demand. Investors in either company are betting on both the technology and the plumbing behind it.

Nvidia is investing $3 billion in Meta's planned equity offering, the Wall Street Journal reported, as the two companies announced a broader partnership. Meta said it will use more Nvidia infrastructure for its next generation of AI agents, including Vera Rubin systems and Vera CPUs, and the companies will expand work around Meta's AI product Muse. Put plainly, a supplier has bought shares in a customer that has just promised to buy more from the supplier.

Translation: an equity offering is when a company sells new shares to raise cash. Meta wants to raise up to $95 billion this way to pay for its AI infrastructure buildout, according to the sources. That is a large sum, and it explains why Meta would welcome any buyer with a checkbook and a reason to be enthusiastic.

Nvidia is not alone. CNBC reported that AMD and Broadcom also signaled nonbinding interest in buying up to $5 billion in Meta shares combined. Translation: nonbinding means they have said they are interested, which costs nothing and commits no one. It is the corporate equivalent of saying you will definitely come to the party.

The arrangement fits a pattern investors have argued about for months, as The Decoder noted. Nvidia gives money to customers such as OpenAI, which use it to build data centers packed with Nvidia chips. Critics call this circular financing and warn it could obscure how much real demand for AI computing exists.

The mechanism is simple enough to follow. Nvidia hands over cash, the customer hands back an order, and Nvidia reports revenue. Each step may be perfectly legitimate, and Meta is a profitable company with genuine needs for computing power. The concern is that when money travels in a loop, it becomes harder for an outsider to tell whether the loop is demand or decoration.

Who gains is fairly clear. Meta gets capital and a priority relationship with the dominant chip supplier. Nvidia gets a stake in a major customer and a stronger claim on its future spending. Who pays is less clear, and will depend on whether the AI agents Meta plans to run on all this hardware earn back what it costs. Watch that question, because the shares being sold are the ones that will answer it.
