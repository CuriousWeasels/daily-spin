Trump Buys Russian Diesel to Lower Prices, Sum Total Under One Day of Demand

Washington eases sanctions on Moscow for a fuel purchase experts say is too small to move prices, while Kyiv and bipartisan senators object.

The wire said: How Trump's diesel deal with Russia will impact the United States and Ukraine · DW

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 15, 2026 14:38 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Over the weekend, President Trump announced a deal to buy Russian diesel, involving a temporary easing of US sanctions, and then an "energy ceasefire" between Ukraine and Russia. Kyiv said it had not been consulted on the second announcement.

Why it matters

The deal tests whether new US sanctions law can restrain a president who wants lower fuel prices before the November 3 midterms. For Ukraine, it raises questions about intelligence sharing and weapons deliveries it relies on.

President Donald Trump announced a deal to buy Russian diesel, which includes a temporary easing of US sanctions on Moscow, DW reported. He then announced what he called an energy ceasefire between Ukraine and Russia. Kyiv said the idea had not been discussed with Ukraine, though it said it would stop attacking Russian energy infrastructure if Moscow did the same. Being informed of your own ceasefire by the press is a form of diplomacy, though not a widely admired one.

The apparent motive is domestic. Midterm elections arrive on November 3, polls suggest Republicans could lose their House majority, and DW reports voters are unhappy with high fuel prices. Translation: when gasoline is expensive, incumbents get a lesson in supply chains.

The difficulty is arithmetic. Thomas Jäger of the University of Cologne told ARD the volume is so small it will have "absolutely no effect on prices." He added that shipments planned before the election would not cover US diesel demand for a single day. His reading is that the goal is "the story," meaning the image of a president tackling a problem voters care about. The fuel is secondary to the footage of fuel.

Critics in Kyiv and the EU argue that Russia will use the revenue to fund its war. Security expert Carlo Masala is less alarmed on the military side, noting that Washington already suspended sanctions on Russian oil exports in March, shortly after US and Israeli attacks on Iran. In his view, Russia lacks a strategy for further advances in the Donbas, and the real damage is political: the symbolism of Americans striking separate deals with Moscow at Ukraine's expense.

Ukrainians have taken it personally. Al Jazeera reported that they accused the US of gambling with their lives as Russian strikes devastated residential buildings in the capital, and that President Zelenskyy said Trump is not on the right side of history. The people living in those buildings did not get a vote on the diesel.

Kyiv has other worries. DW reports US threats to suspend intelligence sharing and block the PURL program unless Ukraine halts attacks on Russian oil refineries. Translation: PURL is the arrangement under which European countries buy US weapons for Ukraine, so the US would be blocking sales that Europeans pay for. Masala said losing US intelligence would be dramatic, though French intelligence, while less comprehensive, could soften the blow. He also noted that interceptor missiles, the program's most important component, have barely reached Ukraine since the Iran war began.

Congress may be the obstacle. Trump signed the so-called Lindsey Graham Act in September, which gives him tools to sanction Russian officials, banks and the shadow fleet, and to tariff buyers of Russian energy. Several Democratic and Republican senators cited that law and the 2022 ban on Russian oil imports in a joint statement calling the diesel deal illegal and demanding the government "immediately correct" it. Former diplomat Daniel Fried called the laws "strong barriers" against a unilateral, unrequited diesel deal.

The administration has passed a sanctions law, signed it, and now must decide whether it applies to itself. Watch whether the senators' statement becomes legislation, and whether the first barrel arrives before the ballots do.
