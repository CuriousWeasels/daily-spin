Musk Confirms He Is Considering Merging His Two Trillion-Dollar Companies, Is Not on Committee Reviewing It

Special committees at Tesla and SpaceX will weigh a deal that would combine a $1.5 trillion carmaker with a $2 trillion rocket firm, and the man who runs both says he is conflicted.

The wire said: Elon Musk confirms he is considering merging Tesla and SpaceX · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 22, 2026 16:08 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Elon Musk has spent the past year pulling his businesses under fewer banners, with SpaceX absorbing xAI and X before its stock market debut in June. On Tuesday he told analysts that a merger with Tesla is under active consideration.

Why it matters

A Tesla-SpaceX merger would be the largest in history and would reshape what Tesla shareholders actually own. Tesla owners are unlikely to notice much change soon, but the company's priorities could tilt further toward AI and away from cars.

Elon Musk said on Tuesday that he is actively considering merging Tesla and SpaceX, according to the BBC. Asked on a call with analysts whether it would be a good idea, he said: "Yes." It is a rare case of a chief executive answering a yes-or-no question with the yes.

The mechanics are less breezy. Musk said special committees have been formed at both companies to examine a deal, that they have not agreed terms, and that there is "no guarantee" they will. Translation: special committees are groups of supposedly independent directors who negotiate on behalf of shareholders when the person on both sides of a deal is the same man. Musk said he is not on either one and described himself as a "conflicted party" as chief executive of both firms.

The numbers are large enough to need their own committee. Tesla is valued at roughly $1.5 trillion and SpaceX at $2 trillion, which the BBC said would make this the largest merger ever. SpaceX's stock rose 8% in after-hours trading on the comments, and Tesla's rose 2%. Markets, it seems, enjoy a hypothetical.

The case for the deal, as Musk described it, rests on overlap in chips, artificial intelligence and software. Tesla brings cars, robots and self-driving technology. SpaceX brings rockets, data centres, satellites and telecoms. The two have already launched Terafab, a joint effort to make computer chips, which the BBC read as a sign Musk sees them working better together. The Tesla Roadster unveiled last week, powered by SpaceX rocket thrusters, was described by one analyst as a "symbolic embodiment" of the idea.

SpaceX is not exactly a quiet tenant. Revenue over the first nine months rose to $27bn from $13.7bn, yet the company lost $1.1bn over that period, despite a $4.4bn profit in the latest quarter. Its cloud compute business, which rents capacity to firms such as Anthropic and Google, brought in $7.5bn in three months, far more than Starlink's $5.2bn. Tesla, meanwhile, reported third-quarter sales of $28.05bn against $28.1bn a year earlier, a dip so slight it could be a rounding opinion.

Shareholders get a vote, and at least 50% of Tesla's must approve, a threshold Musk called "quite a high hurdle." Investors have been accommodating before: last year they approved a pay package for Musk valued at around $1tn. David Nicholson of The Futurum Group put the odds at "100%" and said it is only a matter of when. Musk himself said calling it a certainty would be "overstating" things.

Analysts see who wins. Dan Sheehan of Telos Wealth Advisors said the deal would not add to Musk's wealth but "concentrates it further in one stock." The BBC reported the merger would also give Musk more voting control than he has at Tesla today, which is exactly the sort of detail the special committee is meant to notice. Tesla shareholders, Sheehan said, would likely hold a "smaller slice of a much larger and more complex business," and there is no real precedent for how a public company like Tesla gets priced in such a deal.

For drivers, experts expect little near-term change, with cars described as almost "an afterthought" next to the AI ambitions. Tesla is already narrowing its lineup to the Model Y and Model 3 and building Optimus humanoid robots at a California plant. Regulatory scrutiny is possible, particularly if Democrats gain seats in next month's midterms.

Musk, for his part, compared even discussing the topic to "tap dancing through a minefield." The committees will now try to price the dance. Watch for terms, and for what exactly a Tesla share turns into.
