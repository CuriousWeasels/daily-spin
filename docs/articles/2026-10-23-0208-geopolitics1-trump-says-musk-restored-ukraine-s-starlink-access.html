Ukraine's Internet Returns After Three Officials Explain Nobody Was in Charge of Turning It Off

Trump says Musk 'put it back' after Starlink speed caps hobbled Ukrainian drones, while Musk, Rubio and the White House each describe a different owner of the switch.

The wire said: Trump says Musk has restored access to Ukraine's Starlink network · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 23, 2026 02:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Ukrainian officials reported sudden Starlink restrictions on October 13, leaving the military scrambling. Trump now says Elon Musk has restored access, though Zelensky said speeds were not yet fully back.

Why it matters

A war effort that depends on one private company's satellites, and a government that cannot say who controls them, is a vulnerability for Ukraine and a lesson for every military that outsources its connectivity.

US President Donald Trump said on Thursday that Elon Musk has restored Ukraine's access to Starlink, the satellite internet service its military relies on. The BBC and Al Jazeera both reported the remarks, which followed days of restrictions that hampered Kyiv's forces. Ukraine thus learned that its communications can be switched off and on again, which is the standard first step in tech support and an unusual one in warfare.

The sequence matters. Ukrainian officials announced sudden restrictions on October 13, according to Al Jazeera, and Ukraine first reported an interruption to US intelligence sharing on October 14, according to the BBC. The Wall Street Journal reported that speeds were capped at around 160 km/h, and that Trump was frustrated by this. Translation: the internet had been slowed to the point that Ukraine's offensive drones and interceptors were, in the Journal's account, powerless against Russian attacks.

Earlier on Thursday, Zelensky told reporters speeds had not yet returned to 400 km/h, adding: "We are working on it." He said he was waiting to hear from Secretary of State Marco Rubio, who had promised information within about 48 hours. The Journal then reported that Trump had called Musk and that service looked more normal afterward. The BBC said it had not confirmed that report.

Asked about the call, Trump declined to say who phoned whom. "Bottom line, he put it back," he said. This is a notable admission by implication: if Musk could put it back, Musk could have taken it away, which is a position the Trump administration and Musk have spent the week disputing.

On who flipped the switch, the accounts differ in an instructive way. Rubio said on Wednesday that speeds are governed by a private commercial contract. Musk said on October 14 that Starshield, the military-grade version of Starlink, is owned and controlled by the US government, and that limits are decided by the military and ultimately the president. The White House has repeatedly said it did not order restrictions. Trump said on Friday that it was not him and he did not think it was Musk. Translation: the system has an owner, a controller and a contractor, and each is confident it is one of the other two.

The backdrop is not subtle. Trump has been pressing Zelensky to stop attacking Russian oil refineries, which he argues have pushed up prices. The BBC notes that with midterm elections next month he has waived some sanctions on Russia and agreed to import Russian diesel, which Ukraine and European allies have criticised. Al Jazeera adds that the fuel price problem follows his decision to join Israel in a war on Iran. Critics have worried, according to Al Jazeera, that Starlink caps could be used as leverage on Kyiv, though no source here establishes that they were.

The technical dependence is simple. The networks use some 10,000 satellites in low Earth orbit, and modern drones need fast connections to operate. Ukraine uses civilian Starlink and the more secure Starshield depending on the task. A battlefield strategy built on that arrangement works well until the question of who is in charge comes up.

What to watch next is whether speeds return fully to 400 km/h, and whether anyone is willing to put in writing who owns the switch. Until then, Ukraine has internet, a promise from Rubio, and a presidential assurance that it is the same as it has always been.
