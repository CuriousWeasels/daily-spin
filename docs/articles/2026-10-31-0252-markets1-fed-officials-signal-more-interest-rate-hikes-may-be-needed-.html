Fed Holds Rates, Then Sends Two Officials to Explain Why It Will Not Hold Rates

Two regional Fed presidents said more hikes are coming, hours after the committee held steady and its chairman declined to say what he planned to do.

The wire said: Fed officials are already saying they’ll need to get back to hiking rates · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 31, 2026 02:52 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The Federal Reserve left interest rates unchanged on Wednesday, a month after raising them for the first time since 2023. Two of its officials dissented in favor of a hike, and Chairman Kevin Warsh announced plans to cancel rate votes at three future meetings.

Why it matters

If the Fed raises rates again, borrowing costs for mortgages, car loans and business credit stay higher for longer, with inflation near 3.7% and unemployment at 4.2%. Fewer scheduled rate votes also means fewer clues about when those costs will change.

The Federal Reserve held interest rates steady on Wednesday. By Friday, two of its regional presidents were publicly explaining that rates will need to go up, according to CNBC. Nothing says a pause quite like a speaking tour about the end of the pause.

St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem said "additional firming of monetary policy will be necessary, and soon." Translation: firming means raising rates, a word chosen because it sounds like something that happens to a mattress. Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan went further, saying the committee needs at least two more quarter-point increases. A quarter point, or 25 basis points, is the standard unit of Fed adjustment, small enough to be called gradual and large enough to appear on your mortgage statement.

Logan added that skipping a meeting does not change the destination, which is a reassuring thing to say about a road trip that has apparently lost a few exits. Traders took the hint: CME's FedWatch tool put the odds of a quarter-point hike in December at about 76%, according to CNBC.

The case for acting is fairly plain. Musalem called the economy resilient. GDP grew 3.4%, unemployment is 4.2%, and the personal consumption expenditures index, the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, showed annual inflation above 3%. The Fed's target is 2%. Latest consumer price data show inflation at 3.74%, which is not near 2% by any generous reading of the word near. Fed funds sit at 3.88%, and the 10-year Treasury yields 4.98%, so markets are already charging more for long money than the Fed is for overnight money.

Two voting members, Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, wanted a hike this week and dissented. Former Richmond Fed President Jeffrey Lacker said he was surprised by the lack of urgency, given inflation near three to three-and-a-half percent for so long.

Why, then, did the Fed hold? CNBC's analysis suggests Warsh prefers to keep Wall Street guessing. He said that if the Fed had a firm conclusion, it would announce it with fanfare. He also said financial conditions are tighter, but that he is unwilling and unable to call them tight. Translation: it is a hike in a trench coat, not yet ready to be seen in public.

Before the meeting, a pair of top officials appeared to talk down expectations of a hike. Warsh declined to say he sent them, remarking that he is not some all-knowing person sending messages out. CNBC notes that this gives him plausible deniability, a feature that no central bank has ever listed in its mandate but most have used.

Warsh also announced that one meeting in 2027 and two in 2028 will focus on broader strategy, with no rate decision or statement expected. The Fed will still meet eight times a year. Critics are unimpressed. Former St. Louis Fed President James Bullard warned that a volatile world might force a special meeting anyway. Lacker suggested the opposite remedy: if you want less pressure on each meeting, hold more of them. MarketWatch framed the dispute as a chairman who wants the Fed to say less against officials pushing for more transparency.

PGIM economist Robert Sockin called the hold a policy mistake, arguing that a gradual Fed may have to hike higher later, raising recession risk. Warsh's defenders say the data due before December will settle things, and could even show productivity rising enough to permit growth without inflation.

The next meeting is in December. Until then, the most reliable forecasting tool at the Fed is the schedule of speeches by people who say they do not speak for the chairman.
