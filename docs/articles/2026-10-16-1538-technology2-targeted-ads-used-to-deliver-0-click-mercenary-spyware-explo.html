Spyware Firm Discovers Cheapest Way to Reach Journalists: Buying an Ad

Citizen Lab says an Israeli ad broker delivered zero-click spyware to 45 Al Jazeera devices, in what it believes is the first confirmed case of its kind.

The wire said: Targeted ads delivered 0-click exploits to install mercenary malware · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 16, 2026 15:38 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Citizen Lab, a research group at the University of Toronto, published findings on a campaign it named AdFection, in which mercenary hackers allegedly hid zero-click exploits inside targeted ads. The ads reached iOS and Android devices through websites and installed apps.

Why it matters

If a malicious ad can infect a phone without a single tap, the usual advice to avoid suspicious links stops working. Journalists are the first named targets, but the same ad system reaches everyone else.

Researchers at Citizen Lab said Thursday that mercenary hackers placed zero-click exploits inside targeted online ads, infecting phones that merely loaded a website or opened an app. According to Ars Technica, which reported the findings, this is likely the first confirmed case of zero-click mercenary spyware delivered through targeted advertising. It is a notable achievement in an industry that already sells surveillance as a subscription service.

Translation: a zero-click exploit is an attack that needs no cooperation from the victim. Normal security training tells people not to click on strange links, which assumes the attacker needs a click. Here the ad did the clicking on the user's behalf, in the sense that it simply showed up.

Citizen Lab said it confirmed that the campaign, which it named AdFection, infected 45 devices at the international media organization Al Jazeera in 2024 and 2025. The attacks are invisible unless a user runs special forensic hardware or software, which is not a standard feature of the average newsroom laptop policy. The researchers called the case a clear warning to news organizations that they may be actively targeted, both in newsrooms and as individual journalists, editors and staff.

The ads came from Discover-Tech, an Israeli company that presents itself as a legitimate advertising business. Citizen Lab says it functions as a facilitator for multiple mercenary spyware partners, and described its role as a bridge between ad vendors and the mercenary spyware ecosystem. The researchers said that appears to be the company's primary purpose, which is an unusual line item for a firm that also offers to help publishers monetize their traffic.

The mechanism is the ordinary one. Advertisers bid for the right to show an ad to people in a given demographic, and brokers build detailed profiles from mobile IDs, location, IP addresses and other breadcrumbs. Translation: the same machinery that lets a mattress company find tired people in Ohio can, with a different customer, find a specific reporter. Nothing about the system had to be broken. It only had to be used as designed by someone with a different shopping list.

The scale is the unsettling part. Citizen Lab said it obtained more than 4 billion bidding records handled by Discover-Tech, showing its ads appeared in over 70,000 mobile apps and more than 120,000 websites between March and June 2026. The report notes that the risk extends beyond potential victims to states and civil society at large. Those 4 billion records also suggest that precise targeting, rather than carpet bombing, was the whole point.

What to watch next is whether ad platforms, browser makers and app stores decide that vetting who is allowed to bid is part of their job. So far the ad industry's position on who buys the space has been that the auction is very fast and therefore no one can be asked.
