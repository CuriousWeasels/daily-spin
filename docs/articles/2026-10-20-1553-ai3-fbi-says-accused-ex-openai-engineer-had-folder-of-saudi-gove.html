Accused OpenAI Bugger Stays in Jail; Folder Named 'Saudi Gov Docs' Did Him No Favors

A court denied release for former OpenAI engineer Abdulrahman Alfozan, accused of bugging a secure conference room, after prosecutors cited a trove of one-way tickets and one very candid folder name.

Catch up

Abdulrahman Alfozan, a former OpenAI engineer, is accused of bugging a top-secret conference room at the AI lab to steal its secrets. He has not been convicted, and prosecutors say he acted either for personal business gain or to benefit Saudi Arabia.

Why it matters

The case shows that the most valuable assets at frontier AI labs are now treated as matters of national security, and that rival governments are accused of competing for them. It also hints at how much trust, and how many badge readers, the industry will now need.

On Monday, a court granted the US government's request to keep Abdulrahman Alfozan in detention while it builds its case. Alfozan, a former OpenAI engineer, is accused of planting a listening device in a top-secret conference room at the company. Ars Technica reported the details from a filing by US attorney Craig Missakian, who argued Alfozan should not be released before trial.

Prosecutors say Alfozan did it either for personal business gain or to help the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. Translation: the government has not fully committed to a motive, but it is confident that the bug was not there for the acoustics.

The evidence, according to the filing, includes WhatsApp chats between Alfozan and a member of the Saudi royal family, along with his efforts to find a way to move permanently to Saudi Arabia during what the government called the country's biggest AI push yet. Shortly before his arrest, the FBI says, he opened a folder on his iPhone called "Saudi Gov Docs." The same phone allegedly held photos of a second bug he planted in the offices. Experienced spies reportedly favor filenames like "Vacation_2019," but the government is not obliged to grade on style.

The flight-risk argument is where the story turns into a travel itinerary. Prosecutors say Alfozan had booked a one-way ticket to Riyadh for himself, his wife and their baby, allegedly paid for by the Saudi government. The FBI arrested him days before the flight. His wife and son then booked an earlier flight and left the US without him.

The government then says a member of the Saudi military booked and paid for yet another one-way ticket for the wife and son, this one in business class to Doha, Qatar. Translation: flight risk, in legal terms, is the likelihood a defendant will leave. Here the family has already demonstrated the technique on a small scale.

Prosecutors added that Alfozan, a Saudi national and US permanent legal resident, earned significant compensation from a decade of high-tech work in the Bay Area. They say that leaves him with ample resources, and at least two bank accounts in Saudi Arabia. The Bay Area's salary scale, in other words, is now being entered into evidence against the person who earned it.

To argue that such help is not hypothetical, the government pointed to the earlier case of former Twitter employees charged with spying for the Saudi government. Charges remain pending against one of them, Ali Alzabarah, the US said, partly because a Saudi foreign official helped coordinate a next-day flight to Saudi Arabia shortly after Twitter confronted him. The precedent is not flattering to the argument that this is a one-off.

Alfozan has not been convicted, and the claims so far come from the prosecution's filing rather than a trial. What to watch next is the defense's response, and whether any AI lab starts checking conference rooms the way it checks model weights.
