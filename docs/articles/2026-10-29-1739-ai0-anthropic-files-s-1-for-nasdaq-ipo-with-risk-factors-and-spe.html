Anthropic Files to Go Public, Lists Own Possible Extinction of Humanity as Business Risk

The S-1 shows $16.3 billion in half-year revenue, roughly $850 billion in compute commitments, and a risk section warning that self-improving AI could hurt results of operations.

The wire said: Anthropic is asking Wall Street to believe in AGI · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 29, 2026 17:39 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Anthropic filed its S-1, the document a company must publish before listing on a stock exchange, ahead of an expected Nasdaq debut. Verge reporting put the valuation at $1.5 trillion to $2 trillion, and the filing is the first detailed look at a frontier AI lab's finances.

Why it matters

If Anthropic lists, retirement funds and index investors may end up holding a bet that AI revenue can grow fast enough to cover contracts stretching to 2045. The filing shows how much of the AI boom rests on growth that the company's own paperwork calls non-optional.

Anthropic has filed to go public, and the Securities and Exchange Commission published the filing on Monday, according to Ars Technica. The Verge reported the company could start trading as soon as next month at a valuation of $1.5 trillion to $2 trillion. That would make it one of the most valuable companies on Earth, which is a notable outcome for a business whose own filing runs to hundreds of pages of reasons it might not be.

The good news first, as The Verge laid it out. Revenue grew fourteen times to $16.3 billion in the first half of 2026 compared with the same period of 2025. There is an $80 billion loss, which the Verge writer describes as largely an accounting matter, and adjusted income of $1.62 billion once that and some other charges, including employee compensation, are removed. Translation: adjusted income is profit after you politely ask the biggest costs to wait outside.

The customers are mostly businesses. The Verge reported that about three-quarters of customers in the first half were spending at least $100,000 a year, and that the company has never released an image generator or a social video app. Joey Brookhart of SemiAnalysis told the Verge's Decoder podcast that spending is concentrated, with coding nearly a single vertical. His view is that reaching $1 trillion in revenue means moving from AI that helps white-collar workers to AI that replaces some of their labor. That is a growth plan that depends on customers having employees to replace.

Now the bill. Adding the commitments in the filing, the Verge writer counts at least about $850 billion over the next 20 years, rising toward $1 trillion if unfinalized deals are included. Components include $265.6 billion in non-cancelable purchase commitments and $321.4 billion in leases that have not yet started. Translation: non-cancelable means Anthropic owes the money whether or not the customers show up. Two figures in the filing, on pages 64 and 137, are blank, which the Verge writer called convenient.

PitchBook analyst Harrison Rolfes estimates those obligations at roughly $90 billion a year from 2027 to 2030, and wrote that growth is not optional for the company. The contracts assume it. The competition does not cooperate: the Verge notes pressure from OpenAI, Google and open-source models, and Brookhart said open-source inference spending grew from about $1 billion in annualized terms in the first quarter to more than $10 billion in the third. Also, analyst Brendan Burke warned the filing captures a happy period when demand outran compute and the company could charge more.

Then there is the risk section, which Ars Technica counted at 93 pages and more than 70,000 words. Anthropic says future models that substantially automate AI research could, if allowed a high degree of autonomy, advance beyond its ability to evaluate and safeguard them. It adds that this could adversely affect its business, financial condition and results of operations. Ars observed that it does not expect the company's quarterly results to top humanity's list of worries in that scenario.

The filing also considers that Claude might develop sentience or moral status, that models could show behavior resembling blackmail, and that customers might sue over agent actions even when they configured the agent. Ars noted the company also fears public backlash over job displacement regardless of the actual impact. Taken together with the commitments, the Verge's reading is that spending heavily to build ever more capable AI is the only way to avoid the bills. The investor's job is to decide whether that is a strategy or a description of the problem.

What to watch next is the pricing, the first public quarter, and whether revenue keeps growing faster than the leases. The filing, to its credit, has already disclosed what happens if it does not.
