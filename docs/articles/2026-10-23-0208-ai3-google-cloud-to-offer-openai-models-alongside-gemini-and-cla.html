Google Cloud Adds OpenAI Models, Now Sells Its Own Rivals Under One Roof

Google becomes the only major cloud provider offering Gemini, Claude and GPT on a single bill, a convenience that also funds the competition.

The wire said: Google Cloud now offers OpenAI models alongside Gemini and Claude, becoming the only major provider with all three · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 23, 2026 02:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Google Cloud has added OpenAI's GPT-6 and 6.1 models (Astra, Sol and Luna) to a catalog that already carried Google's Gemini and Anthropic's Claude. OpenAI says Microsoft remains its primary cloud partner.

Why it matters

Businesses with existing Google Cloud contracts can try rival models without new vendor paperwork, which lowers the cost of switching away from Gemini. It also shows that the biggest AI labs now compete and rent each other computers at the same time.

Google Cloud announced that it now offers OpenAI's models alongside its own Gemini and Anthropic's Claude, which Google says makes it the only large cloud provider carrying all three leading closed models. According to The Decoder, three models are available: GPT-6 Astra for demanding work such as coding and research, GPT-6.1 Sol as a cheaper option with similar performance, and GPT-6 Luna as a fast, low-cost choice for everyday tasks. Google, in other words, has opened a store that sells its competitors' best products, and it expects to be thanked for the selection.

The rollout is a private preview for select enterprise customers, with sign-ups open, The Decoder reported. Ars Technica said the models will reach Google's Model Garden in the coming weeks. Translation: Model Garden is a catalog of more than 200 AI models, and it has fewer flowers than the name suggests and more invoices. The sources differ slightly on timing: one says availability starts today for preview users, the other says wider arrival comes over the coming weeks.

The models will also appear in Gemini for Business and in Antigravity, Google's agent-powered coding tool. Ars Technica called the Antigravity arrival perhaps the more important part. Translation: an agent-powered coding tool is software that writes and edits code on its own, and Google has decided it should be able to do so using someone else's brain if the customer prefers.

The billing is where the pitch gets practical. Charges run through a single Google Cloud bill and count toward existing spending commitments, according to The Decoder, and Ars Technica notes that transactions go through the Google Cloud Marketplace. Translation: many companies have promised Google a certain amount of annual spending, and anything bought through the marketplace helps them meet it. A company can therefore adopt GPT without opening a new vendor relationship, which procurement departments regard as the real technological breakthrough. Google also says customer data will not be used for training.

Ars Technica adds that Astra will naturally cost the most to run, but Google's system can shift workloads between models based on performance, latency and efficiency. In plain terms, the platform can send easy jobs to cheap models and hard jobs to expensive ones, much like a law firm sending the photocopying to an intern.

The strategic awkwardness is not hidden. The Decoder noted a recurring tension at Google: computing capacity used to serve cloud customers can strengthen the biggest rivals of its own Gemini team. It said this conflict reportedly even pushed researcher Noam Shazeer, described as a two-billion-dollar talent, back to OpenAI. That detail was reported as hearsay, and the sources do not elaborate.

The logic for Google Cloud is that a landlord does not need to like every tenant, only to collect rent. The logic for Gemini is less comfortable, since every customer who tries GPT on Google's servers is a customer Gemini has to win back. Watch whether the preview turns into broad availability, and whether Google's own model gets any shelf space in the cloud it built.
