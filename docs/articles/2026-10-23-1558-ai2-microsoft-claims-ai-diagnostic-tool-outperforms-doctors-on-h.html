Microsoft Says Its AI Out-Diagnoses Doctors, Pending Contact With Actual Patients

A study in Science reports the system hit 83 percent on hard cases versus 36 percent for generalist physicians, but the doctors had one hand tied and the patients were all on paper.

The wire said: Microsoft says its AI tool is better than doctors at diagnosing conditions · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 23, 2026 15:58 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Microsoft AI, led by Mustafa Suleyman, published a study in Science on a system called MAI-DxO, which has several AI agents act like a panel of doctors that questions a patient, orders tests and proposes a diagnosis. Microsoft calls it a step toward "medical superintelligence."

Why it matters

If AI diagnosis works outside a benchmark, it could ease the shortage of medical expertise and change how hospitals work. Until it is tested on real patients, the numbers are a promising demonstration and not a medical result.

Microsoft said Thursday that its diagnostic AI correctly solved 83 percent of complex cases drawn from The New England Journal of Medicine, according to both The Verge and TechCrunch. Generalist physicians working with reference materials got about 36 percent, and those working without them about 22 percent, The Verge reported. The company published the findings in Science, one of the most prestigious journals in the field, which is a good place to put a press release if you want it read carefully.

The system, MAI-DxO, is what the researchers call a diagnostic orchestrator. Translation: instead of one chatbot guessing, several AI agents take turns playing doctor, debating the symptoms, deciding which tests to order and settling on an answer. It is a medical committee, minus the parking dispute and the lunch order.

On emergency department cases from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, The Verge reported the system scored 80 percent against an average of 47 percent for human physicians. It also tended to reach its answers at a lower estimated cost. That cost is the price of running the AI, and it is not a bill anyone has yet agreed to send to a patient.

The caveats arrive quickly, and to Microsoft's credit, some of them come from Microsoft. TechCrunch noted that cases this hard are normally handled by specialists or teams of physicians, who were not in the comparison. The doctors who were tested were primary care doctors and general internists working individually. Suleyman himself said the tool "needs clinical validation," and the system has only been run on past cases.

The study's own data also complicates the headline. TechCrunch reported that much of the improvement comes from the powerful models underneath, with the orchestration adding gains on top. GPT-5 alone scored 78.9 percent on complex cases at about $8,400 per case. Microsoft's layer cut roughly $3,000 from that but added only about four points of accuracy. On emergency cases, there was little improvement in either accuracy or cost. So the committee helps most on hard cases and weaker models, and mostly confirms what a strong model already knew.

Microsoft's plan is to move from paper to people. Through a partnership with Beth Israel, patients will receive standard care while those who consent also interact with the AI, and the company will compare its recommendations with the human doctors'. Dominic King, Microsoft's AI health lead, told The Verge the testing should begin in the next couple of months, though neither he nor Suleyman would commit to a firm timeline. Google published its own clinical study of a diagnostic AI with Beth Israel in The Lancet this month, so the hospital is becoming a popular venue for robots auditioning as colleagues.

Commercial intentions are not hidden. Microsoft already sells dictation and radiology tools to clinicians, and Suleyman told TechCrunch the company needs the top clinical decision support products within a few years. He also predicts healthcare models will improve by an order of magnitude in 24 to 36 months, while insisting human clinicians will always have a central role. He acknowledged AI could even end up giving doctors more work, though he hopes it removes some of "the drudgery."

The next test is the Boston emergency department on a Friday evening, which King himself described as a place of complexity and diversity. Unlike a case report, the patients there will not wait for the committee to finish deliberating.
