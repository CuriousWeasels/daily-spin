Tesla Cedes EV Crown, Pivots to Selling Cars That Mostly Drive Themselves, Supervised

With BYD now the world's largest EV maker, Tesla is betting its future on Full Self-Driving subscriptions and a possible merger with SpaceX.

The wire said: Tesla gave up the EV crown. Now it wants to win the autonomy race · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 26, 2026 17:43 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Tesla lost its position as the world's largest electric vehicle maker to BYD last year, and Elon Musk has since floated a possible merger with SpaceX, days after a flying Roadster stunt. The Ars Technica report, drawing on Financial Times reporting, describes how Tesla is staking its future on autonomous driving.

Why it matters

If carmakers succeed in turning cars into software subscriptions, the vehicle you buy may become the cheaper part of what you pay for over its life. A Tesla-SpaceX merger would also concentrate more of Musk's businesses, and more of his risk, in one company.

Tesla, which spent years as the undisputed leader in electric vehicles, is now trying to win a different race: autonomy. According to Ars Technica, citing the Financial Times, Tesla ceded its top EV spot to BYD last year and is now leaning on Full Self-Driving software, a product whose name describes an aspiration more than a feature.

Translation: Full Self-Driving still requires a human to sit in the driver's seat and pay full attention. Tesla's supervised version costs $99 a month in the US, which makes it a subscription for the privilege of supervising.

The numbers explain the enthusiasm. Musk's earlier goal of selling 20 million vehicles a year by 2030 has been trimmed to 5 million, against 1.6 million delivered last year. Tesla said on Wednesday that it has its largest order backlog in four years, that more than 75 percent of new North American Teslas are sold with supervised FSD, and that it has 1.7 million active FSD subscriptions, up 67 percent from a year earlier. Tesla has not disclosed how much recurring revenue that produces.

Analysts see why the software matters. Dan Ives of Yorkville Ives called it the "golden goose" for automakers because the margins are so high. He argued that expanding FSD from roughly 10-15 percent of customers to about 50 percent would be worth more than selling an extra 50,000 to 100,000 cars a year, because markets reward each FSD dollar 50-70 percent more than a dollar from a car sale. Translation: investors prefer money that arrives monthly and does not need a factory.

There is a catch, which is regulators. Tesla has yet to win approval for a fully autonomous version of FSD. The supervised version is approved in the Netherlands and several other European countries, but a vote of EU member states on wider rollout has been delayed until December. Xpeng, the Volkswagen-backed Chinese rival, is working with German authorities and aims for EU approval of its XNGP system after new rules are expected early next year. Its general counsel said, "We aim to be one of the first."

Cars still pay the bills for now, generating 75 percent of Tesla's revenue, and Tesla needs heavy AI spending to compete with Big Tech. That is where the SpaceX idea comes in. Musk said the companies were exploring a merger, and at the flying Roadster event he promised results from combining "the world's best car company and the world's best rocket company." Supporters cite shared work on materials, AI, manufacturing and chip access. One top-15 Tesla shareholder told the FT he backed it for the AI innovations at SpaceX.

William Blair analyst Jed Dorsheimer said a deal, if approved by shareholders and regulators, could give Musk 50 percent or more of the combined company. Critics see the risk in the same place. Tesla enthusiast Max Willson warned that combining the businesses risks tying their futures to Musk's most speculative bets, and that trouble in AI could spread to otherwise strong businesses.

Rivals are watching and copying. GM hired former Tesla executive Sterling Anderson to lead product development, while BMW and Mercedes-Benz have partnered with Qualcomm and Nvidia on "Level 2 plus" systems that still require an attentive driver. Volvo's chief executive said Tesla is "probably leading the development in the West." The industry's consensus appears to be that the future is autonomous, subscription-based, and a few regulatory votes away.

Watch the EU vote in December. Until then, the car of the future remains one that drives itself while you keep your eyes on it.
