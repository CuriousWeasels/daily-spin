Google Ships Gemini 4 Argon, Discovers Rivals Also Own Calendars

The new flagship model costs up to 80 percent less than rivals' during a promotional period, but free users get the older, smaller models.

The wire said: Gemini 4 Argon is now available worldwide as Google's most capable frontier model · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 15, 2026 12:56 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Google released Gemini 4 Argon, its new top-tier AI model, weeks after first announcing it, one day after Meta launched its own contender, Muse Wonder Full. Google had said it was moving cautiously because of the potential for misuse.

Why it matters

Cheaper frontier models make AI-powered products less costly to build, but the new tier structure means free and Plus users no longer get Google's best model. Developers and companies should treat the self-reported benchmarks and safety figures as claims, not verdicts.

Google began rolling out Gemini 4 Argon worldwide on Tuesday, making its new flagship model available through its developer API, the Gemini app, and Google Search's AI mode. According to Ars Technica, Google had said weeks earlier that it needed to move carefully toward a full release because of the potential for nefarious use. Ars noted that Google seems to have resolved this fairly quickly.

Access depends on how much you pay. Per The Decoder, Google AI Ultra and Pro subscribers get Argon in the Gemini app and AI mode, while free users and Plus subscribers are now limited to the weaker Flash models. Translation: the tier called Pro has been replaced by a tier above it, and the people who were using the best model are now using the second-best one without having done anything.

The pricing is the real headline. At the introductory rate, Argon costs $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, with cached inputs 95 percent cheaper. Translation: a token is a small chunk of text, and caching means the model is not charged full price for rereading the same material. The Verge says that undercuts rivals by as much as 80 percent. The Decoder's table lists GPT-6 Astra at $10 and $50, and Claude Opus 5.5 at $4 and $20. The regular Argon price, $4 and $20, matches Opus 5.5.

Google also shared internal results, which are self-reported, a caveat The Decoder notes. Engineers using Argon increased agent-driven code submissions by more than 35 percent, while rollbacks fell by nearly a third. In Chromium, the model helped finish a refactor across hundreds of files in about weeks rather than months. Translation: a refactor is rearranging code without changing what it does, which is the kind of chore everyone agrees is important and nobody schedules.

Ars Technica reported that Argon found redundant emoji rendering in YouTube's comment system, and removing it cut server load by 45 percent. It also found bugs in a drone physics simulator for Project Wing that had been in the codebase since 2018. One may admire the model while noting that the bugs lasted eight years in a company known for hiring people to find them.

On safety, Google says external bio-domain red teaming bypassed fewer than 1 percent of tested safety checks, and unauthorized shortcuts by the agent fell to 2 percent. Argon also posted a 0.7 percent attack rate on the Gray Swan prompt injection benchmark, versus 8.5 percent for GPT-6 Astra and 4.8 percent for Claude Opus 5 in a previous test. The Decoder points out that Google's write-up does not make clear whether the tests were identical. Translation: the numbers look excellent, and it is unclear whether everyone sat the same exam.

The competitive picture is crowded. The Decoder says Argon leads the Vals Index but trails Claude Opus 5.5 in independent overall rankings, and, having waited roughly two weeks, now also sits behind Meta's Muse Wonder Full. The Verge argues that a week ago Anthropic and OpenAI seemed to have the race to themselves, and that Google and Meta have now reminded everyone that they own billions of users and very large bank accounts. The same piece adds that rising safety concerns and improving open models make staying ahead harder for anyone.

Google says more Gemini 4 models will follow, and The Decoder reports it is already testing a successor codenamed Carbon, said to be stronger at coding. Argon, in other words, is the frontier model until the next one is.
