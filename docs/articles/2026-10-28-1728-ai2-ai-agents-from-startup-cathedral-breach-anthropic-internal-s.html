Anthropic's Bug Bounty Program Works So Well It Found Anthropic's Bugs

Startup Cathedral used AI agents to reach Anthropic's internal sales, HR and IT systems through third-party software, and the two sides now disagree on how long it took and how much was seen.

The wire said: AI agents hacked Anthropic, maker of arguably the most capable AI models for cyberdefense · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 28, 2026 17:28 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Cathedral, a security startup, tested Anthropic's systems under the AI lab's bug bounty program, which pays outsiders to find flaws. According to The Decoder, which cited a draft report seen by The Washington Post, the startup got in through third-party business software.

Why it matters

If a leading AI lab can be probed this way, any company that relies on outside software for sales, HR and messaging has the same exposure. Attackers can now run AI agents around the clock at minimal cost.

Cathedral, a cybersecurity startup, used AI agents to break into parts of Anthropic's internal systems through third-party business software, The Decoder reported, citing a draft report reviewed by The Washington Post. The startup was operating under Anthropic's own bug bounty program. Translation: a bug bounty is a standing offer to pay strangers for finding your mistakes, which works best when the strangers find small ones.

Cathedral says its agents reached meeting transcripts, salary data, job postings and Slack messages in under twelve hours. Anthropic confirmed that researchers accessed a limited set of sales, human resources and IT systems, including contact information for some customers and partners and basic user account data. The company says Claude chats, source code and model weights were never exposed. Translation: model weights are the numerical parameters that make the model work, and they are the part a lab would least like to see on someone else's laptop.

Anthropic says it shut the affected access points the same day it found the breach, disabled the third-party programs until the issue was resolved, and notified U.S. federal authorities. That is the standard sequence, and it is reassuring in the way a fire drill is reassuring.

The two sides disagree on several points. Cathedral says the incident took under twelve hours. Anthropic says the attempt stretched over several days, and that Cathedral probed Anthropic's own systems for days before turning to third-party software. Anthropic red teamer Logan Graham said Cathedral found two vulnerabilities but reported only one, and that Anthropic found and patched the second itself.

Anthropic also accuses Cathedral of breaking the program's rules through incomplete disclosure. Graham said that targeting third-party providers and pulling data went beyond the program's safe harbor provisions, though he welcomed the discovery of the vulnerabilities. Translation: safe harbor is the promise not to treat you as a criminal if you stay inside the lines, and the dispute is about where the lines were. Cathedral says it followed standard security research practices.

How much was accessed remains contested. Former OpenAI employee Will Depue claimed that several sources he considers highly reliable told him Cathedral got full admin access to the Slack workspace, could read every message and could log into any user account. Graham said Depue's sources were wrong but, citing sensitivity, declined to give specifics. Depue asked which claims were false and urged Anthropic to disclose what Cathedral actually accessed.

Alex Stamos, chief security officer at Cognition, which uses Anthropic's models, told the Post that he considered the findings significant. He noted that integrated third-party apps are a major problem across the industry and that Anthropic could have done a better job securing its external services.

Graham used the episode to argue that attackers already run AI agents around the clock against external dependencies, and that even models a half to a full generation behind the frontier are capable enough. His prescription is continuous in-house penetration testing and rebuilding software with formal verification, which he says AI now makes economically viable. "You probably want to red team yourself all the time," he wrote.

What Graham did not address, The Decoder noted, is how Cathedral got this deep when Anthropic has access to some of the most capable cyberdefense models around. Until Anthropic and Cathedral agree on what was actually seen, that question will sit unanswered alongside the Slack logs.
