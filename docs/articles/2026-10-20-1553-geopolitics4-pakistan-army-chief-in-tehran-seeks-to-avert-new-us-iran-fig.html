Pakistan's Army Chief Flies to Tehran, Fifth Time, to Mediate Between Two Parties He Also Has Obligations To

Field Marshal Asim Munir's latest trip comes as a US carrier heads east, a 14-point deal lapses, and Islamabad's roles as broker and Saudi defender grow harder to reconcile.

The wire said: Pakistan army chief in Tehran: Can diplomacy prevent new US-Iran fighting? · Al Jazeera

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 20, 2026 15:53 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Pakistan brokered an April 8 ceasefire after the US and Israel began striking Iran on February 28, then hosted talks that produced a 14-point memorandum in June. That deal's 60-day window expired in mid-August with the Strait of Hormuz still closed and the US blockade still in place.

Why it matters

The Strait of Hormuz remains shut and a new round of fighting is being openly discussed, so whether mediators can restart talks affects shipping, energy markets and the safety of the Gulf region. The mediator's own security commitments may be undercutting its ability to mediate.

Pakistan's army chief, Field Marshal Asim Munir, arrived in Tehran on Tuesday evening for talks with Iranian officials, according to Al Jazeera, citing Iran's state news agency IRNA. It is his fifth visit to Iran since the April ceasefire that Islamabad brokered. Interior Minister Mohsin Naqvi came along, which suggests the agenda is long enough to need a second person to hold the folder.

The trip follows a Monday visit by Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov, his first to Tehran since the war began in February. Seyed Mojtaba Jalazadeh, a Tehran-based analyst, told Al Jazeera that the timing suggests Iran prefers several diplomatic channels to a single mediator. Translation: when you are in a war, it is prudent to have more than one person to call.

The urgency is easy to explain. Al Jazeera reports that President Donald Trump rejected an Iranian proposal to end the war on September 26, and the US has since sent an additional aircraft carrier, expected by late November. Trump has also endorsed Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu ahead of Israel's October 27 election, and said that if Netanyahu were not re-elected, the US would need to attack Iran on its own. This is an unusual way to describe an election, as a deciding factor in whether a third country gets bombed.

Then there is the paperwork. The June memorandum of understanding, known as the Islamabad MoU, said the US would lift its naval blockade within 30 days, Hormuz would reopen, and roughly $24bn in frozen Iranian assets would be released in phases over 60 days of further talks, including on Iran's nuclear programme. The window closed in mid-August. Iran says the US did not keep its commitments and refuses to reopen the strait until the blockade ends. Each side is waiting for the other to go first, a sequencing problem that diplomats can spend years on.

Experts disagree about whether the document still counts. Andreas Krieg of King's College London called it politically alive but operationally dead. Muhammad Faisal of the University of Technology Sydney said it remains the minimum agreed benchmark, and that neither side has an obvious alternative. Pakistan's Foreign Office, unsurprisingly, says it is still in place. Qatar, meanwhile, is doing much of the detailed negotiating, while Pakistan supplies political access, in Krieg's account.

Pakistan's complication is that it is also a security partner of Saudi Arabia. Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Turkiye signed the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement on August 7, and on October 5 agreed to speed up deployments. Within days, Houthi missiles and drones hit targets in Saudi Arabia, including Riyadh's airport more than once. Iran-backed is the usual description of the Houthis; Iran, per Foreign Minister Ishaq Dar, told him it has nothing to do with them.

Pakistan says its forces will not cross into Yemen to attack the Houthis. Its military spokesman also said that if talks fail and conflict follows, the alliance's collective deterrence would come into play. Krieg argues Pakistan's credibility as a neutral actor is increasingly compromised. Jalazadeh says Tehran sees the pact less as a threat than as a complicating and constraining factor, which is how one describes a neighbour with a very large guest list.

What to watch: whether Munir leaves Tehran with anything more than a sixth visit on the calendar, and whether the carrier arrives before the diplomacy does.
