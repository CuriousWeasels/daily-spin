AI Bubble Gets 10% Odds on Polymarket, 100% Odds of Being Discussed at Lunch

Traders bet on a pre-New Year burst, Ken Griffin calls it a bubble but says keep holding, and neocloud executives insist rental prices are rising.

The wire said: Polymarket traders see a 10% chance the AI bubble bursts before New Year’s Day · MarketWatch

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 21, 2026 16:10 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Investors have spent months debating whether AI spending has outrun AI profits. Neoclouds, firms that borrow heavily to buy Nvidia chips and rent out the computing power, are now the focus of that debate after stress showed up in CoreWeave's debt markets.

Why it matters

If AI computing capacity keeps renting at high prices, the debt behind the data center buildout looks manageable. If it does not, the pain would reach anyone whose index fund quietly became an AI fund, which is most people with a retirement account.

MarketWatch reported that traders on the prediction market Polymarket see roughly a 10% chance that the AI bubble bursts before New Year's Day. Translation: a prediction market is a place where people stake money on outcomes, so a number like 10% is the crowd's price for being right about disaster. It is a modest figure, which suggests the crowd is nervous but has a dinner reservation in December.

Separately, MarketWatch reported that Citadel's Ken Griffin thinks AI stocks are probably in a bubble, yet is not telling investors to dump them. This is a coherent position held by many professionals: the thing is overvalued, and also the thing may keep going up. Translation: he is describing the weather, not canceling the picnic.

The stress point is the neoclouds. CNBC reported that these companies are investing billions to buy Nvidia's graphics processing units (GPUs, the chips that train and run AI models), build data centers and rent out the capacity. Investors grew wary after the spread on CoreWeave's five-year credit default swaps reached levels generally viewed as signs of distress, and its bond yields climbed. Translation: a credit default swap is insurance against a borrower failing to pay, and when the insurance gets pricey, the market is quietly raising an eyebrow.

Enter the executives, who were reassuring. Nebius chief revenue officer Marc Boroditsky told CNBC that his firm is not in CoreWeave's position because it is not carrying that kind of debt. He said Nebius would sell out if it brought its 2027 capacity online, and that 2028 could look similar. Asked whether price per megawatt, which measures what a company can charge for a given amount of computing capacity, would keep rising faster than expected, he answered: "Yeah." It is the most efficient earnings-call answer on record, and also the one least likely to be audited.

Crusoe CEO Chase Lochmiller offered the sharper evidence. He said Crusoe sees higher prices for older A100 chips than in 2020 or 2021, and higher prices for H100s than when they were first deployed in 2023. This matters because investors have been arguing over how quickly GPUs lose economic value as newer chips arrive. If old chips rent for more, the borrowed money looks wiser. If the claim comes only from firms that sell the rentals, readers may wish to note who is holding the tape measure.

SpaceX supplied the day's lift. CNBC reported that SpaceX said revenue at its AI business jumped ninefold to $7.56 billion, and CFO Bret Johnsen said pricing is tracking slightly above the high end of a $30 to $50 per watt range. Nebius stock rose, and CoreWeave, Iren and Applied Digital gained in premarket trading. Boroditsky said supply continues to fall far behind demand, a sentence that is bullish when said by the people selling supply.

For context, SPY, the exchange-traded fund tracking the S&P 500, stood at 772.45 as of October 21, 2026. The bubble question, then, is being asked of an index that has plenty of room to fall. The 10% odds on Polymarket will be the number to watch, along with whether CoreWeave's insurance gets cheaper.
