Ebola Reaches Nigeria, and Three Governments Now Disagree on Where the Patient Has Been

Switzerland says the woman never went to Congo, Uganda says it has no active cases, and investigators are studying one airport on one day in October.

The wire said: Mystery over source of Ebola infection in Nigeria fuels concern about spread of virus · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 23, 2026 15:58 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

An Ebola outbreak caused by the Bundibugyo virus was announced in DR Congo on 15 May and has since spread to Uganda, Kenya and now Nigeria. It has infected more than 9,500 people and killed nearly half of them, and there is no approved vaccine or treatment.

Why it matters

Contact tracing only works if countries agree on where a patient has been, and right now they do not. With no vaccine for this strain, accurate travel histories are among the few tools health officials have.

Nigeria confirmed its first Ebola case of the current outbreak this week, in the capital, Abuja. By Friday, the main question was not how the patient was doing, which health officials describe as stable, but where she had been, which three governments are describing differently.

The patient is a Swiss national of Ugandan origin, married to a Swiss diplomat posted in Abuja. According to DW, Nigerian Health Minister Muhammad Ali Pate said she developed symptoms on 7 October, visited a private clinic on 11 October, and was later confirmed positive and isolated. Officials have identified 44 contacts, including health workers and caregivers.

Uganda's Health Ministry initially said preliminary findings showed she had travelled to eastern DR Congo for business, passed through Uganda without symptoms and returned to Nigeria. Switzerland's foreign ministry told Reuters the opposite: she and her husband did not go to Congo, and she was believed to have caught the virus while travelling in Uganda. DW notes that the Swiss assessment has not been independently confirmed. The WHO and Africa CDC said they had no confirmation of Congo travel.

Uganda, for its part, says it has no active Ebola cases. The Guardian reported that the country declared cases in May and June and was certified Ebola-free by the WHO in August. A Ugandan spokesperson told Reuters that if there were cases, the government would make them public. Translation: the government is confident it has no cases, which is a different statement from the disease having no cases.

Then there is the airport. Africa CDC's Wessam Mankoula said the Nigerian patient and Kenya's first confirmed patient, who died in Nairobi on 5 October, both passed through Entebbe International Airport on 3 October. Investigators are checking whether their movements overlapped. Catherine Smallwood of the WHO said a link was possible but that officials must first compare timing with when symptoms began.

Not everyone is persuaded. Dutch virologist Marion Koopmans, a member of the WHO's Ebola emergency committee, called the theory "very speculative" without evidence of shared space. She said random presence at a large airport is not a supported epidemiological link. Translation: two people being in the same building does not count as contact, because otherwise every airport would be an outbreak.

The timing matters because of how Ebola behaves. Symptoms appear between 2 and 21 days after infection, and according to the WHO, people are not considered infectious until symptoms start. A traveller can therefore pass through screening looking perfectly healthy, which DW notes means even well-run border systems cannot catch everyone.

Kenya has its own gap. According to the Daily Nation, health officials are looking for a Ugandan man listed as a contact of the deceased Kenyan patient. Health Director-General Patrick Amoth said they know his name and passport number but cannot reach him, and the man was reportedly using a South Sudanese phone number. Being untraced does not mean he is infected, though finding exposed people is the whole point of contact tracing.

Pate said Nigeria's case remains contained and tracing continues. Residents in Abuja told DW they are worried, and one noted that many people are already preoccupied with simply surviving economically. The outbreak is already the most geographically widespread on record, though second to the 2014-16 West African epidemic in size, which infected more than 28,600 people.

What to watch next is dull but decisive: symptom-onset dates, airport records and the missing contact in Kenya. The virus has no interest in whose version of the itinerary is correct, but the people trying to stop it do.
