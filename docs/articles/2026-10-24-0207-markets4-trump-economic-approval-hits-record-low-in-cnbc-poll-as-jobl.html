Americans Rate Economy Poor; Stock Market and Jobless Claims Beg to Differ

Trump's economic approval hit a CNBC survey low of 34% as jobless claims neared 1960s lows, a gap that mostly comes down to the price of groceries.

Catch up

CNBC's latest All-America Economic Survey, released less than two weeks before the Nov. 3 midterms, found record-low economic approval for President Trump. Separately, weekly jobless claims fell to 194,000, close to the lowest in nearly six decades.

Why it matters

A labor market that is stable but not hiring, combined with inflation well above target, means many households feel prices more than they feel job security. That feeling is now shaping an election and possibly the Fed's next rate move.

Eight out of 10 Americans now rate the economy as fair or poor, the highest share since December 2023, according to the CNBC All-America Economic Survey released Thursday. President Trump's economic approval fell to 34% from 38% in July, while disapproval rose to 65%. CNBC said that is the highest disapproval for any president it has surveyed going back to Barack Obama, a bipartisan achievement of sorts.

Translation: net approval is approval minus disapproval, and his net economic figure is now -31. His overall approval is steadier at 40%, with 59% disapproving. In other words, voters are willing to hold a mixed view of the president in general but have settled on a firm one about his handling of the economy.

The more interesting movement is inside his own party. Pessimism among Republicans rose 6 points to 35%, the highest of either Trump term. Micah Roberts, the survey's Republican pollster, said the shift happened "because Republicans have" soured, not because Democrats or independents did. When your own pollster delivers that line in a CNBC write-up, it is rarely a compliment.

The cause is not hard to find. Eighty percent of respondents say prices are rising, and 55% say they are rising faster than usual. Food now worries people more than gas, 34% to 23%. Democrats' lead on grocery costs widened to 16 points from 7. Official data supports the mood: CPI inflation, which is the government's measure of price increases over a year, stood at 3.74% in the latest reading, well above the Fed's 2% target.

Meanwhile, the data that usually produces gloom is behaving. Initial jobless claims, meaning first-time applications for unemployment insurance, slipped to 194,000 for the week ended Oct. 17, CNBC reported, just above July's 189,000, the lowest since September 1969. Jefferies economist Thomas Simons said that despite pessimistic surveys, job-cut headlines and the "phantom job-stealing power of AI," claims suggest the labor market is stable.

Stable here has a specific meaning. Employers are not firing anyone, but they are not hiring either. Payrolls rose by just 29,000 in September and have averaged 69,000 a month this year. Unemployment is 4.2%. This is the low-fire, low-hire economy: a comfortable place to be if you have a job, and a lonely one if you are looking for one.

MarketWatch asked the obvious question in a headline: why are Americans so sour when the economy looks great by one measure? Its answer is that a once-in-a-generation investment boom has produced plenty of winners while many people feel left out. The S&P 500 fund SPY closed near 760 and the Nasdaq near 26,009, which is wonderful news for anyone who owns stocks and a nice view for anyone who does not. Brent crude at $115.22 a barrel helps explain why the gas pump is not part of the celebration.

For politicians, the poll is a problem. Democrats lead the generic congressional preference 52% to 45%, a 3-point gain since July, and 72% of Democrats are highly interested in the election versus 62% of Republicans. Both pollsters urged caution. Roberts framed it as a contest between "the map and momentum," and the Democratic pollster, Jay Campbell, said he does not see a realignment election like 1994 or 2008.

The poll also found rare national unity. Ninety-three percent said elected officials should not be allowed to own or trade individual stocks of companies with federal contracts, and 89% said firms tied to a president's immediate family should not get federal contracts. It is the one policy that Americans agree on, and the one least likely to be decided by Americans.

The Fed meets next week. Markets expect it to hold rates in October and raise by a quarter point in December, according to CNBC, with the federal funds rate currently at 3.88%. Officials see the job market as a strength and inflation as the problem. The October jobs report on Nov. 6 arrives three days after the election, which is the sort of timing that lets everyone argue about it afterward.
