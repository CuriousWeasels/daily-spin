Brazil Votes in Runoff Where Both Sides Say Democracy Is at Stake, and Polls Say Nobody Is Ahead

Lula, 80, and Flávio Bolsonaro, 45, enter Sunday's vote effectively tied, with a record number of abstainers, a pardon promise and a Trump subplot.

The wire said: Brazilians vote in presidential election runoff pitting leftist Lula against far-right Bolsonaro · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 25, 2026 15:06 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Brazil's presidential runoff pits incumbent leftist Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva against senator Flávio Bolsonaro, son of ex-president Jair Bolsonaro, who is serving a 27-year sentence for plotting a coup after losing in 2022. Bolsonaro won the first round on 4 October by 47% to 45%.

Why it matters

Latin America's largest country, with 159 million eligible voters, is choosing between two opposing models for security, the economy and its relationship with Washington. A narrow result raises the prospect of a disputed outcome and a pardon for a convicted coup plotter.

Brazilians voted on Sunday in a presidential runoff that polls describe as a tie, though the final published surveys, according to The Guardian, showed Bolsonaro slightly ahead. Lula is seeking a fourth non-consecutive term. Flávio Bolsonaro is seeking the job his father held from 2019 to 2022, before the father moved on to house arrest. Results are expected late Sunday evening.

The history is not on Lula's side. The BBC reported that no candidate who trailed after the first round has ever won the second, and The Guardian noted this has held since democracy returned in the 1980s. Lula therefore has to do something no one has done, at 80, against a rival who won the first round despite not leading most polls.

Lula's main problem is turnout. The BBC said 33 million people did not vote in the first round, and The Guardian reported abstention hit a record that disproportionately affects his voters. Translation: his supporters are plentiful but have a habit of staying home, which is a poor trait in a voting bloc. His campaign has spent the runoff trying to persuade them to leave the house.

It seems to have helped. More than a million people, by Rio's City Hall count, filled Copacabana on Saturday, many in red, and Lula then attended a larger rally in São Paulo. Supporters organised a 10 million-member WhatsApp group called Lula's Army, which is a lot of soldiers for an army whose only weapon is a group chat. Student-led protests, most of which did not involve the president himself, also fed the surge. Political scientist Helcimara de Souza Telles told The Guardian that civil society, not the campaign, has driven his growth.

Bolsonaro's campaign has leaned on crime. He promises an iron-fist approach, including five mega-prisons modelled on those of El Salvador's Nayib Bukele, plus tax and spending cuts. He has also promised to pardon his father and, with an even more conservative congress arriving next year, to change the constitution. He says this will re-democratise the country, a word that critics note sits oddly beside a pardon for a man convicted of trying to cancel an election.

His runoff has been bumpier than his first round. He is under investigation in two federal police inquiries over alleged corruption and money laundering, according to The Guardian. The magazine Piauí also claimed he attended a 2020 boat party with sex workers and vaccine lobbyists. Neither claim has been tested in court as far as the sources say, but they appear to have stalled a candidate who began the runoff with a lead and endorsements from most defeated rivals.

Washington is a character in the race. Donald Trump called Bolsonaro's first-round result a very big victory, according to the BBC, and Lula's government has accused the US of interfering, which Washington called baseless. Lula framed the vote as one over sovereignty, asking whether voters prefer the candidate who kissed the Brazilian flag or the American one. At Bolsonaro's rally, The Guardian saw supporters carrying a flag merging both countries, which presumably settles the question of which one gets kissed.

Security was not abstract either. The Guardian reported that Iara Pimentel, a councillor from Lula's party, was hospitalised with facial injuries after her motorcade was hit by bottles and bricks thrown by Bolsonaro supporters. She said it is unacceptable for people to be attacked for their political views. Analysts also fear that if Lula wins narrowly, Bolsonaro could refuse to concede, as his father did, and Trump might decline to recognise the result.

The electorate is mostly made up. Each side is, in The Guardian's word, crystallised, so the outcome rests with a small band of voters who like neither man. Minas Gerais, where Bolsonaro led by just under 5% in round one, is the key bellwether, since no one has won the presidency without it. Watch the count there tonight, and then watch whether the loser accepts it.
