Meta Pauses Muse Sharing After Private Chats Turn Out to Be Searchable

Researchers at Claroc found 6,800 Muse share links through public DNS records, exposing loan applications, revenue reviews and a staff vacation calendar.

The wire said: Meta pauses Muse sharing after researchers find AI chats on the web · The Verge AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 22, 2026 02:03 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Muse is Meta's new general-purpose AI agent, and it lets users share conversations through web links. Security firm Claroc reported this week that those links could be found through public DNS records, and Meta has now suspended the feature.

Why it matters

A share link is supposed to be private because nobody can guess it. If anyone can look the link up, whatever you pasted into the chatbot, from a loan application to a bank alert, may be readable by strangers.

Meta has paused the sharing feature in Muse, its AI assistant, after researchers showed that conversations users had shared by link could be found by anyone who knew where to look. Security firm Claroc said it enumerated about 6,800 share links, according to Ars Technica and The Verge. Meta told both outlets it is reviewing the report and considering changes to how the links can be discovered.

Share links normally work like an unlisted door: the page is public in principle, but the address is a long, random string that nobody could guess. Translation: the security rests entirely on the address staying secret. Muse reportedly undermined this by building each address from a short phrase describing the conversation, followed by a random ID. Claroc said the hostname usually revealed what a page held before anyone opened it.

The second ingredient was DNS, the internet's directory that matches names to servers. Passive DNS services record those lookups over time and sell searchable databases of them. Translation: once someone clicks a share link, the address can end up in a database that anyone can query. Because the link carries the same title and ID, Claroc said a hostname pulled from DNS rebuilds the full link. As the researchers put it, gathering the links took one query.

After opening a few hundred of the pages, Claroc reported finding a marketing company's internal revenue review marked for its CEO, a small-business owner's loan application with the amount requested, and a real-estate investor's performance report with two property addresses. There was also a to-do list containing a bank fraud alert with a partial account number, and notes for a candidate's confidential call with an AI lab. A workplace's full-year staff vacation calendar was in there too, listing dozens of employees by the week each would be away. Nobody involved appears to have intended any of this to be read by the public.

Ars noted that Mark Zuckerberg said last month that Muse was "built from the ground up for privacy and security." Meta's statement, as reported, says people can share Muse artifacts, which creates a page accessible to those with the link. This is technically accurate, in the way that a diary left on a park bench is technically accessible to those who walk past it.

Claroc co-founder and CTO Asaf Aprozper told Ars he reported the findings through Meta's bug bounty program on October 8 and also tried contacting Meta staff on LinkedIn, without a reply either way. Meta said it opened the bounty program so researchers could raise exactly this kind of scenario. Translation: a bug bounty is a standing offer to pay outsiders for finding flaws, which works best when somebody reads the submissions.

The Verge noted this is not new territory. Conversations with ChatGPT, Claude and Grok have also ended up in Google Search after sharing features made chats more public than users expected. The pattern is consistent: companies build a share button, users treat it as a private envelope, and the internet treats it as a billboard.

What to watch is how Meta redesigns the links. Fixes could include removing descriptive titles from addresses or keeping the pages out of public records. Until then, anyone who shared a Muse chat might consider what was in it.
