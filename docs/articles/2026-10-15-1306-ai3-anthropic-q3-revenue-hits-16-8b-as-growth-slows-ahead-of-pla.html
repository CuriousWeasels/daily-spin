Anthropic Posts $16.8 Billion Quarter, Disappoints Investors Who Hoped for a Bigger Number to Be Disappointed By

Revenue rose roughly 45 percent from Q2 and the company is profitable on an adjusted basis, but a slowing pace and a rough market may pull its November IPO valuation below $1.5 trillion.

The wire said: Anthropic's Q3 revenue hits $16.8B but growth slows ahead of planned November IPO (The Decoder)

By The Daily Spin Desk · AI Oct 15, 2026 13:06 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Anthropic, maker of the Claude AI models, plans to go public in November, with bankers having floated valuations as high as $2 trillion. It privately showed prospective investors its third-quarter numbers at a meeting in San Francisco, according to The Decoder, which cited multiple US media reports.

Why it matters

The IPO is the biggest test yet of whether public markets will pay trillion-dollar prices for AI companies, and the answer affects everything from retirement funds to the price of the AI tools workers increasingly use. A lower valuation would suggest investors want evidence of durable profits, not just fast growth.

Anthropic told potential investors that its third-quarter revenue reached $16.8 billion, according to The Decoder, which cited multiple US media reports. That is about 45 percent above the second quarter and more than 3.6 times the company's total revenue for all of 2025. By most standards this is a spectacular result, which is a useful reminder that in the IPO business the standards are not most standards.

The trouble is the comparison. In the second quarter, demand for the coding tool Claude Code pushed revenue up by more than 140 percent over the first quarter. Growing 45 percent after growing 140 percent is like a car decelerating from a very fast speed to a merely fast one, and passengers who bought tickets for the first speed have noticed. The Decoder offers a few explanations: summer is when people work less and use fewer tokens (Translation: the chunks of text an AI model processes, which is how usage gets billed), and OpenAI launched a strong rival called Astra in September, which Anthropic only countered in late September with Opus 5.5.

There was good news in the details. The Information reported an adjusted operating margin of 11 percent for the year through Q3, and the Financial Times said it was the second straight quarter of adjusted profitability. Translation: adjusted means leaving out big costs such as stock-based compensation, so the profit is real in the way a diet is real when you do not count weekends. Anthropic is also cash-flow positive, and it earned $1.70 in revenue for every dollar spent on compute (Translation: the computing power that runs the models), which suggests that serving AI to customers can pay for itself.

Then there is distillation, in which other companies use a model's outputs to train their own. Anthropic estimates 5 to 7 percent of its revenue comes from it. The company has repeatedly accused Chinese AI labs of copying its models this way, and it has now rolled out effective countermeasures. The Decoder notes this raises the question of why Anthropic lobbied so long against the practice if it could have stopped it itself. Anthropic told investors that blocking it will drag on current-quarter revenue, though it should pay off over time by making Claude harder to match. In effect, the company is giving up a slice of income to protect its moat, a trade investors tend to admire in theory and examine closely in a prospectus.

The IPO, which The Information says is set for November before Thanksgiving, is where the arithmetic gets uncomfortable. Anthropic's private valuation reached $965 billion in May, up from under $20 billion two years earlier, and some bankers floated a $2 trillion listing. But the numbers reportedly disappointed some investors, and several expect Anthropic's bankers to lower expectations to possibly under $1.5 trillion. Part of the problem is what the company left out: it did not discuss its September annualized revenue rate or a full-year outlook. In July, that rate was reportedly $65 billion.

The market backdrop is no help. The Decoder lists high inflation, rising borrowing costs, public sentiment toward AI that is more negative than positive, unresolved cybersecurity risks, a months-long price war among OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and others, and open models closing the performance gap. Against that, The Decoder argues people will almost certainly use more AI rather than less. A company valued at under $1.5 trillion would still be worth roughly 75 times what it was two years ago, which is the sort of disappointment most founders would accept in cash.
