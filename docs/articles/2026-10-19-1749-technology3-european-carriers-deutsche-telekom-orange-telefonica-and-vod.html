Four European Carriers Sign Letter Promising to Someday Compete With Starlink, Pending Satellites

Deutsche Telekom, Orange, Telefónica and Vodafone will build the ground side of a direct-to-phone satellite service, with the part in orbit left to a partner to be named later.

The wire said: Major European mobile carriers are teaming up for a ‘direct’ satellite program without Starlink · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 19, 2026 17:49 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

SpaceX's Starlink has been expanding into direct-to-device service, in which satellites connect to ordinary phones without a dish. Europe's big carriers now say they want a homegrown alternative.

Why it matters

Whoever controls satellite spectrum will decide who provides coverage in rural areas and during outages, and whether Europe depends on an American company for it. For ordinary customers, the practical result is still some years of dead zones.

Deutsche Telekom, Orange, Telefónica and Vodafone have signed a letter of intent saying they are considering a joint bid for spectrum that would support a European rival to SpaceX's Starlink in direct-to-device satellite service, The Verge reported. A letter of intent is a document in which companies state that they intend to discuss doing something. Translation: nobody has built anything, but everyone has agreed on the font.

The plan is a division of labor. The four carriers would set up the ground network infrastructure, along with coordination and monitoring functions in Europe. They say they would not invest directly in a satellite constellation. That part would come from a satellite partner who has not been selected, or at least not announced.

This leaves the project with the ground crew, the paperwork and no aircraft. Building a rival to the dominant satellite constellation is usually considered to require a constellation, which is the expensive, rocket-dependent part. The carriers appear to have looked at that line item and decided to specialize in everything else.

The stated goals are universal coverage in underserved areas, more resilient public networks, protection of critical communications, and support for a European constellation. The last item is a tidy admission that the constellation does not yet exist and that the carriers are in favor of it. The Verge noted that the initiative would pair European governance and market access with proven satellite technology, which is how one describes a partnership in which the proven technology is the missing piece.

The move has an American precedent. AT&T, T-Mobile and Verizon announced their own collaboration earlier this year to combine spectrum in rural areas and dead zones. Spectrum, for the uninitiated, is the limited range of radio frequencies that governments license, and the thing carriers would most like to hold while their competitors do not.

Politics sits underneath all of this. In May, the EU said it was considering reserving part of the 2 GHz band for local companies to offer satellite services, according to the Financial Times as cited by The Verge. SpaceX has criticized the idea, claiming it would worsen service in Ukraine. It is a position that happens to align a security argument with a commercial interest, which is a common and efficient arrangement.

The incentive for the carriers is straightforward. If satellites can connect directly to phones, the companies that sell mobile plans risk becoming the middlemen to a service someone else provides from orbit. Banding together to hold the spectrum keeps them in the transaction. Customers in remote areas, meanwhile, will continue to enjoy the traditional European coverage map until a satellite partner is chosen.

What to watch is the spectrum decision in Brussels and the name of the satellite partner. Until then, the continent's answer to Starlink remains a very well-organized ground floor.
