Kyiv Sleeps in Subway as Ukraine Tries to Ground Moscow's Flights

Russia's strikes have made Kyiv a front-line city, while Ukraine's drones have shut Moscow's airports almost daily in October, DW reported.

The wire said: Russia's attacks on Ukraine's capital, Kyiv, are intensifying. DW asked people on the ground what life is like · DW

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 24, 2026 02:07 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Russia has expanded its attacks on Kyiv in 2026, killing more civilians than in the same period of previous years. Ukraine has responded with drone strikes that repeatedly halt flights at Moscow's major airports.

Why it matters

Both campaigns target the machinery of ordinary life, from heating and school attendance in Kyiv to airline schedules and ticket prices in Russia. Winter supply problems in Ukraine and insurance costs for airlines are the practical pressure points to watch.

Russia's attacks on Kyiv have intensified to the point that DW now describes the capital as a front-line city. Until a few months ago it was comparatively peaceful next to areas near the fighting. Translation: the city that used to be the safe option has been reclassified, without anyone consulting its residents.

The details are domestic. With too few shelters for 4 million people, subway stations serve as dormitories, and from about 10 each night people lay out mattresses and small tents. Halyna, who lives in the Lukyanivka district, told DW she takes her 5-year-old son Roman to the station most evenings, even when no siren has sounded. In September, a Russian drone struck an apartment building in Pechersk and killed 14-year-old Daniel Umanskyj as he did homework. In early October, a drone hit a school in southwestern Kyiv, though pupils and teachers were already in shelters.

The numbers are grim. The Kyiv military administration says 140 people were killed in the capital in the first eight months of 2026, against 172 in all of 2025. DW notes that if the pace holds, this could be the deadliest year for Kyiv since the 2022 invasion. Warehouse strikes have caused supermarket shortages not seen since the early months of the war, and emergency power outages resumed at the end of September.

School attendance now depends on shelter capacity and the length of the siren. One school principal told DW her shelter holds more than 500 students, so first through ninth grades can attend in person. A parent at DW's Kyiv bureau described the problem plainly: you have to work, and the child stays home alone. Authorities have shortened the curfew to 1 a.m. to 5 a.m. and let some facilities keep operating during yellow alerts, while red alerts, which can mean ballistic missiles, require immediate shelter. The Kyiv International Institute of Sociology reported that 72% of residents are prepared to endure the war as long as necessary.

Meanwhile Ukraine is pursuing a different kind of urban inconvenience. DW reports, citing the outlet Agentstvo, that drone strikes forced Moscow's Domodedovo, Sheremetyevo, Zhukovsky and Vnukovo airports to halt operations almost daily in October, sometimes for up to nine hours. President Zelenskyy announced in early September that Russian airspace would become unsafe and warned airlines and insurers. Ukraine also sent a warning to the International Civil Aviation Organization.

The mechanism is bureaucratic. Russia's aviation authority has a so-called carpet protocol that closes airspace whenever an aircraft violates it, weapons are used or unidentified objects appear. Translation: any drone, armed or not, can ground a runway. Analyst Jan Matvejev told DW that the drones need neither warheads nor long range for this, and that Ukraine can produce hundreds of combat-ready long-range drones a day. He added that if Russia could shoot them all down, it would have done so by now.

The risk is not confined to timetables. Consultant Oleksandr Laneckij warned that Russia might lower the threshold for declaring the protocol, raising the chance of incidents like the 2024 Azerbaijan Airlines crash, which investigators say was caused by a Russian air-defense missile and killed 38 people. Matvejev said machine-gun fire at low drones poses little danger to aircraft, but missiles raise the risk considerably.

Foreign airlines will decide on three factors, Laneckij said: military threat, demand and insurance cost. Fewer Russian carriers mean more demand for the foreign ones that remain, which gives them a reason to stay. As he put it, staying depends on the price they must pay for the danger. Watch the insurers, who tend to settle geopolitical arguments faster than diplomats.
