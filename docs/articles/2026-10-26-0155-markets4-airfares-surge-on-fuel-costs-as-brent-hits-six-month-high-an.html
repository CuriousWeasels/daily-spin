Airlines Report Fewer Passengers, Higher Fares, and Total Calm

U.S. carriers say flying planes less full at much higher prices is the plan, even as jet fuel at more than double last year's level cuts into profits.

The wire said: Fewer people are flying as airfare soars. Here's why airlines aren't worried · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 26, 2026 01:55 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Jet fuel prices have spiked this year, and airlines have passed much of the cost to travelers through higher fares. After a round of third-quarter earnings, executives say they prefer fewer, higher-paying passengers to fuller planes.

Why it matters

Expensive tickets look set to persist through the holidays and likely beyond, since airlines are trimming capacity and betting on premium demand. If you were hoping for a bargain, the industry has noted your hope and declined to price it in.

Fewer Americans are flying this year, and airline executives would like everyone to know this is fine. CNBC reported that the number of people screened at U.S. airport checkpoints is on track for its first annual decline since 2020, while fares keep climbing. At the Wings Club gala in Manhattan on Friday, executives told CNBC that demand does not worry them. Fuel does.

The strategy has a name. American Airlines Chief Commercial Officer Nat Pieper said the carrier is "actively prioritizing yield versus load factor." Translation: yield is how much revenue each seat brings in, and load factor is how full the plane is. The airline has decided it would rather have a half-empty plane of expensive tickets than a full one of cheaper ones. American's load factor fell to 83.4% in the third quarter from 86% a year earlier.

The prices explain the confidence. Fares in September were up more than 22% from a year earlier, according to the latest inflation data cited by CNBC, and Hopper expects the most expensive holiday ticket prices in years. Southwest's average third-quarter fare was $231.69, up close to $50, and that excludes its new fees for checked bags and seats nearer the front. Its passenger count fell 3.2% to about 33.5 million, yet CEO Bob Jordan said both fares and volumes beat the company's forecast. Translation: fewer customers paying much more counts as a good surprise.

The reason is fuel, which is the biggest expense after labor. U.S. Gulf Coast jet fuel was $4.63 a gallon, more than double a year ago, according to the Energy Information Administration. United expects to pay an average of $4.80 a gallon in the fourth quarter, up from $3.90 in the third. All four of American, United, Delta and Southwest cut their 2026 profit forecasts this month, and American is the only one expecting a loss for the year.

Not everyone is shrinking. United flew 2.4% more passengers and said unit revenue, the money earned per seat flown a mile, rose 16%, its biggest jump since the pandemic. CEO Scott Kirby said that continued focus on yield over load factor "alone could get us to double-digit margins next year." Since April, about 9.3 million fewer people were screened than in the same period of 2025, a drop of less than 2%, according to the TSA.

Premium cabins are doing much of the work, with seats that cost hundreds or thousands of dollars more than coach. Airlines are also cutting growth plans, which makes seats scarcer and can push prices higher if demand holds. Spirit Airlines went out of business in early May, and Bank of America said some of its customers have likely stopped flying. Ed Bastian of Delta, the evening's honoree, cited geopolitics and record fuel costs but said he was not worried about the industry's resilience.

Supply problems are easing, with Boeing and Airbus ramping up production and the FAA approving the Max 7 and Max 10. The next test is the smaller carriers, starting with JetBlue next week, followed by Frontier and Allegiant, whose customers are more price sensitive. If they show passengers pulling back, we will learn how much pricing power is really power and how much is just fewer people willing to pay.
