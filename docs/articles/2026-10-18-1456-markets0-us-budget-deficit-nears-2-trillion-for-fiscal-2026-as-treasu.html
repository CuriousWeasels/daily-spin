U.S. Posts $1.992 Trillion Deficit, Its 25th Straight, Citing Growth Plan Not Yet Observed

The fiscal 2026 gap grew 12% as interest costs passed $1 trillion, while 10-year yields sit at 5.1% and the political fix remains scheduled for later.

The wire said: U.S. budget deficit swells to almost $2 trillion for fiscal 2026, up 12% in a single year · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 18, 2026 14:56 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The Treasury reported Friday that the government ran a $1.992 trillion deficit in fiscal 2026, up from $1.775 trillion a year earlier. Long-term yields have climbed to levels not seen since 2002, driven partly by the war with Iran, heavy bond issuance and rising oil prices.

Why it matters

Higher Treasury yields feed directly into mortgage rates, which CNBC says hover around 7.5%, and into the cost of every other loan priced off them. The more the government pays in interest, the less room it has for anything else, including cutting the deficit.

The United States ended fiscal 2026 with a budget deficit of $1.992 trillion, the Treasury Department reported Friday. That is about 12% above last year's $1.775 trillion and the third-largest on record, behind only 2020 and 2021, when the government was fighting a pandemic. This time the cause is less dramatic: it is simply what the government costs, minus what it collects. It was also the 25th consecutive year of deficits, the last surplus having arrived in 2001.

CNBC reported that both receipts and outlays hit records, which is the fiscal equivalent of a household announcing its best-ever income and its worst-ever month at the same time. Social Security cost nearly $1.7 trillion and Medicare almost $1.1 trillion. Net interest, meaning what Treasury pays on its debt minus what it receives, topped $1 trillion, up from $970 billion. Translation: the country now spends more than a trillion dollars a year for the privilege of having already spent money.

Revenue did its part, within limits. Individual income taxes brought in more than $2.8 trillion, up about 7%. Corporate income tax fell roughly $70 billion to $381 billion, which CNBC attributed to the tax cuts in last year's One Big Beautiful Bill. Tariffs set a record for gross customs duties, but after the Supreme Court ruled many of them unconstitutional, refunds sent billions back to retailers. A tax that is collected and then returned is, strictly speaking, a loan from importers.

The deficit is about 6.2% of GDP, according to CNBC's analysis. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has argued the target should be below 4% by the end of Trump's term, and CNBC concluded there is almost no way to reach it. Bessent's preferred route is growth: 'we have to grow our way out of it,' he said in Dallas last month. The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget calculates that this would require a decade of 7.2% growth. The second quarter was recently revised up to 2.2%, which is a start in the sense that a journey of a thousand miles begins with a step.

Bond traders are less patient. The 10-year yield stands at 5.1%, up 114 basis points since the end of February. Translation: a basis point is a hundredth of a percentage point, so lenders now demand more than a full point of extra annual payment than they did before the Iran war began on Feb. 28. Oil and diesel prices are up sharply, and traders worry that central banks may have to raise rates. Brent crude was most recently at $126.55 a barrel, which suggests they are not being paranoid.

Supply is part of the problem. Investors want extra compensation for holding long bonds because the government keeps issuing them, while companies are issuing hundreds of billions in debt to build artificial intelligence infrastructure. The Treasury is competing for savers' money with data centers. The CBO estimates that if rates run a half-point above its forecast, the 10-year deficit total rises by $1.9 trillion; a 1.5-point overshoot adds $6 trillion. The CBO expects the 10-year yield to average 4.3%, against 5.1% today.

The mortgage market is the visible casualty. Redfin said pending home sales fell 4% in September, the biggest monthly drop in three years, with the average 30-year fixed rate near 7.5%. Mortgage rates track the 10-year yield, so Washington's borrowing habits are now a line item in the cost of buying a house in any state.

Meanwhile, Bank of England deputy governor Sarah Breeden told CNBC that hedge funds have become a bigger part of government bond markets, making a broad shock more likely than before. The IMF puts hedge fund gross assets at about $13 trillion, and the Office of Financial Research says they held a record 7% of the $30 trillion Treasury market at the end of 2025. Many use basis trades, which means buying Treasurys, shorting the matching futures and borrowing heavily to magnify a tiny price gap. It works until it doesn't, which is the standard disclosure for leverage.

The politics offer little relief. Republicans are widely forecast to lose at least the House in the Nov. 3 midterms, and CNBC noted that Rep. Hakeem Jeffries, the likely next speaker, wants to reverse $1 trillion in healthcare cuts. Trump, for his part, pledged $5,000 checks to every adult citizen if Republicans swept the election. CNBC notes that higher yields are also drawing buyers to Treasurys, so expensive borrowing is not yet a crisis. For now it is merely a very large invoice, with the deadline unspecified.
