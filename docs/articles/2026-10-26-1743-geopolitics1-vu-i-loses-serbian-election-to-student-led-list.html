Serbian Voters Retire Vučić, Who Had Not Planned to Be Retired

A student-led list took 52.7% of the vote to the ruling party's 39.2% in snap elections, and Aleksandar Vučić conceded after 14 years in power.

The wire said: Celebrations in Serbia after veteran ruler Vučić loses election to student-led protest movement · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 26, 2026 17:43 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Protests began after a canopy collapsed at Novi Sad railway station and killed 16 people, an accident blamed on corruption and negligence. Aleksandar Vučić called snap parliamentary elections after roughly two years of demonstrations.

Why it matters

A government that has balanced EU candidacy against warm ties with Moscow is being replaced by one with no track record, so Serbia's course on Europe and Russia is open to change. The result is also a rare case of an entrenched leader losing a vote to a movement with no obvious leader.

Serbia's long-standing leader Aleksandar Vučić admitted defeat on Monday in snap parliamentary elections, losing to a student-led protest movement. "It is clear we have lost these elections," he said, according to the BBC. It is an unusual sentence for a man who has been in power since 2012, and he appears to have said it anyway.

The numbers were not close. With 97% of polling stations counted, the election commission said the Student List had 2.25 million votes and Vučić's Serbian Progressive Party 1.67 million. The BBC reported preliminary shares of 52.7% against 39.2%. Translation: this was not a recount situation, and there is no obvious margin to argue over.

The movement began less than two years ago after a canopy collapsed in front of the Novi Sad station and killed 16 people. Protesters blamed corruption and negligence. Vučić and his party have repeatedly denied corruption, but voters appear to have weighed the denials against the canopy.

Vučić had served two terms as president and could not seek a third, so he led his party's list with the aim of returning as prime minister, a post he left in 2017. Translation: when the constitution limits the number of terms, the usual workaround is to move to another job in the same building. This time the voters declined to hold the door.

The Student List has no obvious leaders, and its members are not used to politics. It fielded candidates for the 250-seat parliament from across society, including doctors, actors, academics and sports personalities. This is either a broad coalition or the cast of a very ambitious charity gala, and for now it is also the majority.

Its manifesto promises to purge officials who broke rules in cracking down on protesters, to stop party loyalists from leading public enterprises, and to free the public broadcaster from political control. Each pledge is a plan to remove something the previous government built. Nebojša Vladisavljević, a political scientist at the University of Belgrade, told BBC Serbian the result gave the list "absolute legitimacy" to govern for the coming years.

The foreign reactions were revealing. Vučić backed Serbia's EU candidacy while refusing to impose sanctions on Russia over the invasion of Ukraine, and both audiences are now reacting. European Commissioner Marta Kos said the door to the EU is open. Kremlin spokesperson Dmitry Peskov praised Vučić and noted that "it takes two to tango", which is the diplomatic way of saying the dance card has changed.

Hungary's prime minister, Péter Magyar, whose party ended Viktor Orbán's 16 years in power in April, said he was delighted to hear that his victory had encouraged the students. Regional strongmen are now being retired by ballot box often enough that someone should start a support group.

Practical questions remain. The Student List must choose a prime minister, and presidential elections are due in December, with parliament speaker Ana Brnabić filling the role in a caretaker capacity. The movement spent two years proving it could win an election. Next it has to prove it can run a ministry, which is where most protest movements find out how long the paperwork is.
