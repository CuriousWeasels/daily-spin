Cities Discover Robotaxis Are Subject to Zoning, Noise Rules, and Local Opinion

With Congress stalled for years, towns and states across party lines are writing their own robotaxi rules, and the industry is learning that the patchwork is permanent.

The wire said: Cities want to put the boot on robotaxis · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 19, 2026 01:58 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Federal legislation on autonomous vehicles has been stuck in Congress for years, leaving cities and states to set their own rules. The Verge reports that local officials from Minneapolis to San Diego to Austin are now doing exactly that.

Why it matters

Whether a driverless car can pick you up, drop you at the airport, or exist in your neighborhood will depend on local ordinances, not one national standard. Companies face rising compliance costs, and residents gain more say over what operates on their streets.

Cities and states across the political spectrum are drafting their own rules to rein in robotaxis, The Verge reported, because Congress has not passed a federal framework for autonomous vehicles in years. The industry had hoped to avoid a patchwork of local rules. It has received a quilt.

The history is instructive. According to The Verge, Nevada legalized self-driving cars on public roads in June 2011, years before commercial robotaxis existed, largely because Google lobbied for it. The law even exempted drivers from text-messaging bans while the automated system was engaged. Translation: the first rule written for robot cars was a loophole for the humans in them.

Municipalities generally cannot set vehicle manufacturing standards, but they hold broad authority over street operations, and they are using it. Minneapolis is weighing an ordinance requiring human monitors inside autonomous vehicles, which would effectively ban driverless operation. San Diego's City Council passed a resolution seeking to block Waymo from the airport and give local agencies direct control of autonomous vehicles in emergencies.

Austin firefighters, meanwhile, have raised concerns that the Tesla Cybercab has no steering wheel or pedals, which complicates moving a stalled vehicle. They want rapid-response towing protocols and direct lines to human operators. The Daily Texan reported that Tesla gave Austin first responders three days of training and emergency guidebooks. Translation: the manual is the steering wheel now.

The pushback is not neatly partisan. The Verge notes that Republicans were once seen as friendlier to the technology, and that New York City, Chicago, and Washington, DC, all Democrat-led, still have not legalized driverless cars. But Kentucky, Oklahoma, South Dakota, and Iowa are now considering tighter rules as well. In California, bills would let cities cap fleet sizes, limit operating hours, or ban robotaxis locally, and require physical override access or remote shut-off for police and fire departments.

Zoning is the quieter weapon. Culver City explored emergency moratoriums on building permits for AV fleet facilities, and Santa Monica used noise and traffic ordinances to restrict 24-hour charging hubs. The Verge observes that even robotaxis are not immune to NIMBY complaints. NIMBY, for the uninitiated, means Not In My Back Yard, and it has now been extended to vehicles that do not have a driver to complain about it.

The companies have options, none of them simple. Waymo is planning expansion to London, Tokyo, and Munich, and Uber is lining up Manchester, Madrid, and Dubai. But the US remains the industry's best chance at profit, so Waymo, Zoox, and Aurora are favoring states with explicit preemption laws, which stop cities from banning the technology. Industry groups are lobbying for more of that power nationally. Streetsblog reports Uber and Waymo have spent more than $15 million lobbying state officials in New York alone.

Even a federal law would not fix this. The Verge notes that proposals like the SELF DRIVE Act would leave cities and states in charge of local road use, so cities will remain the main arbiters of robotaxi permission for the foreseeable future.

The mood on the sidewalk is similar. The Verge also reported that Carolyn Marvin was arrested and charged with disorderly conduct in Philadelphia after refusing to move out of an Uber delivery robot's path. She told ABC13 she has been protesting the robots for taking human delivery jobs by standing in front of them. Police asked her to move, she declined, and they cuffed her. Earlier this year, another robot was kicked over in Center City.

The industry's advice, per The Verge, is to get used to the patchwork. The patchwork, for its part, has no plans to get used to the industry.
