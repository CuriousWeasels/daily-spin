France Holds Nationwide Protest Over Prices, Schools, and Everything Else Currently Going Wrong

Tens of thousands marched on Saturday as students, unions and Yellow Vest remnants merged grievances; 166 were arrested by midday, according to the Interior Ministry.

The wire said: Protesters clash with police during nationwide demonstrations in France · Al Jazeera

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 17, 2026 14:30 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Weeks of student protests over run-down schools and teacher absences have turned violent, with police and protesters injured. On Saturday, unions and Yellow Vest activists joined to protest rising prices and inequality.

Why it matters

A government facing a presidential election next year now has a single street movement covering prices, schools and police conduct, which is harder to settle than any one of them alone.

Protesters and police clashed in Paris, Marseille and other French cities on Saturday during a nationwide day of demonstrations against the rising cost of living, Al Jazeera and DW reported. Police used tear gas in Paris, and the Interior Ministry said 166 people had been arrested nationwide by midday. The country, in short, was having a disagreement about the price of groceries, and the grocery prices were not present to respond.

The ministry counted 140 protest actions and 32,800 participants at midday. Organizers, according to DW, had planned more than 600 demonstrations, and a police source told AFP that authorities expected nearly 200,000 people. Translation: the official crowd estimate is the number the government expects before it sees the crowd, and the organizers' number is the one they give before they have to count it.

The protests widen a movement that began last month with high school students complaining about teacher absences and dilapidated buildings. DW reported that more than 900 police and several protesters have been injured in those earlier protests, and that the internal police watchdog is investigating at least two dozen cases of alleged police violence. Both outlets reported that a teenager lost a hand, with DW saying it was caused by a stun grenade. A demand for functioning classrooms has therefore acquired a second demand, which is that the response to it stop maiming children.

Saturday's lineup added trade unionists, teachers and remnants of the Yellow Vest movement, which DW noted held weekly protests from November 2018 to June 2020, starting over fuel prices. The result is a coalition with a shared mood and no single request. Prime Minister Sebastien Lecornu described it as a moment of fatigue and an atmosphere of 'perma-crisis: wars, inflation, and issues surrounding purchasing power.' That is an unusually frank diagnosis from a government, and it comes without a prescription.

Local media cited by Al Jazeera reported that protesters breached the town hall in Marseille and looted shops, and that a tourism office was set ablaze in Lyon. Al Jazeera also stressed that much of the day was peaceful, with unions, teachers and ordinary citizens marching without incident. Authorities in Paris had banned rallies near the Elysee, the prime minister's office and the Champs-Elysees, as well as a march through the city center, which is a way of saying the demonstration was welcome as long as it took place somewhere no one important would hear it.

President Emmanuel Macron, speaking in Brussels, said the right to protest comes with respect for people and property. The government has also blamed the hard-left LFI for pouring fuel on the fire, an accusation the party denies, DW reported. Meanwhile Jordan Bardella of the far-right National Rally, which DW says leads the polls ahead of next year's presidential election, predicted 'a day of chaos for France' while visiting a car show. Predicting chaos is a low-risk position for an opposition leader, since it is rewarded if it happens and also if it is merely discussed.

What to watch next is whether the government answers the schools, the prices or the policing, since the crowd has now asked about all three. It has so far addressed the right to protest, which no one had asked about.
