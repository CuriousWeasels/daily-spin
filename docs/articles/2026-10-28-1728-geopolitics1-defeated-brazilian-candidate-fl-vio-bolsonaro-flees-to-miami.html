Defeated Brazilian Candidate Concedes Nothing, Except Three Countries and a Gulfstream

Flávio Bolsonaro, who lost Sunday's election and refused to concede, landed in Miami after a passport order, a border crossing and two private flights.

The wire said: Flávio Bolsonaro arrives in Florida after fleeing Brazil · NPR World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 28, 2026 17:28 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Flávio Bolsonaro, a senator, lost Brazil's presidential election to incumbent Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva on Sunday and has not conceded. A Supreme Court justice cancelled his passport in a money-laundering investigation, which he denies wrongdoing in.

Why it matters

The opposition's leader now sits abroad while Brazil's right wing faces a leadership vacuum, and a new dispute opens between the hemisphere's two most populous countries. His immunity from prosecution as a senator ends in February, so the question of where he lives is also a question of whether he can be tried.

Flávio Bolsonaro, the defeated far-right candidate in Brazil's presidential election, landed in Miami on Wednesday morning with his wife and two daughters, according to DW. The family stepped off a Gulfstream G400 at Miami-Opa Locka airport. The trip had begun the previous night in Argentina, which is a notable route for a man who had said two days earlier that he had no intention of leaving the country.

On Monday, Bolsonaro posted on Instagram that he would hand over his passports and defend himself against what he called a travesty. Translation: he would comply with a court order, right up until the deadline. The Guardian reported that a Supreme Court justice cancelled his passport minutes after the result and gave him until 11am Tuesday to surrender it to the federal police. He did not appear.

The court order relates to a federal police investigation into a suspected money-laundering scheme involving betting companies, worth about £680m ($905m), according to the Guardian. Bolsonaro denies wrongdoing. His immunity from prosecution as a senator ends in February when his term does, which, as incentives go, is the sort a lawyer would describe as clarifying.

The route was assembled with some creativity. Argentina's migration agency said the family crossed at the Tancredo Neves International Bridge in Misiones province and boarded a private flight to Guayaquil, Ecuador, at 11.30pm Tuesday, adding that they had valid documentation and faced no legal impediment. How, when his Brazilian and diplomatic passports were cancelled? AFP reported that he also holds Italian citizenship, which turns out to be a very good thing to have in a drawer. Brazilian police, the Guardian said, found no record of him passing a regular Brazilian immigration checkpoint, and were asking Argentina for details.

Accounts of the earlier steps differ. Argentine outlet Infobae reported he had fled to Argentina on Monday night and a plane in Puerto Iguazú failed to take off, while Todo Noticias reported he crossed on foot and dropped the flight plan when news leaked. The Guardian noted it is unclear whether the planes were the same. Folha de S Paulo reported that the escape was organized by a lawyer close to the family, which is one way to describe legal services.

Washington helped smooth the landing. A State Department spokesperson said Bolsonaro and his family would be granted visas to allow entry, according to the Guardian. Metrópoles had reported that allies said he planned to request political asylum and that the Trump administration had been informed. Donald Trump, DW noted, has called the extended voting times a very bad look and has previously claimed Jair Bolsonaro, Flávio's father, is a victim of judicial persecution.

Lula was not generous. At an event for his 81st birthday, he said the Bolsonaros' first homeland is the United States, and that a citizen who sneaks out of his country owes something. Lawmakers from his Workers' party asked the Supreme Court to order Bolsonaro's arrest and to place him on Interpol's Red Notice list. Translation: a request that other countries' police treat him as wanted.

The departure leaves a gap on the right. Flávio won 48.21% of the vote, which analysts quoted by the Guardian read largely as an anti-Lula vote. His campaign chief, Rogério Marinho, said he had been surprised, having talked the day before about Flávio leading the opposition with his 60m votes. Asked whether that is possible from abroad, Marinho pointed to Venezuela's María Corina Machado. His father is in jail, according to DW, and his brother Eduardo lives in the US with an arrest warrant waiting at home.

Political scientist Camila Rocha told the Guardian power may drift away from the sons. Candidates more comfortable in Brazil, such as Tarcísio de Freitas and Michelle Bolsonaro, may be paying attention. The family business of Brazilian politics, it seems, is now being run largely from Florida.
