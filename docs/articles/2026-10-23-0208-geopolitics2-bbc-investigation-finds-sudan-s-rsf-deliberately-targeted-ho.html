BBC Finds Sudan's Hospitals Were Hit 43 Times, Which Is Not a Rounding Error

A BBC investigation documents at least 43 attacks on health sites in el-Fasher; the RSF calls it propaganda, and international law calls the act illegal.

The wire said: Hospitals deliberately targeted by Sudan's RSF fighters, BBC investigation finds · BBC World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 23, 2026 02:08 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Sudan's army and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces (RSF) have been at war since April 2023. The RSF besieged the Darfur city of el-Fasher and took it in October 2025.

Why it matters

If health facilities can be struck repeatedly with little consequence, the rule protecting them exists mostly on paper, and civilians in Sudan and in other wars pay for that. The findings may also feed UN and legal scrutiny of possible war crimes.

A BBC investigation has concluded that RSF fighters systematically destroyed the healthcare system of el-Fasher during the siege and capture of the Darfur city. Using sources on the ground, the BBC documented at least 43 separate attacks on hospitals and health sites between May 2024 and October 2025, some of them hit more than once. The RSF rejects the findings. Its health minister called them propaganda, which is one way to describe a list of 43 attacks.

Under international law it is illegal to attack health facilities or their staff during conflict. Translation: hospitals are supposed to be the one place in a war where everyone agrees to stop aiming. UN human rights chief Volker Türk called the findings "deeply disturbing" and said the attacks were serious violations of international humanitarian law that may amount to war crimes.

The human toll among medical workers, according to the BBC's data, runs from April 2023 to October 2025: 120 injured, 55 killed and 10 forcibly disappeared or abducted. The BBC's timeline traces how the system was dismantled step by step. The main Teaching Hospital closed within weeks of the war starting, and services moved to South Hospital. South Hospital was shelled in late May and early June 2024 and was raided on 8 June. Its director, Dr Izzeldin Ahmed Dawaud Asow, told the BBC he survived a beating by pretending to be dead.

Then the pattern repeated. Staff moved to the Saudi Maternity Hospital, which the BBC says was struck on some days up to 25 times with shelling and drones. The Specialised Children's Hospital relocated twice and was shelled and closed both times. The main dialysis centre was hit, relocated, and hit again. Six doctors described this to the BBC as systematic targeting. A surgeon, Dr Muhamed Suliman, said the aim was to push civilians out: "It was deliberate. It was planned."

The RSF's defence is that South Hospital was a military base. Its health minister, Dr Alaa Eldin Nugud, told the BBC missile launchers were on the grounds and showed a video of weapons remains filmed more than a year after the hospital closed. Translation: hospitals lose protection if used to commit an "act harmful to the enemy," so the argument is that the building stopped being a hospital. The BBC says it found no independent evidence for this, and doctors told it the facility was not part of the base.

The picture is not one-sided on the broader question. Both sides have been accused of attacking health facilities. In March 2026 the WHO condemned the bombing of al-Daein hospital in RSF-held eastern Darfur, which killed dozens; the army is believed to have carried out the drone strikes but denies it. Still, the WHO told the BBC that el-Fasher is among the clearest and most severe examples of the conflict's effect on health care in Sudan. UN investigators have also reported that fighting there bore the "hallmarks of genocide."

The rule that hospitals are off limits is among the most widely accepted in the laws of war. The BBC's chronology suggests it has been tested to destruction. What to watch is whether a detailed timeline from a news organisation produces anything a courtroom would recognise.
