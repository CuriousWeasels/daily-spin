Economy Grows 3.4% While Inflation Declines to Leave, Fed Says It Needs More Data

Third-quarter GDP beat forecasts and jobless claims hit near-1960s lows, but September prices rose 0.5% and the Fed is still in no hurry.

The wire said: Third-quarter U.S. GDP increases a better-than-expected 3.4% as September inflation accelerates · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 29, 2026 17:39 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

The Federal Reserve voted Wednesday to hold its benchmark rate at 3.75%-4%, saying it wanted to evaluate incoming data. Thursday's Commerce Department reports supplied a large amount of it.

Why it matters

Strong growth alongside 3.6% inflation makes rate cuts harder to justify, which keeps borrowing costs high for mortgages, car loans and businesses. Energy-driven price increases hit household budgets directly.

The U.S. economy grew at a seasonally adjusted annualized rate of 3.4% in the third quarter, beating the 3.1% economists expected and the 2.2% pace of the second quarter, the Commerce Department reported Thursday, according to CNBC. The same morning, the department said inflation accelerated in September. The Federal Reserve, which had just finished saying it would wait for more information, received it promptly.

Start with the prices. The personal consumption expenditures price index, the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, rose 0.5% in September, its largest monthly gain since March, putting the annual rate at 3.6%. Translation: if you bought the same basket of goods a year ago and again today, it costs 3.6% more, against a Fed target of 2%. Core PCE, which strips out food and energy because they bounce around, rose 0.3% for the month and 3.1% for the year. Officials generally treat core as the better guide to where inflation is heading, which is convenient, since it is the less alarming number.

The culprit was energy. Gasoline and other energy goods rose 9.2% in the month, and energy goods and services broadly rose 5.4%, CNBC reported. Economists fear this could spread elsewhere, and there are early hints: services prices excluding energy and housing rose 0.4%, and housing costs rose 0.3%. Brent crude, the global oil benchmark, stood at $123.10 as of Oct. 27, according to the latest official data, which suggests the gasoline problem is not shy.

The growth side was just as notable. Consumer spending rose 3.8% in the quarter, matching the prior period. Gross private domestic investment jumped 19.4%, driven by a 20.9% surge in equipment spending and an 8.4% rise in intellectual property products. CNBC attributed the strength to the artificial intelligence build-out, plus healthy consumer and government spending. Translation: companies are buying a great deal of computer equipment, and the economy is counting it as growth.

Real sales to private domestic purchasers, which Fed officials use as a gauge of underlying demand, rose 4.9%, up from 4.6%. The labor market offered no counterargument. Initial jobless claims fell to 194,000 for the week ended Oct. 24, below the 200,000 forecast and near levels last seen in the late 1960s. Few people are being laid off, which is pleasant for them and less so for anyone hoping demand would cool by itself.

There was one piece of better inflation news. On a quarterly annualized basis, PCE prices rose 2.1% on the headline measure and 2.5% for core, down from 5% and 3.3% in the second quarter. That is the sort of statistic that looks excellent until one remembers the month of September happened.

Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh said Wednesday that the period ahead is "information rich" and that he and colleagues are keen to take notice of it. Chris Rupkey of Fwdbonds said growth is "powering ahead" despite geopolitical risks and higher energy costs. Stock futures were negative after the releases and Treasury yields fell, which is a market way of saying it is not sure which half of the report to believe. The Fed funds rate sits at 3.88%, below both headline inflation and the 10-year Treasury yield of 4.99%.

The Fed now has more information, and it points in two directions at once. Watch whether energy prices keep leaking into services before the next meeting, because that is the data point likely to decide whether the information-rich period ends in patience or a rate increase.
