GM Pays $220 Million After Selling Drivers' Habits to People Who Raise Premiums

A coalition of 36 state attorneys general says the carmaker passed braking and acceleration data to brokers, and that some drivers paid for it with higher insurance bills.

The wire said: GM to pay $220 million to resolve claims it sold driving habits data · The Verge

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 30, 2026 03:06 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

General Motors ran a program called Smart Driver that tracked how people drove. A coalition of 36 state attorneys general alleged the company sold that data to brokers without telling drivers.

Why it matters

Cars are increasingly rolling sensors, and the data they collect can end up in insurance pricing. The settlement requires GM to limit what it collects and obtain express consent first, which sets a template other automakers may notice.

General Motors has agreed to pay $220 million to settle claims from 36 US attorneys general that it illegally sold driver and location information to data brokers, The Verge reported, citing earlier reporting by Reuters. The settlement also requires the company to limit the driving data it collects and to obtain "express consent" from drivers before collecting it. In other words, GM must now ask first, which is a policy most people assumed was already in place.

The case centers on Smart Driver, a now-discontinued program that recorded acceleration, braking and trip length. According to the states, GM passed that information to data brokers, which made it available to insurance companies that could then raise premiums. Translation: data broker is a middleman whose business is buying information from people who collected it and selling it to people who can profit from it. Your braking habits were, in effect, a commodity with a distribution chain.

The states also alleged that GM failed to tell drivers they were enrolled in Smart Driver or that their data was being collected. Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield, who co-led the case with Washington's Nick Brown, said in a press release that GM told drivers the program would help them drive more safely and promised not to share data with insurers without permission. "People bought a car, not a tracking device," he said. He added that some drivers paid for the data sale with higher premiums.

GM, for its part, said through spokesperson Charlotte McCoy that the agreement addresses a product it ended in 2024 and reinforces steps to strengthen its privacy practices. She said connected vehicle data enables safer, more helpful and personalized experiences, and that GM is committed to being clear about how information is used. The statement is a reminder that in corporate communications, a $220 million payment is a reinforcement.

The deal arrives just months after GM agreed to pay $12.75 million to settle similar claims in California. The multistate coalition included New York, Florida, Michigan, Nevada and Pennsylvania, among others. Two settlements in a short span suggest that regulators have found a theory of the case they like, and that the sums are growing.

The mechanism is simple. A driver gets a feature pitched as safety, the company gets a data stream, and an insurer gets a reason to reprice risk. The only party in that chain not consulted was the one sitting in the driver's seat. What to watch next is whether other automakers with connected-car programs review their own consent screens, ideally before a coalition of attorneys general does it for them.
