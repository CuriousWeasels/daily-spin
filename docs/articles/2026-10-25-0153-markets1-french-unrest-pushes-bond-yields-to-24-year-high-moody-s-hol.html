France Keeps Its Credit Rating, Loses Its Calm, Pays Investors Like It's 2002

Moody's left France at Aa3 with a negative outlook while 10-year yields topped 5% and more protests were scheduled for the weeks ahead.

The wire said: What next for France as riots rattle markets? Three questions facing investors ahead of more protests · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 25, 2026 01:53 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

France has spent months with a fragmented parliament, a large deficit and a budget that opposition lawmakers are resisting. Last weekend more than 500,000 people marched over public services and living costs, and the unrest has since reached bond and stock markets.

Why it matters

When a government pays more to borrow, the bill eventually lands on taxpayers through less room for spending or higher taxes. Prolonged unrest also hurts shops, hotels and exporters, which is where ordinary jobs and sales live.

Moody's said on Friday that it was keeping France's credit rating at Aa3, a level it still calls high grade, while maintaining a negative outlook, according to CNBC. Translation: the agency has not downgraded France, but it has left the door open and put a note on it.

The reasoning was blunt. Moody's said the negative outlook reflects the risk that political fragmentation will keep impairing how the legislature functions. It added that instability, visible in the current social tensions, could hamper the government's ability to address the deficit, the rising debt burden and higher borrowing costs. It said the outlook could return to stable if institutions manage to narrow the deficit.

The market had already offered its own review. The yield on France's 10-year bond, known as the OAT, rose 11 basis points on Thursday to its highest since 2002, and CNBC reported it passed 5% this week. Translation: a basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point, and a yield is what investors demand to lend you money. France is being asked to pay more for the privilege. The gap over German Bunds, the euro zone's benchmark, is the widest since November 2011.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. France's deficit is roughly 5.5% of GDP, against a European Union limit of 3%. The government wants 5% in 2027, down from a projected 5.4% in 2026. More than $1 trillion of debt matures by 2030, and the finance ministry warned a month ago that debt could reach 121.7% of GDP in 2027. S&P Global and Fitch already cut their ratings in 2025, so Moody's is the last of the big three to keep France where it was.

The National Assembly resumes debating the budget's tax measures on Oct. 27, and opposition lawmakers are resisting. Alain Durré of Natixis said the government could use Article 49.3, which pushes a bill through without a final vote unless lawmakers pass a no-confidence motion, or use executive ordinances. Matthew Ryan of Ebury was less hopeful, saying that even if the budget passes it may be so riddled with concessions to left and right that the deficit is left insufficiently addressed. This is the legislative equivalent of a diet plan approved after every committee member adds a dessert.

Growth is the second worry. ING had expected GDP growth of just 0.3% in 2026 and 0.6% in 2027, and economist Charlotte de Montpellier said it could be weaker if unrest continues. For scale, she cited an INSEE estimate that the Yellow Vest movement may have trimmed late-2018 growth by 0.1 percentage point, and the Banque de France's figure of roughly the same for the 2023 pension protests. Markets can look past short disruptions, she said, but a lasting loss of confidence matters more.

The corporate damage has begun. Carrefour shares fell more than 14% on Wednesday after it cut its profit outlook and reduced investment, citing the unrest and rising energy costs. Its chief executive, Alexandre Bompard, called the situation unprecedented. ING cited chambers of commerce estimates that more than 2,000 businesses were directly affected, and the CAC 40 is down 4.5% over the past month. Retail, hospitality, tourism and transport are the most exposed.

Luxury is insulated but not immune. LVMH gets only 8% of its 2025 revenue from France, but Carmignac's Kevin Thozet noted that tax-free tourist purchases make up around 20% of its French revenue. Investing.com reported that luxury stores in France were boarding up windows ahead of fresh protests, which is one way to protect inventory and another way to discourage tourists from browsing.

The calendar is crowded: demonstrations on Oct. 24, the budget debate on Oct. 27 and a union-backed national strike day on Nov. 5. Thozet warned that another six months of political vacuum is a very long time for markets. France retains its rating for now, and investors are watching whether it can retain a budget.
