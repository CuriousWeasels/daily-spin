Meta and SpaceX Raise $135 Billion Between Them to Buy Chips, Insist This Is Normal

Meta plans up to $95 billion in stock sales while SpaceX lines up $40 billion in GPU leasing, as the cost of AI outgrows the usual piggy bank.

The wire said: Meta plans up to $95 billion in stock sales to fund its AI buildout · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 27, 2026 02:33 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Big tech companies are spending at a historic pace on AI data centers and chips, and the money has to come from somewhere. This week Meta and SpaceX each disclosed new ways of paying for it.

Why it matters

The companies building AI are now financing it through share sales and equipment leases on a scale that reshapes who owns them and who can afford to compete. If debt markets stay wary of AI-only firms, smaller players may be squeezed out or absorbed.

Meta plans to sell up to $95 billion in new stock to fund its AI infrastructure buildout, according to The Information, as relayed by The Decoder. Of that, about $22.5 billion would come from Class A common stock, per a Meta announcement. Another $12.5 billion would come from depositary shares, each representing one-twentieth of a new preferred share. Translation: a way of selling stock in slices small enough that more people can buy one.

The preferred shares convert automatically into common stock in August 2029, unless they convert sooner. The remaining $60 billion would come from ongoing open-market sales once new Class C shares begin trading, likely no earlier than the first quarter of 2027. That is a long runway for a company that wants the money for chips now, but that is what happens when you announce a financing plan the way other people announce a holiday schedule.

The Class C shares are the clever part. Meta currently has Class A shares with one vote each and Class B shares with ten votes each, and entities controlled by Mark Zuckerberg hold most of the Class B. The new Class C shares carry no voting rights at all. Translation: investors can hand Meta tens of billions of dollars and receive, in exchange, a share price and nothing else. According to the announcement, Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom have expressed non-binding interest in buying up to $5 billion of the stock, which means some chip suppliers may fund the purchase of their own chips. That circle is tidy, if not exactly new.

SpaceX, meanwhile, chose a different route. In a regulatory filing Monday, it disclosed a binding commitment for roughly $40 billion in financing for AI chips and related data center equipment, which it called the largest committed GPU financing. An entity managed by Apollo Global Management will buy the hardware and lease it to SpaceX for about five to five-and-a-quarter years, starting as each batch is delivered. Translation: Apollo buys the expensive computers, SpaceX rents them, and both sides get to describe this as a partnership.

The deal is expected to close by year-end, with leases starting in the first half of 2027. CFO Bret Johnsen said it diversifies funding sources and covers a notable portion of what SpaceX needs for its growth plans. He also cited less-than-one-year payback periods on AI infrastructure investments. TechCrunch reported that SpaceX's AI segment, which includes Grok, the Cursor coding tool and cloud compute, brought in $7.6 billion of its $14.6 billion in third-quarter revenue, more than Starlink and launches combined.

The spending is the other half of that story. Capital expenditures reached nearly $20 billion, up from $6.9 billion a year earlier, and Johnsen said SpaceX expects to spend $35 billion to $40 billion in the fourth quarter alone. Put differently, a single quarter's planned spending is about the size of the entire Apollo deal.

Elon Musk used the occasion to comment on competitors. Citing SpaceX's investment-grade status and steady cash flow from Starlink and launches, he argued that debt markets have largely closed to companies with no major revenue outside AI data centers. The 200-plus neoclouds, firms built to rent out GPUs, face what he called on X "guppy-level death and consolidation." Translation: when lenders get nervous, the company with other businesses gets the loan. It is a point with some logic, made by a man whose own company just received $40 billion.

The two deals share a premise: the chips cost more than even the richest companies like to pay from cash on hand. Meta is asking shareholders to cover it while keeping its voting structure intact. SpaceX is asking a private equity firm to own the hardware while it keeps the output. Whether investors who are, in TechCrunch's phrase, squeamish about runaway spending and limited profits keep lending is the next thing to watch.
