Serbian Students Win Election, Ending Populist's Decade-Plus Run Without Needing a Single Graduation Ceremony

A student-backed movement has won Serbia's parliamentary election, according to NPR, ending more than a decade of dominance by a Moscow-backed populist leader.

The wire said: 'The smell of freedom': Serbia's student movement celebrates election triumph · NPR World

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 27, 2026 16:54 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

NPR reports that a student-backed movement has won Serbia's parliamentary election, ending more than a decade of rule by a populist leader described as Moscow-backed. NPR's report quotes celebrants describing the result as 'the smell of freedom.'

Why it matters

A change of government in Serbia could shift the country's balance between Moscow and the West, though the sources provided do not say how far or how fast. For Serbians, it means the first change in political leadership in over ten years.

The plain version: Serbia held a parliamentary election, a student-backed movement won it, and a populist leader who had dominated the country for more than a decade, and who NPR describes as Moscow-backed, lost his grip. NPR called it a political earthquake. Political earthquakes are usually reported as such after the fact, since the people who feel them first are rarely the ones who are asked to forecast them.

The mood on the streets, per NPR's headline, was one of celebration, with a participant quoted as calling the moment 'the smell of freedom.' It is a vivid phrase, and one that probably reflects the fact that a decade is a long time to wait for a change of scent.

A word on the term 'populist,' since it does a lot of work in this story. Translation: a politician who wins support by presenting himself as the voice of ordinary people against elites, and who often becomes, over a decade or so, the most elite person in the building. Dominance of this kind generally means control over the machinery that shapes elections: media coverage, state institutions and patronage. That a movement built by students beat it is the notable part.

The 'Moscow-backed' label matters because Serbia has long sat between Russia and the European Union, courting both and committing to neither with any enthusiasm. A new governing force raises the question of whether that balancing act continues. The source text available to us does not say what the winners plan to do about it, so anyone offering a confident forecast is guessing, which in foreign policy commentary is a respected profession.

What happens next is mostly the unglamorous part. Winning an election is the portion of governing that comes with balloons. The rest involves forming a government, deciding who gets which ministry, and discovering which drawers contain what. Students accustomed to organizing protests are about to learn that the opposition has no agenda for the first session of a budget committee.

The kicker is a familiar one in politics: the hard part of beating a long-serving incumbent is that, eventually, you are the incumbent. Serbia's new leaders have just earned the right to be blamed for everything.
