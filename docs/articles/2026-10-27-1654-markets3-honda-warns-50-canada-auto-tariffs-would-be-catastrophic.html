Honda Calls 50% Canada Tariff 'Catastrophic,' Hopes Cooler Heads Prevail Before New Year

An American Honda executive warned that doubling duties on Canadian vehicles and parts on Jan. 1 would hurt the entire industry, and asked for a three-country deal instead of two separate ones.

The wire said: Honda warns 50% Canada auto tariffs would be catastrophic for auto industry · CNBC Markets

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 27, 2026 16:54 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

President Trump has threatened to raise tariffs on Canadian vehicles and parts from 25% to 50% on Jan. 1 if the U.S. and Canada fail to reach a trade deal. The U.S. has also declined to automatically extend the USMCA, the North American trade pact, putting it on an annual review.

Why it matters

Cars built in North America cross borders many times before they reach a showroom, so tariff changes can raise costs across the whole supply chain. Buyers could eventually see that in sticker prices, though the sources do not say by how much.

Jennifer Thomas, senior vice president of corporate affairs at American Honda Motor, told a Reuters automotive conference in Detroit on Tuesday that a 50% tariff on Canadian vehicles and parts would be "catastrophic for the entire industry," according to CNBC. The trigger is Jan. 1, the date President Trump set in August for doubling the current 25% rate if no deal emerges. The calendar, in other words, is the only party in these talks that has been perfectly clear about its position.

Thomas said she is "cautiously optimistic" that cooler heads will prevail. Translation: Honda has no way to influence the outcome and has decided that hope is a reasonable line item. Her optimism rests on recent reports that Washington and Ottawa have restarted talks, including on easing levies on critical materials such as aluminum.

What Honda really wants is not a deal but a bigger deal. Thomas said the company prefers a three-country arrangement including Mexico, like the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement, or USMCA, over separate bilateral arrangements. CNBC reported that trade groups for Honda and every major U.S. automaker have lobbied the same way. Translation: when the entire industry agrees on something, it is usually because the alternative involves a spreadsheet nobody wants to build.

The reason is parts. Many automotive officials have raised concerns because components cross North American borders repeatedly during vehicle production, so a tariff is not paid once but can be paid at several stops along the way. A bolt that goes to Canada and back does not experience this as a vacation.

The USMCA itself is in an awkward state. Trump negotiated it in his first term to replace NAFTA, and it took effect in 2020. He later soured on it and accused Canada of "ripping off the United States of America for years." U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer announced months ago that the U.S. would not automatically extend the pact, shifting it from a 16-year trilateral extension to an annual review. Translation: instead of a long lease, the deal is now month-to-month with an annual inspection.

Honda is the second-largest vehicle producer in Canada behind Toyota, but the company says more than 60% of the vehicles it sells in the U.S. are built domestically at multiple U.S. assembly plants. That is a meaningful cushion, though Thomas made clear it does not make the industry immune. She said she never thought she would see North America put at such a disadvantage, noting that some overseas countries have better tariff rates.

She has a point on the numbers. CNBC noted the Trump administration has struck deals with Japan and South Korea, both major vehicle exporters to the U.S., that include 15% tariffs on vehicles. Canadian vehicles face 25% now and possibly 50% in the new year, meaning a neighbor with an integrated supply chain could pay more than distant exporters.

Markets, for now, are not panicking. SPY closed at 769.14 on Oct. 27 and the VIX, Wall Street's fear gauge, stood at 18.39 on Oct. 26, a number that suggests investors are concerned but not yet rearranging their furniture. With about two months left, the question is whether negotiators move before the date does.
