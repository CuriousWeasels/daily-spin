US Tells Federal Agencies to Delete DeepSeek, Without Saying By When or Exactly Why

A Homeland Security order bars civilian agencies from buying or running DeepSeek products, including anything 'built with' them, though sources disagree on how much reasoning came with it.

The wire said: US orders federal agencies to remove China’s DeepSeek AI from their systems · TechCrunch AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 20, 2026 15:53 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

DeepSeek, a Chinese AI lab, rattled markets in January 2025 when its R1 reasoning model arrived and Nvidia shares sold off sharply. Washington has been restricting its use in government ever since, starting with a December 2025 law covering intelligence systems.

Why it matters

Any company selling software to the federal government may now need to know whether DeepSeek is somewhere inside its product, a question many vendors may not be able to answer quickly. The order also shows how the US-China AI rivalry is moving from chip export rules into procurement paperwork.

The Department of Homeland Security has ordered federal civilian agencies to stop buying products and services from Chinese AI startup DeepSeek and to remove existing ones from government IT systems, TechCrunch reported. The order also covers third-party products built using DeepSeek technology, though agencies can request exceptions. It is a ban with a built-in suggestion box.

TechCrunch said DHS cited national security concerns, including the worry that sensitive data from US government users could be accessible to Chinese authorities under Chinese law. The Decoder, citing the Wall Street Journal, reported something different: that the notice names no specific security concerns and sets no removal deadline. The sources do not reconcile this, so readers may choose between a stated worry and an unstated one.

The legal basis, according to The Decoder, is a 2018 US law on supply chain security in federal procurement. Translation: the government has a statute that lets it declare a vendor too risky to buy from, and it has now pointed that statute at a chatbot. This extends a law passed in December 2025 that targeted DeepSeek's use in intelligence national security systems. Civilian agencies were, until now, apparently free to use it for whatever civilians do with a chatbot.

The tricky phrase is 'built with'. The Decoder noted that it is unclear what that means and whether AI gateways such as OpenRouter, which route requests to many different models, fall under the ban. Translation: an agency may be using a product that uses a service that uses a model, and nobody has said how many layers down the inspector is expected to dig. Procurement officers should expect a busy year of reading vendor documentation.

The order lands amid a broader argument over Chinese open-weight models, meaning models whose trained parameters are published for anyone to download. The Decoder, citing the New York Times, said that over the summer the government weighed sanctions, a trade blacklist and rules on which models agencies can use. It backed off for now after Nvidia, Microsoft, Google and Meta objected, while OpenAI and Anthropic had lobbied for restrictions. The companies that sell AI to the public were, in short, divided according to who stood to lose from the rule.

There is also the matter of distillation, a technique in which one model is trained on the outputs of another. TechCrunch said a September US report accused DeepSeek and five other Chinese companies, including Alibaba and Moonshot AI, of large-scale distillation to extract information from American systems. OpenAI told lawmakers it had seen suspected attempts by DeepSeek to distill its models, and Anthropic said it had identified unauthorized attempts by DeepSeek and other Chinese labs to collect outputs from Claude. Translation: the allegation is that the student copied the teacher's homework by asking the teacher a very large number of questions.

Beijing objected. Foreign Ministry spokesperson Mao Ning said China opposes 'overstretching the concept of national security and politicizing technology and trade issues.' Bloomberg reported last week that China is itself considering export restrictions on 23 AI-related technologies, including chip designs and certain advanced models, citing security and misuse risks. Both governments, it seems, have discovered national security as a growth sector.

What to watch next is the guidance. Until DHS defines 'built with' and sets a deadline, agencies have an order to comply with and no date by which to have done so.
