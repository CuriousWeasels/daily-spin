Markets Confident Fed Will Hold Rates, Based on Chairman Who Declines to Say

Futures price just a 15% chance of an October hike, though Kevin Warsh has rejected the habit of telling investors his plans in advance.

The wire said: Analysis: Everyone expects the Fed to hold rates in October. Kevin Warsh could surprise us all · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 18, 2026 14:56 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Kevin Warsh, the new Fed chairman, helped push the FOMC to a unanimous rate increase in September. The committee meets again on Oct. 28, and traders expect a pause.

Why it matters

If the Fed hikes against expectations, mortgage, credit card and business borrowing costs could move quickly and stock and bond prices could swing. With CPI at 3.7%, the case for tighter policy is not imaginary.

According to CNBC, traders are betting heavily that the Federal Reserve will leave interest rates alone when it meets on Oct. 28. Futures markets put the chance of a hike at 15% as of Friday morning. That is a confident bet, placed on a chairman who has declined to confirm it.

The reasoning goes like this. Fed Vice Chairman Philip Jefferson and New York Fed President John Williams recently gave remarks suggesting little urgency to raise rates, and markets repriced toward a hold. Together with Kevin Warsh, they form an informal leadership group known as the troika. Translation: the three people who usually decide things before everyone else votes on them.

The trouble, CNBC's analysis argues, is that the old rule that the lieutenants speak for the chairman may not survive the leadership change. Warsh has repeatedly rejected the convention of telling markets what he plans before the committee meets. He also said at the September press conference of the Fed's own projections, "Those aren't my forecasts." Investors have been reading signals from a communication system its new operator has openly declined to use.

Then there is the inflation data. Consumer prices rose 3.7% for the year through September, and producer prices gained 6.3%. CNBC says that implies core PCE, the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, of about 3.1% year over year. Translation: prices are rising at a pace that is roughly half again the Fed's 2% target, which Warsh called "a firm, fixed target" at Jackson Hole.

Against that, Warsh said in Bangkok that "the world has broadly conformed to our expectations" since September, and he stressed that he was not signaling a decision either way. That can be read as comfort with a pause. It can also be read as a sentence that commits him to nothing, which is the point of the exercise.

Other numbers cut in different directions. The fed funds rate stands at 3.88%, the 2-year Treasury yields 4.77% and the 10-year yields 5.1%, so the bond market is already pricing in more tightening than the Fed has delivered. The Chicago Fed's financial conditions index has risen about 0.05 points since Jackson Hole but remains negative, meaning conditions are tighter yet still loose overall. Warsh has leaned on that looseness to justify his hawkish stance.

A hike that markets do not expect would produce volatility, and it would embarrass Jefferson and Williams, whose remarks helped shape those expectations. CNBC notes Warsh may instead wait to gauge the committee and settle near its middle. The VIX, a measure of expected stock market swings, sat at 16.46 on Oct. 15, which is not the level of an investor class bracing for impact.

The practical advice is to read the Oct. 28 press conference carefully. Until then, a 15% chance is merely what markets assign to a thing they have decided not to think about.
