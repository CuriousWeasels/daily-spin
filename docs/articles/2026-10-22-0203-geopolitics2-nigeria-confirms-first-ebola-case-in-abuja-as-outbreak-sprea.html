Nigeria Confirms Ebola Case as Uganda Politely Notes the Patient Was Only Passing Through

A woman who flew in from Uganda tested positive in Abuja for the Bundibugyo strain, which has no vaccine or specific treatment, and 44 contacts are now being traced.

The wire said: Nigeria confirms Ebola case as outbreak spreads into western Africa · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 22, 2026 02:03 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

A Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak has been spreading since it was declared in mid-May, centered in eastern Congo, with cases later reported in Uganda and Kenya. Nigeria set up an Ebola task force in June and stepped up surveillance two weeks ago after Kenya's first cases.

Why it matters

Bundibugyo Ebola has no approved vaccine or specific treatment, so containing it depends almost entirely on speed: fast testing, isolation and contact tracing. A case in Africa's most populous country, in a capital city, tests whether those systems work in practice.

Nigeria's health ministry confirmed on Tuesday its first case of Ebola in the current outbreak, in the capital, Abuja. The patient is a woman who arrived from Uganda and tested positive for the Bundibugyo strain, according to the Guardian, Al Jazeera and DW. It is the first appearance of the disease in West Africa in this outbreak, and the fifth country to detect it, according to the WHO.

The timeline is where the story gets uncomfortable. The Guardian and DW report that she began showing symptoms on October 7 and was admitted to a private Abuja hospital on October 11. Al Jazeera reports she was not tested until October 19, after officials learned she had recently been in Uganda. Ayoade Alakija, a Nigerian physician who co-chairs a G7 committee on global health investment, noted that it took 12 days from falling ill to being tested, potentially exposing many other people. Translation: the surveillance system worked, just not at the speed of the virus.

Authorities have identified 44 contacts, most of them healthcare workers, according to Al Jazeera. They include her husband, a Swiss national who lives with her in Abuja. Officials are also tracing passengers who flew with her, though they do not believe she was symptomatic on the flight. Health Minister Muhammad Ali Pate said, "We are doing everything possible to contain this case."

Then there is the question of where she caught it, which several governments are keen to answer with 'somewhere else.' Uganda's health ministry said she only transited through the country and is a Nigerian on a business trip to eastern Congo, adding that "the location of exposure remains under investigation." Nigerian authorities have not disclosed her nationality, and an unnamed official told AFP she is a diplomat whose condition has deteriorated into "multi-organ failure." The sources do not reconcile these accounts, so readers will have to hold both.

The stakes for Uganda are more than reputational. DW notes that the WHO declared it Ebola-free in late August, after 42 days without a confirmed case. Translation: 42 days is two full incubation periods, the standard waiting time before declaring an outbreak over. Uganda insists it has no confirmed active cases, but the Nigerian patient's travel history could put that status back into question.

The numbers explain the urgency. The outbreak has infected more than 9,000 people and killed more than 4,300, according to DW, with the Guardian citing figures above 9,500 and 4,600. The sources differ on the totals, but agree on a fatality rate near 50%. The WHO says it is the fastest-spreading on record. DW adds that improved testing may account for some of the increase, and that it is the second-largest ever, behind the 2014-16 West African epidemic, which caused about 11,300 deaths over roughly 30 months.

Nigeria has done this before. In 2014 its first case, in Lagos, produced fears of a rapid spread but was brought under control within three months, with 20 infections and eight deaths, according to Al Jazeera. When the task force was announced in June, Femi Gbajabiamila, chief of staff to President Bola Tinubu, said Nigeria did not want a repeat of when "everyone was scrambling to respond." The 12-day gap suggests the scramble has been rescheduled rather than cancelled.

Africa CDC director general Jean Kaseya said the outbreak is "no longer one country's fight" and will convene African health ministers next week to strengthen the cross-border response and review hospital capacity. Kenya, meanwhile, has recorded nine cases since October 6, which authorities believe stem from a single cluster. For now, the thing to watch is whether those 44 contacts stay at 44 and whether any of them develop symptoms.
