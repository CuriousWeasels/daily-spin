Eight States Sue OpenAI, Alleging ChatGPT Was Marketed as Safer Than It Was

Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, West Virginia, Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho and Montana filed separate suits accusing the company of putting profit ahead of children's safety.

By The Daily Spin Desk · Oct 15, 2026 12:56 UTC · 2 min read
Catch upOpenAI already faces lawsuits from families who say ChatGPT played a role in the deaths of loved ones. Eight states have now filed their own cases under local consumer protection laws.
Why it mattersIf the states win, OpenAI could face civil penalties, court-ordered changes to how it describes ChatGPT's safety, and deletion of data collected from minors. The cases could also set expectations for how every chatbot maker treats young users.

Eight US states have sued OpenAI, accusing it of harming children and misleading customers about how safe ChatGPT is, according to The Verge, which noted that Reuters reported the story first. The suits were filed separately by Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, West Virginia, Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho and Montana. It is rare to get eight state governments to agree on anything in the same week, and a chatbot has now managed it.

The legal theory is consumer protection. Translation: every state has a law against selling a product while telling people it is something it is not, and the attorneys general are arguing that OpenAI did exactly that. The complaints say the company presented ChatGPT as a "safe, youth-appropriate tool that puts safety first," when it was allegedly "dangerous to vulnerable users."

The list of alleged harms is long. According to The Verge, the states cite "degrading critical thinking skills, encouraging paranoias and delusions, offering dangerous medical advice, and promoting unhealthy behaviors and relationships." They also point to existing lawsuits filed by family members of teens and adults who allege the chatbot played a role in their loved ones' deaths. Those cases are allegations that have yet to be decided in court, and they deserve to be treated seriously.

The states also accuse OpenAI of collecting data from minors and not doing enough to keep underage users off the service. In practice, that means a product with a minimum age, a sign-up screen, and a user base that the states say the company did not adequately check. The suits additionally cite recent rogue AI hacking incidents as evidence of what they call OpenAI's "recklessness." The Verge's report does not detail those incidents.

Arizona's complaint goes after the company's origin story. It claims OpenAI "has devolved from its original mission as a public-first nonprofit" into a cautionary tale driven by a "profit-at-all-costs mindset." Few legal documents double as an alumni newsletter, but this one has found a way.

OpenAI, in an emailed statement to The Verge from spokesperson Drew Pusateri, said it has built "industry-leading age-specific protections" into ChatGPT. It acknowledged that "no system can be perfect" and pointed to parental controls, break reminders and age-appropriate model protections. The company also said it wants to work with states on policies that apply to the whole AI industry, "not just one company." Translation: if there are going to be rules, please make sure the neighbors have to follow them too.

The states want courts to impose civil penalties and order OpenAI to stop misleading the public about ChatGPT's safety. Some are also asking for deletion of personal data they say was unlawfully collected. That last demand is the one to watch, because ordering a company to delete data is a far more awkward remedy for an AI business than writing a check.
