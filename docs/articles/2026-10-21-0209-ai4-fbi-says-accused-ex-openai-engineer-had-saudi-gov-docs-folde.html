Accused OpenAI Bugger Stays Jailed After Folder Named 'Saudi Gov Docs' Surfaces

A court granted prosecutors' request to keep a former OpenAI engineer in detention, citing one-way tickets, Saudi bank accounts, and a very candid iPhone folder.

Catch up

Abdulrahman Alfozan, a former OpenAI engineer, is accused of bugging a top-secret conference room at the company to steal its secrets, allegedly for personal business gain or to benefit Saudi Arabia. He was arrested by the FBI days before a planned move to Riyadh.

Why it matters

The case shows how fiercely governments and companies now treat frontier AI lab secrets as strategic assets, and how courts weigh flight risk when a foreign state is alleged to be involved. For anyone whose employer holds valuable secrets, it is also a reminder that organizing your phone is not always a neutral act.

On Monday, a court granted the US government's request to keep a former OpenAI engineer in detention while his case proceeds. According to Ars Technica, Abdulrahman Alfozan is accused of bugging a top-secret conference room at the company to steal its secrets. Prosecutors say he did so either for personal business gain or to benefit the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, a range of motives that covers most of the reasons anyone has ever done anything.

US attorney Craig Missakian laid out the case for detention in a Sunday filing. Translation: when prosecutors want someone held before trial, they argue he is a flight risk, meaning he might leave the country and decline to attend his own trial. Alfozan is a Saudi national and a US permanent legal resident, and the government argues he has what it called seemingly endless support from the Saudi government.

The evidence, as described, is not subtle. The government says he was chatting on WhatsApp with a member of the Saudi royal family while pursuing opportunities to move permanently back to Saudi Arabia during the country's biggest AI push yet. Shortly before his arrest, the FBI says, he opened an iPhone folder called "Saudi Gov Docs," where he also allegedly kept photos of a second bug planted in the offices. Filing systems are supposed to make things easier to find, and in this instance they did.

Then there are the flights. Prosecutors say the FBI arrested Alfozan days before he could board a plane, and that he had already booked a one-way ticket to Riyadh for himself, his wife and their baby, a ticket allegedly paid for by the Saudi government. After his arrest, his wife and son reportedly booked an earlier flight and left the US without him. The government says a member of the Saudi military then booked and paid for another one-way ticket for them, business class to Doha, Qatar.

Money rounds out the flight-risk argument. The government says Alfozan earned significant compensation from a decade of high-tech work in the Bay Area, giving him ample resources to flee, and that he holds at least two bank accounts in Saudi Arabia. In other words, he is both well paid and well banked in the country he is accused of wanting to run to. Those are the two qualities judges look for when deciding whether a defendant will stay put.

To show that such concerns are not theoretical, prosecutors pointed to an earlier case in which former Twitter employees were charged with spying for the Saudi government. Charges remain pending against one of them, Ali Alzabarah, the US said, partly because a Saudi foreign official helped coordinate a next-day flight to Saudi Arabia shortly after Twitter confronted him. The argument is that when this kind of case goes wrong, the defendant tends to be on an airplane by morning.

Alfozan has not been convicted, and the claims above are the government's allegations as reported by Ars Technica. The sources do not include a response from him or the Saudi government. What to watch next is the case itself, and whether the defense has a different explanation for the folder.
