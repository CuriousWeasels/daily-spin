U.S. Borrows $2 Trillion a Year, Bond Market Asks for 5.1% and a Plan

A $1.992 trillion deficit, a 5%-plus 10-year yield and a divided Congress on the way leave no obvious exit from the debt problem, CNBC reported.

The wire said: Analysis: Why a $2 trillion deficit and 5% Treasury yields are becoming America's biggest economic challenge · CNBC Economy

By The Daily Spin Desk · Markets & EconomyOct 27, 2026 16:54 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

The U.S. deficit hit $1.992 trillion in fiscal 2026, up 12% from last year, while the 10-year Treasury yield has climbed 114 basis points since the Iran war began on Feb. 28. Companies borrowing heavily for AI infrastructure are now competing with the government for the same pool of investor money.

Why it matters

The 10-year yield sets the tone for mortgage rates, which hover around 7.5%, and for what the government pays to borrow, which feeds back into the deficit. Without a political fix, higher rates make the debt harder to shrink and everyday borrowing more expensive.

The federal government ran a deficit of $1.992 trillion in the 2026 fiscal year, Treasury data showed Friday, according to CNBC. That is up 12% from last year and roughly 6.2% of GDP. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has said the target should be below 4% by the end of President Trump's term, which CNBC politely describes as something the U.S. has almost no way of hitting.

Meanwhile, the 10-year Treasury yield traded at 5.1% on Friday, up 114 basis points since the end of February. Translation: a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so the government's borrowing cost has risen by about 1.14 percentage points in under eight months. The latest official reading, from Oct. 23, put it at 5.0%.

Several things are pushing yields up. The war with Iran sent oil and diesel prices higher, and bond traders now want extra yield in case central banks, including the Federal Reserve, raise rates. Translation: lenders are charging more now because they expect to be hurt by inflation later. Brent crude was at $115.22 as of Oct. 20, which suggests they have a point.

There is also a supply problem. Investors believe Washington will keep issuing bonds in volume, and corporations are issuing hundreds of billions in new debt to build AI infrastructure. CNBC noted that global investors seeking safe long-term debt now have attractive alternatives to Treasurys. The government, once the only borrower in the room, is now one of several people asking for the same money.

Ordinary people feel this through housing. The average 30-year mortgage hovers near 7.5%, and Redfin said pending home sales fell 4% in September, the biggest monthly drop in three years. Mortgage rates follow the 10-year yield, so a debate in Washington about spending becomes a debate in your kitchen about whether the house is still affordable.

Rates also make the deficit itself worse. The Congressional Budget Office estimates that if rates stay half a point above its forecast, the 10-year cumulative deficit will be $1.9 trillion higher, and $6 trillion higher if they overshoot by 1.5 points. The CBO expects the 10-year yield to average 4.3% over the period, against 5.1% today. In other words, the budget office is assuming rates will behave better than they currently are.

Bessent's answer is growth. "We've got this mountain of debt, and we have to grow our way out of it," he said in Dallas last month. The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget calculates that balancing the budget by growth alone would take a decade of 7.2% GDP growth. Second-quarter growth was recently revised up from 1.5% to 2.2% annualized, so the gap is a mere five percentage points.

Politics offers little help. Republicans are widely forecast to lose at least the House in the Nov. 3 midterms, and Rep. Hakeem Jeffries, the likely next speaker, wants to reverse $1 trillion in healthcare cuts. Trump, meanwhile, pledged $5,000 checks to every adult citizen if Republicans swept the election, according to CNBC. Divided government can produce surprising compromises, though the starting positions are not close.

Not everyone sees a capital shortage. World Bank President Ajay Banga told CNBC that availability of capital is "not really an issue," noting the bank mobilized $112 billion last year, about triple the figure three years earlier. He said investors may worry about circular financing and AI business models, but emerging markets face less hesitancy because they fund bridges and roads. MarketWatch, for its part, reported that AI financing has become harder and costlier as scrutiny of the spending boom grows.

CNBC's bottom line is that a full debt crisis remains further out, and that high yields also attract buyers to Treasurys, since expensive borrowing is not the same as a crisis. Nobody knows what debt level is unsustainable, though the total reached $40 trillion this year. The one thing the bond market has not priced in is a bipartisan plan, which may be the most reasonable assumption on the board.
