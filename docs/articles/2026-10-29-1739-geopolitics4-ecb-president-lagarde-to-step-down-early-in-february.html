Lagarde to Leave ECB Nine Months Early, Conveniently Before French Voters Get a Say

The ECB president will step down on February 7, giving current EU leaders time to pick her successor before France's April presidential election.

The wire said: European Central Bank President Lagarde to step down early · Al Jazeera

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 29, 2026 17:39 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Christine Lagarde has led the European Central Bank since November 2019, after running the IMF and serving as French finance minister. Her eight-year term was due to end in October 2027, and eurozone leaders choose who replaces her.

Why it matters

Whoever runs the ECB sets interest rates for 21 countries, which affects mortgages, business loans and government borrowing costs. Choosing the successor before France votes could change who gets to do the choosing.

Christine Lagarde announced on Thursday that she will leave the presidency of the European Central Bank on February 7, 2027, about nine months before her term was scheduled to end, according to Al Jazeera and DW. In a short video she said she was leaving "with a sense of good work done and an ECB that is fit for the future". No official reason was given, which in central banking is usually where the reasons live.

The timing invites speculation, and both outlets reported it. France holds a presidential election in April, and Al Jazeera noted that polls suggest far-right candidate Marine Le Pen is favoured to win. The ECB president is chosen by the leaders of the eurozone's 21 countries. An early exit lets the current leaders, including Emmanuel Macron, who cannot run again, make the appointment before the next French leader arrives. Translation: the people who would like to pick the referee are picking the referee while they still hold the whistle.

Markus Ferber, a German member of the European Parliament, said that if the departure looks timed around national elections, it "damages trust" in an institution that should stand above domestic politics. This is a delicate point for a central bank, whose entire product is the belief that it does not care who wins elections. European Council President Antonio Costa said the process to find a successor would start immediately and proceed "without delay".

The backdrop is not calm. DW reported that the ECB meets this week and is expected to hold its deposit rate at 2.5 percent while signalling that it will probably rise again before year-end. The bank has already raised rates twice since June to fight inflation driven by the energy shock from the Iran war. Al Jazeera put inflation at almost double the bank's 2 percent target. Translation: the thing the bank exists to prevent is currently happening at twice the permitted volume.

France is the other item on the agenda. DW said French bond yields, which reflect the cost of government borrowing, hit a 25-year high last week amid a deadlock over next year's budget. There is speculation the ECB could for the first time use the Transmission Protection Instrument, a tool that lets it buy a eurozone member's bonds if yields rise uncontrollably and financial stress threatens to spread. Most observers, DW said, think France does not yet meet the strict conditions. The word "yet" is carrying considerable weight there.

Lagarde's record is mixed, as Al Jazeera laid out. She arrived in 2019 as the first ECB chief without formal economics training or central banking experience, and some market participants warned of a "credibility risk". She then steered the bank through the pandemic and Russia's invasion of Ukraine, though it was criticised for reacting too slowly to the 2022 inflation surge. Leaders from Merz to von der Leyen offered warm tributes, and Macron thanked her for her "determined action".

Markets shrugged. Goldman Sachs's Simon Dangoor told Reuters that institutional behaviour is likely to outlast Lagarde and be similar whoever takes over. Sources told AFP that leaving early would give her "greater freedom to express her views", and she has hinted she could join the French presidential debate. So the next thing to watch is who gets the job, and whether the person leaving it ends up on a campaign poster.
