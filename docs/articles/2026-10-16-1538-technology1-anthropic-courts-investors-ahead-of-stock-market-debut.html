Anthropic Tells Investors It Is Both Very Profitable and Worried About Ending Humanity

The AI firm's revenue rose 45% to $16.8bn in a quarter, and it adjusted its way to a profit, as chip stocks fell on fears about AI spending.

The wire said: AI giant Anthropic woos potential investors ahead of stock market debut · BBC Technology

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 16, 2026 15:38 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Anthropic, maker of the Claude AI models, is expected to list on the stock market and has been courting investors at an event in San Francisco. The pitch lands in a week when AI-linked shares have been falling across the US and Asia.

Why it matters

Anthropic and OpenAI's revenue figures are a gauge of whether the AI industry can pay for its own enormous infrastructure. If it can't, the pension funds and index funds holding tech shares will find out with everyone else.

Anthropic has been drumming up support for an expected stock market listing, and according to the BBC, its revenue rose 45% to $16.8bn (£12.7bn) in the three months to the end of September. That follows $4.7bn in the first quarter of the year and $11.6bn in the second. For a company that sells software, this is the kind of growth chart that makes a prospectus nearly write itself.

The company also told investors at its San Francisco headquarters that it made a profit in the third quarter on an adjusted basis. Translation: adjusted profit is profit calculated after leaving out costs the company considers one-off, a category whose membership is decided by the company. The BBC noted that the method ignores non-recurring costs, and investors reportedly left the event optimistic about growth and the path to profitability.

That path matters because the whole industry is trying to persuade the market of the same thing: that spending billions on technology will eventually produce billions in profit, and not merely billions in invoices from chip suppliers. Anthropic's numbers, along with those of rival OpenAI, serve as a gauge of whether the sector can finance the infrastructure it says it needs. OpenAI is also eyeing a listing expected to value it at more than $1tn, though its chief executive Sam Altman said in September it will not go public this year as it addresses safety concerns.

Safety was on the agenda at the Anthropic event too, which is a slightly unusual feature of a sales pitch. The BBC reports that chief executive Dario Amodei has called for the pace of development to slow. In September, one of the company's researchers warned of a greater than 10% chance that AI could kill all humans within a decade, and a former researcher said people working on the technology were genuinely frightened by its speed. The company also said it had disrupted attempts to use its model for malicious activity that could support biological weapons development.

So the investor proposition is, in effect, a fast-growing company that is candid about the risks of what it sells and is also selling more of it. Investors can hold both thoughts at once, provided the second one keeps rising by 45% a quarter. US President Donald Trump, for his part, has said researchers are bringing up things that won't happen and that in the race with China, whoever wins AI wins.

The timing was awkward. Shares in chip makers including AMD, Nvidia and Intel fell sharply on Wednesday. In Asia on Thursday, Softbank fell more than 9%, Samsung 9.5% and SK Hynix 11%. The slide followed remarks by Broadcom chief executive Hock Tan, who said at a conference in Singapore that many investors have no clue and have set extreme expectations, adding that it is almost like they are in a casino.

Then came US inflation data showing prices rose faster than expected in September. Translation: if prices climb faster, the Federal Reserve is more likely to raise the cost of borrowing, which makes it harder to justify paying today for profits promised years from now. That is an uncomfortable backdrop for any company asking investors to value future profits at a premium.

Anthropic's listing is therefore a test of whether the market prefers a growth chart or a casino metaphor. Watch the filing, and watch how much of the profit survives once the adjustments are removed.
