Alberta Votes 76% to Stay in Canada, Separatists Say 24% Is Basically a Mandate

With nearly 1.9 million ballots counted, unofficial results show a lopsided win for unity, and a separatist movement already turning on the premier.

The wire said: Alberta voters overwhelmingly reject plan to leave Canada in referendum · The Guardian

By The Daily Spin Desk · GeopoliticsOct 21, 2026 16:10 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Albertans voted Monday on 10 referendum questions, one of which asked whether to begin a legal process that could lead to a second vote on leaving Canada. Unofficial Elections Alberta results show roughly 76% voted to remain.

Why it matters

The result removes, for now, the threat of a prolonged constitutional fight in Canada's oil-producing province, but Premier Danielle Smith's party now faces a revolt from its own separatist wing. Investors and Calgary officials worried about capital flight get a reprieve, not a guarantee.

Albertans voted overwhelmingly to remain in Canada, according to unofficial results from Elections Alberta. With nearly 1.9 million votes counted, roughly 76% backed staying and 24% backed beginning a process toward separation. The Guardian reported that polling-station delays at about 100 of 1,400 locations pushed results back by more than two hours, which is the longest anyone waited for anything to be settled.

Technically, the question was not whether Alberta should become independent. It asked whether to begin a legal process that could lead to a second vote. Translation: a vote to hold a vote, which separatists found frustrating, since it was the one referendum in which they could lose twice. It was also one of 10 questions on the ballot, covering immigration, healthcare, education and the province's constitutional powers. Elections Alberta said the remaining results could take days.

Prime Minister Mark Carney, who grew up in Edmonton, said Albertans had chosen unity. He said the result would let the country speed up efforts to diversify trade and build major infrastructure, and make Canada a global energy powerhouse with Alberta at its centre. Before the vote he had warned against residents simply wanting to send a message, calling a vote against Canada a dangerous bluff. Conservative leader Pierre Poilievre, who holds a seat in the province, said Albertans were not quitters.

Pollster David Coletto told the Guardian that voters regarded the exercise as a distraction. He described a general sadness about energy spent on the referendum rather than on threats they see more urgently, including the Trump administration. His summary of the mood: Albertans have grievances with Ottawa but are proudly Canadian, and the marriage may be rocky but few want it to end. One radio host put the price at $130 million of taxpayer money and said someone owes Albertans a refund. The sources do not say who, exactly, is expected to write the cheque.

The separatists did what political movements do after a 76-24 loss and found the positive. Keith Wilson, a movement leader, said at least a quarter of Albertans feel the province needs to be treated better, while cautioning that final numbers could fluctuate. David Parker, a longtime far-right organiser, was blunter, calling the result bleak and saying it was a defeat, though it changed nothing about the circumstances Albertans face.

The more consequential fight may be inside Premier Danielle Smith's United Conservative Party. Smith holds her job in large part because separatists within the party ousted her predecessor, Jason Kenney, and she has spent recent months trying to placate both separatists and federalists. The referendum became a political liability, with her party's support tumbling and a one-time conservative stronghold lost in a by-election. Smith said Albertans voted for a stronger Alberta in a united Canada.

Separatist Jeffrey Rath told Global News that independence will stay a dream while Smith, whom he called an avowed federalist, remains in power. He is pushing for a special general meeting to remove her and to take back ownership of the party. So the premier has won a referendum that her own party may punish her for winning.

Calgary Mayor Jeromy Farkas, an opponent of independence, had worried that uncertainty could trigger capital flight, meaning money leaving a place because investors dislike not knowing what country it will be in. He cautioned that no result will permanently put the question to bed, and said separatists see this as a long-term project. Watch the UCP meeting: the next vote on Alberta's future may be held by about the same people who just lost this one.
