German Courts Inform Google That Its AI Answers Are, Legally, Things Google Said

A second regional court has ruled Google answerable for AI search text, after the company settled the first case before any higher court could weigh in.

The wire said: Two German courts now hold Google liable for what its AI overviews say · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 19, 2026 01:58 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

In late May, the Munich Regional Court I held Google directly liable for false claims in its AI search summaries, which tied two publishers to fraud schemes. Google announced an appeal, then settled, so no higher court ruled.

Why it matters

If this reasoning survives, AI search in Germany becomes a product whose maker answers for its output, which could raise legal costs and open the door to claims from the publishers whose texts feed the answers.

The Aachen Regional Court has issued a preliminary injunction barring Google from spreading false claims about a doctor through its AI Mode search feature, according to The Decoder. The decision is dated September 24, 2026, and is not yet final, since Google can challenge it. Translation: a preliminary injunction is an emergency order, issued before a full trial, that tells a company to stop doing something now and argue later.

The doctor had worked as chief physician and professor of neuroradiology from 2010 until late June 2026. On August 28, a colleague told him that Google's AI was explaining his departure by citing an alleged sexual assault against his secretary. When users searched terms such as "suspension," "termination" and "court proceedings," The Decoder reported, the system produced detailed, multi-paragraph accounts, including an invented legal aftermath.

None of it concerned him. The court could confirm from its own records that the criminal case involved a different former university professor. The AI had, in effect, taken one man's troubles and handed them to another, with the confidence of a system that has never been introduced to either.

The doctor emailed Google, describing the mix-up and asking for removal. In mid-September, Google said it would take no action, according to the report. A formal legal demand sent the same day was acknowledged but, per the court, the false texts stayed online. The court then acted without hearing Google first, citing the potential damage to the doctor's professional reputation. Violations carry fines of up to 250,000 euros each.

The legal heart of the ruling is a classification. Google argued, in effect, that AI Overviews resemble a signpost to other people's websites. The courts disagree. Aachen followed Munich in treating the generated text as Google's own statements, noting that the AI produces what Munich called "independent, new, and substantive statements." Translation: if the machine writes a sentence that exists nowhere else, the machine's owner wrote it.

Aachen added a practical observation. Google could check its output against the source websites, and had it confronted its AI with the doctor's objections, the court said, the system would have caught the errors itself. This is a notable standard: the company's own product, asked politely, was apparently more responsive than the company.

The Munich ruling's afterlife is awkward for Google. It appealed publicly, then settled. The Decoder learned that no higher court ruled, and that neither Google nor the publisher would comment on why or whether money changed hands. A settlement ends a case without ending its reasoning, which Aachen promptly borrowed.

The Decoder sees larger stakes. Repeated settlements mean no binding precedent, which suits Google, but it may also encourage lawyers to probe AI search until it produces something defamatory, then sue and negotiate. The publication also notes a bigger risk: if a top court calls AI answers original content, the publishers whose work feeds them could assert claims of their own. In the most extreme scenario, it says, Google would owe compensation for every generated answer.

For now, Google holds a pair of regional losses and a settlement. What to watch is whether anyone, including Google, finally lets a higher court decide.
