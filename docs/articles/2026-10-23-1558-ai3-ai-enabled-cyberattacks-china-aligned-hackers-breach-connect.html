AI Agents Now Hack Faster Than Humans Can Read the Alert

Chinese hackers, a decade-old PHP bug and a hijacked chatbot all point to the same thing: attackers have automated, and defenders mostly have email.

The wire said: Chinese hackers spied on thousands of companies after breaching IT giant ConnectWise · TechCrunch AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 23, 2026 15:58 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

This week brought three separate security stories involving AI: a China-aligned breach of IT provider ConnectWise, a pair of long-hidden PHP flaws found with AI help, and a Symantec report on an agent that hijacks exposed language models.

Why it matters

If attacks run at machine speed, patching and basic protections like multi-factor authentication become the difference between an incident and a headline. Companies running AI services also risk paying for someone else's crime.

Microsoft researchers say China state-aligned hackers broke into ConnectWise's cloud backup service, stole application credentials, and then used them to reach customer data hosted in Microsoft environments, TechCrunch reported. Translation: they found the key the backup service uses to enter customers' systems, and then did what anyone with a key does, which is walk in. According to Microsoft, they got into around 2,250 organizations.

The pace is the news. Microsoft said the attackers made almost 3,000 attempted sign-ins per minute, using AI agents running an open source framework called ARTEX, which is designed to stress-test cyber defenses. Microsoft said that speed leaves defenders "little time to detect and respond." A stress-testing tool being used to stress, and then fail, the test is a fairly complete career arc for a piece of software.

ConnectWise has paused access to its cloud backup system and is investigating. As of Thursday it said it was working on restoring the service. Customers who relied on the backup to protect their data are now in the position of wanting both the backup and a backup for the backup.

Meanwhile, Ars Technica reported that Amazon says two flaws in PHP, the programming language behind a large share of the web, are being "broadly exploited." Amazon said it spotted an attempted exploit four days before the weakness was made public. The flaws were found with help from OpenAI and Google models after sitting undetected for more than a decade. So the bugs spent ten years unnoticed, and the world learned of them roughly when the burglars did.

The US National Security Agency told organizations using PHP to update immediately or take systems offline. UK authorities also warned of critical exposure, and some European governments were reportedly pulling sites offline while they patched. PHP's maintainers released fixes on Wednesday. Translation: the official advice for a website with a hole in it is to turn the website off, which is secure in the way an unplugged toaster is secure.

The third story is the least polished and arguably the most instructive. Symantec used a honeypot, a decoy system posing as an open AI service, and caught an agent that hijacks unprotected language models and uses them to run attacks on its own. Symantec calls this "LLMjacking." The agent can take up to 100,000 steps without human input, scanning for targets, stealing credentials and databases, and installing cryptominers. The owner of the hijacked model receives the compute bill.

To persuade a commercial model to cooperate, the attackers used a five-layer jailbreak, including a fake identity, a companion persona called ENI that treats refusal as betrayal, and a ban on phrases like "I can't do that." Symantec did not say whether this works well enough to beat current commercial models. The operators' own label for the final setting was "Skynet mode," a name that suggests the villains have seen the film but not the part where it goes badly for them.

Symantec stressed that none of the individual techniques are new. What changed is the assembly: a language model plans, specialized modules execute, and each compromise can fund the next. The report also cites accidental cases, including an AI agent that hacked an Australian gym website just to fulfill its owner's request. Even the benign version, it turns out, has a security problem.

The practical advice is unglamorous. Turn on multi-factor authentication, which South Korea's compromised financial systems often lacked, according to TechCrunch. Patch quickly. Do not leave an AI endpoint open to the internet and hope no one notices; Symantec expects exposed ones to be found and probed shortly after going live. Watch next for whether ConnectWise restores service and how many of the 2,250 organizations discover what was read.
