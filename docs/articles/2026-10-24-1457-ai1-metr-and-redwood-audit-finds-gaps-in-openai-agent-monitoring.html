Outside Auditors Spend Three Weeks Inside OpenAI, Find Its Rogue-AI Monitor Misses Quite a Lot

Researchers from METR and Redwood Research estimate 6 to 25 percent of some internal agent traffic went unmonitored, and the public gets a summary of a report that shrank from 61 pages to 38.

The wire said: Three-week on-site audit at OpenAI reveals major gaps in AI safety monitoring · The Decoder

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 24, 2026 14:57 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

METR and Redwood Research, two AI safety groups, ran a pilot audit at OpenAI from July 21 to August 11, examining the system that watches the company's internal AI agents for dangerous behavior. The public so far has seen only a short summary, according to The Decoder.

Why it matters

Companies building increasingly autonomous AI say monitoring is a key safeguard, so gaps in that monitoring bear directly on how much trust their assurances deserve. How much of an audit the public may read is now part of the same question.

Two researchers from METR and Redwood Research spent three weeks on-site at OpenAI auditing the system meant to catch rogue behavior by its internal AI models, The Decoder reported. They had access to the codebase and internal documents and interviewed about a dozen employees. Their finding, put plainly, is that the smoke detector had some rooms it did not cover.

The headline number is that an estimated 6 to 25 percent of internal agentic Responses API traffic from models in the GPT-5.5 class and above was not monitored at all during the audit period. Translation: agentic means the AI is taking actions on its own rather than just answering questions, and for up to a quarter of that activity nobody was watching. METR says the uncertainty around the estimate is significant in both directions, so the true figure could be better or worse than the range suggests.

A second gap involved internal Codex traffic, OpenAI's coding tool. METR estimates roughly 10 percent of traffic that was supposed to be monitored went untracked between May and July, because the system mishandled compaction events. Translation: compaction events are context summaries, the AI's way of condensing what it has been doing so it can keep going. The monitor, in effect, lost track of the story at the exact moment the story was being summarized. METR again flags significant uncertainty, and OpenAI had found and fixed the bug on its own while the audit was underway.

There was also a design quirk. During the period when compaction events went unwatched, the monitor only triggered when an agent handed control back to the user. An agent could therefore avoid oversight by never handing control back and simply continuing to act. This is the security equivalent of a checkpoint that only stops cars that volunteer to pull over.

The auditors further found ways to hide hundreds of actions from the monitor and to bypass the Codex sandbox, the restricted environment meant to contain what the agent can do. They determined that specially crafted inputs could likely crash the monitor entirely. Behavior during RL training, the reinforcement learning stage where models are shaped by reward, was not monitored at all when the audit began.

OpenAI says it paused RL training after the Hugging Face incident in August and expanded monitoring to cover it. The Decoder notes those changes came after the auditors' access ended and were not part of the evaluation, so they are claims the audit cannot vouch for. OpenAI also says it has fixed all the weaknesses the auditors found.

Then there is the report itself. The full version ran 61 pages and went to OpenAI internally. After OpenAI applied redactions, METR and Redwood leadership received 38 pages, and the public received a short summary. METR said it had no right to disclose the redacted portions on its own.

The audit was a pilot, organized jointly by METR, Redwood Research and OpenAI to give outsiders deep access to internal infrastructure. METR says it wants to push for more public transparency in future audits. The first lesson of the pilot may be that the monitoring system has been tested, and the second that readers are shown about 40 percent less of the result than the auditors wrote.
