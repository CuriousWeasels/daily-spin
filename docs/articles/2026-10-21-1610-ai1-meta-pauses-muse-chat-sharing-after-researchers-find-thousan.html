Meta Pauses Muse Sharing After 'Private' Links Turn Out to Be Searchable

Security firm Claroc found 6,800 Muse conversation links through public DNS records, including loan applications and a confidential executive-search call.

The wire said: Meta Muse exposes users' personal and business secrets when they share conversations · Ars Technica AI

By The Daily Spin Desk · AIOct 21, 2026 16:10 UTC · 2 min read

Catch up

Meta recently launched Muse, a general-purpose AI agent that Mark Zuckerberg said was built for privacy and security. It lets users share conversations through web links, which researchers say were easy to discover.

Why it matters

Anything typed into an AI assistant and shared by link may be less private than it looks, and people have been pasting loan applications and revenue reviews into these tools. Meta's pause is a reminder to treat a share button as a publish button.

Meta has paused the sharing feature in Muse, its new AI agent, after researchers at the security firm Claroc reported that they could find thousands of supposedly private shared conversations with a single query. According to Ars Technica, the researchers enumerated 6,800 share links. Meta told Ars and The Verge it paused the feature while it reviews the report and considers changes to how the links can be discovered.

Share links normally work like an unlisted door. The page exists, but the address is a long string of random characters that nobody should be able to guess. Translation: the security rests on the address being a secret, so the address had better stay one.

According to Claroc, Muse built each address from a short phrase describing the conversation, followed by a random ID. The phrase sat in the hostname, the part of the address that computers look up in DNS. Translation: DNS is the internet's phone directory, and Muse put a description of each conversation next to its phone number.

The researchers used passive DNS, per Ars. These are commercial services that keep historical records of which domain names pointed where, collected from sensors on the infrastructure that answers such lookups. Once someone clicked a share link, the lookup was recorded in a searchable database. Claroc says the hostname usually revealed what a page held before they opened it, and that a hostname pulled from DNS was enough to rebuild the full link.

After opening a few hundred of the links, the researchers found material that was plainly not meant for the public. Ars listed a candidate's notes for a confidential executive-search call with a frontier AI lab, a marketing company's internal revenue review marked for its CEO, and a small-business owner's loan application with the amount requested. There was also a to-do list containing a bank fraud alert with a partial account number, a real-estate investor's performance report with two property addresses, and a workplace's full-year vacation calendar. "It took one query," the researchers wrote.

Claroc's co-founder and CTO, Asaf Aprozper, told Ars he reported the findings through Meta's bug bounty program on October 8 and also tried contacting people at Meta on LinkedIn. He got no reply either way. Meta's statement said it opened the bounty program so researchers could raise different scenarios, which is a fair description of what happened, if not of the response time.

The episode sits awkwardly beside Mark Zuckerberg's claim last month that Muse was "built from the ground up for privacy and security." It is also not new. The Verge noted that conversations with ChatGPT, Claude and Grok have previously ended up in Google Search after sharing features made chats more public than users expected.

Meta says it will consider changes. Watch for whether the fix is a longer random link, a link that cannot be derived from a conversation's title, or an option that does not involve the whole internet. Until then, the safest sharing method remains the one that predates all of this: not pasting the loan application in the first place.
