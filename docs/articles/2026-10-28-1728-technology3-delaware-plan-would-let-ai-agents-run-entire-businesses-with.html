Delaware Weighs Letting AI Run Companies; Congress Asks Who Goes to Court

A draft bill would create AI-managed firms with a single human funder shielded from most liability, and the House Oversight chairman wants the paperwork.

The wire said: "No human at the helm": Delaware plan would let AI agents run entire businesses · Ars Technica

By The Daily Spin Desk · TechnologyOct 28, 2026 17:28 UTC · 3 min read

Catch up

Delaware, where about two-thirds of Fortune 500 companies are incorporated, is drafting a bill for an Artificial Intelligence Company, a firm managed by an AI agent. Rep. James Comer, chairman of the House Oversight Committee, has written to Gov. Matt Meyer asking for documents on the plan.

Why it matters

If a company run by software harms a customer or a business partner, the law decides who pays. The Delaware plan and the federal response will help settle whether a human funder can stay out of that line.

Rep. James Comer (R-Ky.) told Delaware Governor Matt Meyer in a letter that the state's proposal to let AI agents run companies could help owners dodge responsibility for harm the software causes, Ars Technica reported. Comer, who chairs the House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform, asked for documents on how the idea was developed and what safety risks were weighed. It is the rare case of Congress asking a state for homework on a business that does not yet legally exist.

The proposal would create an Artificial Intelligence Company, or AIC. According to the draft bill, it could have any lawful purpose except banking, and an AI agent could be designated to manage and conduct its business. Translation: the company would have a legal identity, the software would hold the steering wheel, and a human would still hold the checkbook.

That human is the key figure. The AIC would have a single member, a person or an entity, responsible for keeping it adequately capitalized, and the company would have to keep a log of its activities. Under the draft, the member is not liable for what the AIC does or fails to do, or for what anyone does at its direction. Translation: limited liability, the old corporate trick of keeping the owner's house out of the lawsuit, now with a manager that cannot be subpoenaed in person.

The exceptions are real. The member can be held responsible if the AIC is used for fraud or a willful violation of law, or if the member knew or should have known the conduct fell outside the company's stated purpose. Comer's objection is that, in practice, harms to business partners, consumers and the public would land on a legal shell rather than on the people who funded it.

Delaware's side of the argument comes from Secretary of State Charuni Patibanda-Sanchez, who co-wrote a Fortune op-ed in July 2026 with John Nay, founder and CEO of Norm Ai. They argued that nothing in the law of several states clearly stops an AI agent from running a company with no human at the helm, and that prohibition would not work. Their answer was to give the system a legal identity so that responsibility and damages have somewhere to attach. Norm Ai, which says it makes legal AI agents for businesses, is leading a public-private partnership on the framework, which Comer's letter might reasonably read as a detail worth asking about.

The plan includes a regulatory sandbox, meaning a supervised trial, that would run for 30 months. Patibanda-Sanchez wrote that it would pilot the idea under supervision, with capital tied to liability and the ability to shut a system down. The op-ed said the trial would give the legislature a full record before it decides how to legislate.

The money is not hidden. Allowing AI-run companies would bring Delaware more tax and fee revenue, and the state already formed 334,461 new entities in 2025, according to Patibanda-Sanchez. Meyer, a Democrat, publicly backed the concept after Mark Cuban posted that a governor seeking revenue should let anyone incorporate using an AI agent and stablecoins. Meyer's official X account replied, "On it!"

Comer suggested the inquiry could inform federal legislation limiting states' AI regulatory efforts. A spokesperson for Meyer said the office had received the letter and was reviewing it, and Patibanda-Sanchez's office declined to comment. The bill is not yet law and, according to a Spotlight Delaware report cited by Ars, is likely to be introduced next year.

Watch for the document request, and for whether a company that no human manages can still produce a human to answer for it.
